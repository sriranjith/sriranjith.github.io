## Discover

Imagine a sweet shop with six jars on the counter.

You look at all six. Lemon sherbets, liquorice, toffee, jelly beans, chocolate
buttons, mints. You think about it for a moment, you pick the toffee, you pay,
and you go outside happy, eating toffee.

Now imagine a different sweet shop. This one has two hundred jars, floor to
ceiling, all along both walls and around the corner. There are eleven kinds of
liquorice. There is something purple you have never seen before.

You are thrilled. You walk up and down the aisle. You look at everything twice.
Then, somewhere around the fourth or fifth pass, a strange feeling starts up:
you can only have one bag, and every single jar you don't pick is a jar you
don't get.

You end up buying the toffee.

Outside, eating exactly the same toffee as before, you are not quite as happy —
because now you are wondering about the purple ones.

Or you might not buy anything at all. You might just stand there for a while and
then leave, which is a very odd thing to do in a shop containing two hundred
kinds of sweet, every single one of which you could have had.

That, roughly, is the claim: that past some point, adding more of a good thing
can make people choose worse, enjoy it less, or not choose at all. It became one
of the most repeated findings in popular psychology. It is also a first-rate
example of how a striking result can be believed by millions of people long
before anyone has checked whether it is reliably true.

## Understand

Standard economics has a simple and reasonable position on extra options: they
cannot hurt. If you didn't want the purple ones, ignore them. An option you can
decline is worth nothing or more than nothing, never less. Add options, and the
best available choice can only get better or stay the same.

Barry Schwartz's 2004 book *The Paradox of Choice: Why More Is Less* argued that
this misses what choosing actually costs a person. His case had four parts, and
they are worth separating because they are not equally well supported.

**Choosing is work.** Comparing six things is easy. Comparing two hundred is a
serious cognitive task, and one that most people are not being paid to do.
Somewhere past a certain number, the effort of choosing well starts to eat the
benefit of choosing well. Faced with that, people take shortcuts, choose badly,
or postpone — and postponing indefinitely means not choosing at all.

**Every option you reject stays with you.** The economist's term is opportunity
cost, and Schwartz's point is a psychological one: with six options, the thing
you gave up is one jar of liquorice. With two hundred, you have given up a
hundred and ninety-nine things, several of which were probably excellent. The
imagined pleasure of the rejected options is subtracted from the real pleasure
of the chosen one.

**Regret becomes available.** If you had two choices and picked wrong, that was
bad luck. If you had two hundred and picked wrong, that was your fault. The
larger the option set, the more plausible it becomes that a better choice was
sitting right there, and the more the disappointment converts into
self-blame.

**Expectations rise to meet the menu.** A shop with two hundred jars is
implicitly promising that one of them is perfect for you. When the toffee turns
out to be merely nice, the gap between nice and perfect is felt as a
shortfall — a shortfall that a six-jar shop never created.

Put together, the argument is that added options bring a small, steady gain in
the quality of the best available choice and a growing cost in effort,
opportunity cost, regret and inflated expectation. Past some crossover point,
the second exceeds the first.

### What is actually established

Here the entry has to be careful, because the popular version of this idea is
much stronger than the evidence for it.

**The strong claim — that adding options reliably makes people choose less and
enjoy it less — is not supported.** Two large meta-analyses, in 2010 and 2015,
looked at the accumulated experiments and found the average effect of more
choice on whether people choose at all to be close to zero. Not small.
Approximately nothing. Individual experiments found effects in both directions,
some quite large, and they largely cancel.

**A weaker claim survives, and it is the interesting one.** Choice overload is
real but *conditional*. It appears when particular circumstances are present and
vanishes when they are not, which is why studies that did not control those
circumstances produced a scatter of results averaging out to zero. The
conditions that show up consistently are:

- **time pressure** — the choice must be made quickly;
- **unclear preferences** — you don't already know what you like, so the options
  cannot be filtered against anything;
- **complex options** — each one takes real work to evaluate, on several
  dimensions that don't reduce to a single number;
- **no dominant option** — nothing in the set is obviously best, so the decision
  cannot be short-circuited.

When those hold, a large set can genuinely stall people. When they don't —
when you know what you want, have time, or one option is clearly best — more
choice is usually neutral, and often helpful. Nobody is paralysed by a large
bookshop, because they came in for a particular book or are happy to browse
without buying.

**And one part of Schwartz's book has held up better than the headline.** It is
about people rather than menus, and it is covered below.

### Why "paradox" is still the right word

Not because the strong claim is true. Because there is a genuine tension that
survives the collapse of the strong claim: the conditions under which more
choice helps and the conditions under which it hurts are *both* real, and no
settled theory says where the line falls. Twenty years of experiments have
established that the answer is "it depends", identified several of the things it
depends on, and not produced a model that predicts, for a given menu and a given
shopper, which way it will go. That is what an open problem looks like.

## Examples

**The jam study.** Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper set up a tasting table at
Draeger's, an upmarket grocery in Menlo Park, California. On some hours the table
offered 6 varieties of jam; on others, 24. Everything else was held constant, and
anyone who tasted got a discount coupon redeemable in the shop, so purchases
could be tracked.

| | 6 jams | 24 jams |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Passers-by who stopped | 40% | 60% |
| Of those, who bought | 30% | 3% |
| Passers-by who ended up buying | ~12% | ~1.8% |

The big table was better at attracting attention and roughly six times worse at
selling jam. That single table is the origin of essentially every popular
retelling of the paradox of choice, and it is worth knowing that the second row
rests on a modest number of actual purchases in one shop on one weekend.

Iyengar and Lepper's paper reported two further studies. In one, students offered
6 essay topics for extra credit were more likely to write an essay than students
offered 30, and their essays were graded slightly higher. In another, students
choosing a chocolate from 6 varieties were more satisfied with their choice than
those choosing from 30.

**The pension study.** Iyengar, with Gur Huberman and Wei Jiang, examined
enrolment in hundreds of American employer retirement plans and found that
participation declined modestly as the number of available funds rose. This is
often cited as the strongest real-world case, and it has the right shape for
overload — unclear preferences, complex options, no dominant choice, and a
decision that can be postponed indefinitely at no immediate cost. It is
observational rather than experimental, so the direction of causation is not
locked down.

**The studies that found the opposite.** Less famous, and the reason the average
is near zero. Experiments have found people more satisfied with a chosen coffee
or chocolate from a larger assortment, more likely to buy from a larger range,
and more likely to return to a shop with more variety. Benjamin Scheibehenne and
colleagues ran their own replications of the jam paradigm before the
meta-analysis and did not find the effect.

**What the meta-analyses found.** Both used a standardised effect size, where by
long convention 0.2 counts as a small effect, 0.5 as medium and 0.8 as large.

| Study | Scope | Mean effect | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010) | ~50 experimental comparisons | close to zero | No reliable main effect; conditions unidentified |
| Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman (2015) | ~99 observations | close to zero overall | No main effect, but four moderators reliably shift it |

The two teams agree on the finding and differ on what to make of it. Both found
an average near nothing. Scheibehenne's group emphasised that the variation
between studies was large and unexplained, and concluded that nobody yet knew
when overload happens. Chernev's group, with five more years of data, argued
that the variation is systematic and identified four moderators — choice-set
complexity, decision task difficulty, preference uncertainty, and decision goal
— that predict a good deal of it. That second position is roughly where the
field sits today.

## Explore

**The replication problem, stated plainly.** The jam study was published in 2000
in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, one of the most selective
journals in the field. It is elegant, it was run in a real shop with real
shoppers, and its numbers are dramatic. It is also, on its own, one field
experiment. When Scheibehenne, Rainer Greifeneder and Peter Todd assembled every
comparable experiment they could find in 2010 — published and unpublished,
roughly fifty comparisons — the mean effect of assortment size on choice was
indistinguishable from zero, with enormous variance between studies. Their
conclusion was not that the jam result was fabricated or that the effect is
imaginary. It was that no one could yet say when it appears, which is a
devastating thing to be able to say about an idea that had by then been
converted into retail strategy, public policy advice and several thousand
management presentations.

Two structural features of research make this outcome likely rather than
freakish. Journals prefer surprising positive results, so failed replications of
a beloved finding tend not to get written up, and the published record drifts
away from the true average — publication bias. And a study with few participants
can only reach statistical significance if the effect it happens to measure is
large, so small studies that do get published systematically overstate the size
of what they found. A single striking result is, for both reasons, the least
reliable kind of evidence there is, and the one most likely to end up in a book.

**Being fair to Schwartz.** He was reporting the best available evidence at the
time he wrote, and the strong version of the claim was mainstream psychology in
2004, not a fringe position. He has since acknowledged the meta-analytic results
and moderated the claim, arguing that the effect is real but conditional — which
is roughly where Chernev's meta-analysis also lands, and is a more defensible
position than the one the book's subtitle advertises. It is worth separating the
book from its reception: *The Paradox of Choice* contains a good deal of
carefully hedged discussion, and the culture extracted from it a single
memorable slogan and the jam.

**The part that held up: maximisers and satisficers.** This is Schwartz's more
durable contribution, and it relocates the whole problem from the menu to the
person.

The distinction descends from Herbert Simon, who coined *satisficing* in 1956 to
describe an agent that searches until it finds an option meeting a threshold and
then stops, rather than one that surveys everything and optimises. Schwartz, with
Andrew Ward, John Monterosso, Sonja Lyubomirsky, Katherine White and Darrin
Lehman, built a questionnaire in 2002 to measure where a person sits on that
spectrum — whether they habitually search for the best or accept the good
enough.

The finding, replicated many times since, is that **maximisers report lower
satisfaction than satisficers**: less happiness with their decisions, more
regret, more upward counterfactual thinking, lower reported optimism and life
satisfaction. And in the most quotable result, Iyengar, Rachael Wells and
Schwartz followed graduating students through a job search in 2006 and found that
maximisers obtained jobs with starting salaries around 20 per cent higher than
satisficers — and felt worse about them. The paper is titled "Doing Better but
Feeling Worse", which is the entire finding in four words.

Note carefully what this does to the original thesis. It says the problem is not
that the shop has two hundred jars. It is that some people respond to two hundred
jars by attempting to evaluate two hundred jars, and that this is a losing
strategy: the marginal improvement from more searching is small, the effort is
large, and the vivid awareness of the rejected alternatives is corrosive.
Satisficers standing in the same shop are fine. **The overload is in the
strategy, not the assortment** — which is a considerably more interesting claim
than "more is less", and one that generalises well beyond shopping.

This work is not beyond challenge either. Later researchers, notably Nenkov and
colleagues in 2008, argued that Schwartz's maximising scale conflates several
distinct things — high standards, extensive search, and difficulty deciding —
and that they behave differently: high standards on their own are not associated
with unhappiness, while the search-and-difficulty components are. If that is
right, the finding is not "wanting the best makes you miserable" but "searching
exhaustively and struggling to commit makes you miserable", which is both more
plausible and more actionable.

**The disagreement that remains.** No one now defends the strong claim, and no
one seriously argues the effect never occurs. The live question is whether the
moderators identified so far are a genuine theory or a post-hoc list. A model
that says overload happens under complexity, uncertainty and time pressure is
much better than nothing, but it has not yet been used to make a risky forward
prediction — to specify in advance which of two untested assortments will
produce the effect and by how much. Until it is, the honest status is: a real
phenomenon, unreliably summoned, imperfectly understood.

## History

**1956.** Herbert Simon introduced *satisficing*, arguing that real decision
makers with limited time and attention do not optimise but search until a
threshold is met. The idea that the act of deciding has a cost enters economics
here, and everything in this entry descends from it.

**1970.** Alvin Toffler's *Future Shock* coined *overchoice*, predicting that
industrial societies would generate more options than their citizens could
process. It was speculation without data, but it named the worry a generation
before anyone tested it.

**2000.** Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper published "When Choice is Demotivating:
Can One Desire Too Much of a Good Thing?" in the *Journal of Personality and
Social Psychology*, volume 79, pages 995–1006. Three studies; the first is the
jam table. The paper is careful, its authors were appropriately cautious about
its scope, and it became one of the most cited findings in consumer psychology.

**2002.** Schwartz and colleagues published the maximising scale in the same
journal, establishing the maximiser–satisficer literature.

**2004.** *The Paradox of Choice: Why More Is Less* appeared from Ecco.
Schwartz combined the choice-overload experiments with his own work on
maximising, regret, adaptation and opportunity cost, and drew a conclusion about
affluent societies: that the expansion of options past a certain point had
stopped adding to well-being and started subtracting from it. The book sold
extremely well.

**2005.** Schwartz's TED talk carried the idea to an audience of millions and
fixed the jam study in popular memory as an established fact. For most people who
know the phrase "the paradox of choice", this is where they got it.

**2010.** Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd published "Can There Ever Be Too
Many Options? A Meta-Analytic Review of Choice Overload" in the *Journal of
Consumer Research*, volume 37, pages 409–425. Mean effect near zero; large
unexplained heterogeneity; no identified conditions. The popular idea and the
research literature separated at this point and have not rejoined.

**2015.** Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman published a second meta-analysis in the
*Journal of Consumer Psychology* confirming the absent main effect but
identifying four reliable moderators. This is the current mainstream position:
the effect exists, conditionally, and the conditions are partly known.

**Since.** The episode became a standard teaching case in the replication crisis
that reshaped psychology through the 2010s — cited alongside ego depletion and
social priming as an instance where an appealing, widely believed effect turned
out to rest on much less than its fame implied.

## Why It Matters

The most valuable thing about this entry is not the finding. It is the shape of
what happened to it.

A single well-designed experiment produced a dramatic result. The result was
memorable, told a satisfying story about modern life, and was picked up by a good
popular book. From there it entered business advice, journalism, policy
discussion and general knowledge — and it completed that entire journey before
anyone had established that the effect reliably occurred. When the checking
finally happened, ten years later, the average across fifty experiments was
approximately zero.

Three practical lessons come out of that, and they apply well beyond this case.

**A single striking study is a hypothesis, not a fact.** However elegant the
design, however respectable the journal, one experiment tells you that something
happened once in one setting. The relevant question is never "was this study
good?" but "what does the accumulated evidence look like?" — and for a genuinely
new finding, the honest answer is usually "there isn't any yet."

**Direction is not magnitude.** "More choice reduces purchases" is a claim about
direction and can be true in some settings while being useless. What you need is
the size of the effect and how much it varies. An average effect of 0.02 with
large scatter and an average of 0.5 with tight scatter both sound like "the
effect is real" in prose, and they mean entirely different things in practice.
Popular retelling almost always drops the number, because the number is the part
that does not fit in a sentence.

**Meta-analysis is where findings go to be tested.** Pooling every study,
including the boring ones and the ones that found nothing, is the only way to see
past publication bias — the tendency for surprising positive results to be
written up and unsurprising null results to sit in a drawer. It is unglamorous
work that almost never produces a headline, and it is what turned "more is less"
into the far more useful "more is sometimes less, and here is when."

There is a fourth lesson, easy to miss, and it argues against over-correcting. A
near-zero average effect does not mean nothing is there. It can also mean that a
real effect is being cancelled by an equally real effect running the other way,
which is precisely what Chernev's moderators suggest is happening here. The
correct response to a failed replication is rarely "the phenomenon is fake"; it
is "the phenomenon is conditional, and we have not yet identified the
conditions." Choice overload survived that transition, in reduced and better
specified form.

And the practical residue is genuinely useful. If you are designing a menu, a
form or a shelf, the number of options matters far less than whether the person
in front of it knows what they want, whether the options can be compared without
effort, and whether one of them is obviously reasonable. Structure, defaults and
comparability do more than counting.

## Modern Relevance

**Streaming catalogues and recommender systems.** The commercial answer to large
assortments has not been to shrink them but to hide them. A catalogue of tens of
thousands of titles is presented as a ranked row of about six, personalised. This
is the moderator theory implemented as an interface: the underlying set stays
enormous, while the set actually presented is small, ordered, and constructed so
that one option looks like the obvious one. Whether this makes people happier
than a genuinely smaller catalogue would is an open question, and one that the
companies with the data have little reason to publish.

**Retirement and health-insurance menus.** The clearest policy application, and
the one with real money attached. Pension plans and public insurance exchanges
face exactly the conditions the moderators identify: options that are complex,
preferences that are unclear, no dominant choice, and an outcome decades away.
The interventions that work — default enrolment, tiered menus that present a
handful of curated options before the full list, standardised comparison formats
— do not reduce the number of available choices. They reduce the difficulty of
the decision, which the evidence suggests is the variable that actually matters.

**Dating apps and hiring.** Both put a person in front of a large, weakly
differentiated set with unclear preferences and no dominant option, and both
generate the complaints the theory predicts: difficulty committing, persistent
suspicion that a better option is one more swipe away, lower satisfaction with
the choice eventually made. The maximiser–satisficer framing fits these better
than the assortment-size framing does. The problem is less the size of the pool
than the fact that the format invites exhaustive search and makes the rejected
alternatives permanently visible.

**Product ranges and the shelf.** Retailers periodically cut their assortments,
sometimes citing the jam study directly. The results have been mixed enough to be
instructive: some ranges have been trimmed and sales rose, others were trimmed
and customers went elsewhere for the item that disappeared. Which happens depends
on whether shoppers arrive knowing what they want — and in a supermarket, mostly
they do, which is why the effect is hard to reproduce there.

**AI assistants and the shape of the question.** Systems that answer with a
single recommendation rather than a ranked list are, in effect, betting that the
cost of choosing exceeds the value of choosing. The maximiser literature suggests
that bet is often right and that the resulting satisfaction may be higher — and
also names the price, which is that the person never sees the alternatives and
cannot tell whether the answer was good. That is the same trade the sweet shop
makes when it puts six jars on the counter instead of two hundred, with the
significant difference that the six jars are visible and the filtering is not.

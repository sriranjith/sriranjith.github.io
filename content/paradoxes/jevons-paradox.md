## Discover

In 1865 a young English economist did the arithmetic on Britain's coal and
frightened Parliament.

Britain ran on coal. Coal made the iron, drove the mills, moved the trains,
heated the houses and powered the navy. Coal was the reason a small wet island
was the richest country on earth. And Jevons calculated that the seams were going
to run out.

The obvious answer, then as now, was efficiency. Steam engines had been getting
dramatically better. James Watt's engine used a fraction of the coal that Thomas
Newcomen's had, for the same work. Every year engineers squeezed more out of
every ton. Surely that was how you make the coal last.

Jevons said the exact opposite, and he said it flatly:

> It is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is
> equivalent to a diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth.

His evidence was the previous hundred years. Watt's engine had cut coal use per
unit of work enormously — and Britain's coal consumption had not fallen. It had
exploded. Because once the engine was cheap to run, people put engines in places
that could never have justified one before: in mines, in mills, in ships, in
locomotives, in workshops in every town.

Efficiency had not saved the coal. **Efficiency was the reason the coal was
disappearing.**

## Understand

Jevons's argument has a simple engine at its centre. Efficiency does not just
reduce how much fuel a job needs. It reduces the **price of getting the job
done** — and things that get cheaper get bought more.

Think of what people actually want. Nobody wants coal. People want warm rooms,
moved goods, lit streets, forged iron. Fuel is what those things cost. Double the
efficiency of the machine and you halve the price of the service.

If the price of anything else halved, you would expect people to buy more of it.
The question is only *how much* more.

### Three ways the demand comes back

Economists now call the recovered consumption the **rebound effect**, and it
arrives through three distinct channels.

**Direct rebound.** You do more of the same thing. A car that goes twice as far on
a litre makes driving cheaper per mile, so some people drive further. A cheaper-
to-heat house gets kept warmer, or heated in rooms that used to be left cold.

**Indirect rebound.** The money you saved gets spent on something else, and that
something else took energy to make and run. A household that saves £400 a year on
heating does not burn the money. It buys a flight, or a larger television, or
puts it in a pension that funds a factory.

**Economy-wide rebound.** This is Jevons's own version and the largest of the
three. Cheaper energy services make production cheaper across the whole economy.
That lowers prices, raises real incomes, shifts which industries are viable,
attracts investment into energy-using sectors, and — over decades — changes what
gets invented. The steam engine did not just make existing mills cheaper. It made
the railway possible, and the railway created demand for coal that had not existed
in any form before.

### The scale that matters

The rebound is usually expressed as a percentage of the savings that get eaten
back:

- **0%** — the engineer's assumption. Efficiency doubles, fuel use halves,
  behaviour unchanged.
- **Between 0% and 100%** — **partial rebound**. Fuel use still falls, but by less
  than the engineering calculation promised. This is what almost all measurements
  find.
- **100%** — the savings are exactly cancelled.
- **Above 100%** — **backfire**. Total fuel use rises. This is the Jevons paradox
  in its strong form.

The distinction between partial rebound and backfire is the entire live argument.
That rebound exists is not seriously disputed by anyone. Whether it typically
exceeds 100% is disputed hard, and the answer depends on which channel you
measure, over what time horizon, and at what scale.

### Where the honest uncertainty sits

Direct rebound is measurable and moderate. Steve Sorrell's 2007 review for the UK
Energy Research Centre — still the most thorough survey — found direct rebound for
household energy services in developed economies generally below 30%, with
personal transport and space heating in the 10–30% range. Kenneth Gillingham,
David Rapson and Gernot Wagner reached compatible conclusions in 2016, putting
most direct estimates between 5% and 40%.

Economy-wide rebound is where the argument lives, and it is much harder to
measure, because you are asking a counterfactual question about the entire
economy over decades. Estimates in the literature run from about 20% to well over
100% depending on the model, and modelling choices — how substitutable energy is
for capital, whether technical change is treated as free — largely determine the
answer.

**The honest summary is this.** Rebound is real and is routinely ignored by
engineering forecasts, which systematically overstate savings. Backfire is
possible, is well documented in a few specific historical cases, and is not what
most careful studies of individual efficiency measures find. Jevons was right
about the mechanism and probably right about coal in nineteenth-century Britain.
Whether he is right about a refrigerator standard in 2026 is a separate question
that has to be answered separately.

## Examples

**The arithmetic of rebound.** Suppose a technology doubles efficiency — twice the
service per unit of energy. What happens to energy use depends entirely on how
responsive demand for the service is to its price. Write $\eta$ for the elasticity
of demand for the service:

| Elasticity $\eta$ | Service consumed | Energy consumed | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | unchanged | **−50%** | no rebound |
| 0.5 | +41% | **−29%** | partial rebound |
| 1.0 | +100% | **unchanged** | full rebound |
| 1.5 | +183% | **+41%** | backfire |
| 2.0 | +300% | **+100%** | strong backfire |

Backfire needs $\eta > 1$: demand for the service has to be elastic. That is the
whole condition, and it explains the pattern in the evidence. Mature,
near-saturated services have inelastic demand — nobody heats their house to 40°C
because heating got cheap — so refrigeration and space heating rebound partially.
New or unsatisfied services have elastic demand, and those are where backfire
turns up.

**Jevons's own case: iron.** James Neilson's hot blast, patented in 1828, cut the
coal needed to smelt a ton of Scottish pig iron by roughly two-thirds. Jevons
pointed out what happened next: Scottish iron output multiplied many times over
in the following two decades, and the total coal burned by the Scottish iron
industry rose rather than fell. The efficiency gain made Scottish iron cheap
enough to sell everywhere, and cheap iron is a product with enormously elastic
demand.

**Lighting, over seven centuries.** Roger Fouquet and Peter Pearson assembled UK
lighting data from 1300 to 2000. The real price of a unit of light collapsed by
orders of magnitude — candles to gas to filament to fluorescent — and consumption
of light per person rose by a far larger factor still. Spending on lighting as a
share of income did not collapse in step with the price, because people kept
buying more light. This is about as clean a historical case of backfire as
exists, and it is why the introduction of LED lighting was greeted in the rebound
literature with the observation that the world would probably end up with a great
deal more light rather than much less electricity.

**A counterexample worth taking seriously.** US household refrigerators use
roughly a quarter of the electricity per unit that they did in the mid-1970s,
after federal standards and California's earlier rules. Over the same period the
average refrigerator got larger and more households owned one — genuine rebound,
in both quantity and quality. Total US residential refrigeration electricity use
fell anyway, and substantially. Nobody buys four refrigerators because the
electricity is cheap. Refrigeration is a saturated service with inelastic demand,
and for saturated services the engineering calculation is roughly right.

**Induced demand on roads.** The closest modern analogue outside energy. Adding
lanes to a congested urban motorway lowers the time-cost of a trip, and trips
respond: people drive further, at different times, from further-out homes.
Gilles Duranton and Matthew Turner's 2011 study of US interstates found an
elasticity of vehicle-miles to lane-miles close to 1 — capacity increases were
matched roughly one-for-one by new traffic. That is full rebound in a system with
no fuel in it, which is a useful demonstration that the mechanism is about prices
and demand, not about coal.

## Explore

### What exactly is being claimed?

A great deal of confusion here comes from four different claims travelling under
one name.

1. **Rebound exists.** Efficiency improvements produce less saving than the
   engineering calculation suggests. Essentially undisputed.
2. **Rebound is often large.** Enough to matter for policy design. Widely
   accepted, with the size contested.
3. **Backfire occurs.** Some efficiency improvements increase total consumption.
   Accepted for specific historical cases; contested as a general pattern.
4. **The Khazzoom–Brookes postulate.** Economy-wide, in the long run, energy
   efficiency improvements *systematically* increase energy consumption above
   what it would otherwise have been. This is the strong claim, and it is the
   genuinely disputed one.

Someone who says "the Jevons paradox is real" and someone who says "the Jevons
paradox is overstated" are frequently asserting (1) and denying (4), and
therefore agreeing.

### The Khazzoom–Brookes camp

Daniel Khazzoom argued in 1980, in the *Energy Journal*, that US appliance
efficiency standards would deliver much less than promised because the standards
lowered the cost of the service. Leonard Brookes made a broader argument through
the 1970s and 1980s: energy efficiency is a form of productivity growth, and
productivity growth expands output, and expanded output uses energy. Harry
Saunders gave the position its name and its formal shape in 1992, showing that in
a standard neoclassical growth model with plausible parameters, energy efficiency
improvements raise long-run energy consumption.

The core of the argument is that treating efficiency as a way to cut energy use
confuses a *supply* improvement with a *demand* restraint. Making energy services
cheaper is economically identical to discovering more energy. Nobody expects
discovering an oil field to reduce oil consumption.

### The measured-rebound camp

Sorrell, Gillingham, Rapson, Wagner and most empirical energy economists accept
the mechanism entirely and dispute its magnitude. Their objections to the strong
version:

- **Energy is a small share of costs.** For most sectors of a modern rich economy,
  energy is a few percent of input costs. A large proportional fall in that few
  percent produces a small fall in total costs and therefore a small output
  expansion. The Jevons mechanism was powerful in a nineteenth-century economy
  where coal was a dominant input; it is weaker in an economy where it is not.
- **Saturation.** Rich-country demand for many energy services is close to
  satisfied. Elasticities below 1 are the norm, and the table above shows that
  below 1 there is no backfire.
- **The general-equilibrium estimates are model artefacts.** Economy-wide rebound
  figures above 100% tend to come from models whose substitution elasticities were
  assumed rather than estimated, and the results move a great deal with those
  assumptions.

Gillingham and colleagues make a further point that is often lost: even a rebound
of 60% leaves 40% of the savings intact, and the rebound itself consists of people
consuming more of something they value. Rebound is partly a *benefit* being
counted as a failure. An efficiency measure that lets poor households heat their
homes properly has not failed because the energy saving was smaller than
projected.

### Where the honest disagreement is sharpest

The unresolved question is about **long-run, economy-wide, general-purpose
technologies**. Direct rebound on a single appliance is settled and modest.
Whether a century of pervasive efficiency improvement across an entire economy
raises or lowers total energy use is not settled, because the counterfactual is
unobservable and the models that construct it disagree.

Two facts are worth holding together. Global energy consumption has risen more or
less continuously for two centuries alongside continuous efficiency improvement —
which is suggestive but proves nothing, since population and output rose too. And
energy intensity per unit of GDP has fallen steadily in most rich countries since
the 1970s, with total consumption roughly flat or falling in several of them —
which is also suggestive and also proves nothing, since some of that is offshored
manufacturing.

Serious people read this evidence differently. The entry's status is *debated*
for that reason.

## History

**1865.** William Stanley Jevons, then thirty, published *The Coal Question: An
Inquiry Concerning the Progress of the Nation, and the Probable Exhaustion of Our
Coal-Mines*. The efficiency argument is chapter VII, "Of the Economy of Fuel". The
book made his reputation; John Stuart Mill and William Gladstone both cited it,
and it contributed to a Royal Commission on coal supplies and to Gladstone's
argument for retiring the national debt while Britain could still afford to.

Jevons's forecast was wrong in its particulars. He predicted British coal
production would become uneconomic within a century as seams deepened. British
coal output peaked in 1913 and declined for reasons he did not anticipate —
oil, gas, imports, and the collapse of the industries that consumed it. The
mechanism he described has aged far better than the prediction he used it for.

Jevons is better remembered as one of the three independent discoverers of
marginal utility, with Carl Menger and Léon Walras, in the early 1870s. He drowned
while swimming near Hastings in 1882, aged 46.

**1970s–1980s.** The oil shocks made energy conservation a central policy
question and revived the argument. Leonard Brookes, an economist at the UK Atomic
Energy Authority, argued from the mid-1970s that efficiency policy would not
deliver the projected reductions.

**1980.** Daniel Khazzoom published "Economic Implications of Mandated Efficiency
Standards for Household Appliances" in the *Energy Journal*, formalising the
direct rebound argument for appliance standards.

**1992.** Harry Saunders published "The Khazzoom–Brookes Postulate and
Neoclassical Growth" in the *Energy Journal*, naming the position and deriving it
within a growth model.

**1990s–2000s.** The debate became empirical. Amory Lovins and the efficiency
advocacy community argued the effect was small and being used as an excuse for
inaction; Brookes and Saunders argued back in the same journals. The exchange in
*Energy Policy* through the 1990s is unusually direct.

**2005–2007.** Blake Alcott's survey of the history in *Ecological Economics*,
and Sorrell's UKERC review, established the current framing: rebound
unambiguously real, backfire situational, magnitude the open question.

## Why It Matters

**It changes what an efficiency policy is for.** If the goal is reducing total
energy use or emissions, efficiency alone is an unreliable instrument, because
the savings it produces are partly spent. Policies that work on quantity or price
directly — carbon pricing, emissions caps, fuel taxes — do not have this problem,
because they raise the cost of the service rather than lowering it. The standard
policy conclusion in the literature is not "abandon efficiency" but "pair
efficiency with a price or a cap", so that the freed-up demand meets a constraint.

**It explains a persistent forecasting failure.** Engineering estimates of savings
from efficiency programmes have a long record of overshooting. Utility programme
evaluations routinely find realised savings well below projections. Rebound is not
the only reason, but it is a systematic one, and the gap is now large enough that
regulators build discount factors into their accounting.

**It reframes the relationship between technology and resource use.** The
intuitive model is that technology is how a fixed appetite is satisfied with less.
Jevons's model is that technology is how the appetite grows. Which model is right
determines whether you expect innovation alone to solve a resource problem. Anyone
arguing that a technology will reduce consumption of something owes an account of
why the demand for the cheapened service will not expand.

**It is an argument that has been repeatedly rediscovered.** Induced demand in
transport planning, the observation that faster computers get filled with heavier
software, and the finding that agricultural intensification does not reliably
spare land for nature — sometimes called the Borlaug hypothesis and its
counter-evidence — are all the same structure arrived at independently. That
recurrence is itself evidence the mechanism is general.

## Modern Relevance

**Data centres and AI.** Every generation of hardware and every algorithmic
improvement has cut the energy needed per unit of computation by a large factor,
and total data-centre electricity consumption has risen. Cheaper inference means
more inference: uses that were not worth the compute become worth it. The
projected electricity demand from AI training and serving is the current
highest-profile case of the argument, and the elasticity of demand for computation
appears to be well above 1.

**LED lighting.** Roughly an order of magnitude more efficient than incandescent.
Global installed lighting has expanded correspondingly, satellite imagery shows
outdoor lit area and radiance increasing year on year, and the light-pollution
literature treats this as a settled example of rebound in action.

**Electric vehicles.** Cheaper per mile to run than petrol cars, which by the
mechanism means more miles driven. The direct rebound here is measurable and
modest, and the emissions arithmetic still favours electrification substantially
— but the miles-driven increase is real, and it is why some analysts argue road
pricing has to accompany the transition.

**Cryptocurrency mining.** More efficient mining hardware does not reduce the
energy used to mine, because the difficulty adjusts and the equilibrium is set by
the price of the coin against the cost of power. This is a limiting case where
rebound is essentially exactly 100% by construction — an unusually clean natural
experiment in the mechanism.

**Water and land.** Efficient irrigation was expected to leave more water in
rivers. In several basins it has not, because farmers responded by irrigating more
land or switching to thirstier crops. The same argument runs in agriculture over
whether higher yields spare land or, by making farming more profitable, expand
the frontier.

## Deep Dive

### Defining rebound precisely

Let $S$ be the quantity of an energy service consumed, $E$ the energy input, and
$\epsilon = S/E$ the efficiency. Then $E = S/\epsilon$, and in logarithmic
changes

$$\hat{E} = \hat{S} - \hat{\epsilon}$$

The price of a unit of service is $p_S = p_E/\epsilon$, so an efficiency
improvement is a proportional fall in the service price:
$\hat{p}_S = \hat{p}_E - \hat{\epsilon}$. Holding the energy price fixed,
$\hat{p}_S = -\hat{\epsilon}$.

Let $\eta = -\,\partial \ln S / \partial \ln p_S \ge 0$ be the own-price
elasticity of demand for the service. Then $\hat{S} = \eta\,\hat{\epsilon}$, and

$$\boxed{\;\hat{E} = (\eta - 1)\,\hat{\epsilon}\;}$$

The **rebound** is $R = \eta$, usually quoted as a percentage. The regimes fall
out immediately:

- $\eta = 0$: $\hat E = -\hat\epsilon$. Energy falls one-for-one with efficiency.
- $0 < \eta < 1$: energy falls, but by less than the efficiency gain.
- $\eta = 1$: $\hat E = 0$. Exactly cancelled.
- $\eta > 1$: $\hat E > 0$. **Backfire.**

So the entire question of whether Jevons's strong claim holds for a given service
reduces to whether demand for that service is elastic. Stated this way it is
neither mysterious nor especially paradoxical — which is why the entry's `nature`
is a counterintuitive result rather than a contradiction.

### Why the measured elasticity is not the whole story

The formula above holds the energy price fixed. It does not. If efficiency
improvements reduce aggregate demand for energy, the energy price $p_E$ falls,
which reduces $p_S$ further and induces more service demand. Writing $\sigma$ for
the elasticity of energy supply, the general-equilibrium rebound exceeds the
partial-equilibrium figure whenever supply is not perfectly elastic. This is one
of several reasons economy-wide estimates run above direct ones.

Three further terms are omitted by the simple decomposition:

- **Substitution in production.** Cheaper energy services shift factor
  proportions toward energy. The magnitude depends on the elasticity of
  substitution between energy and capital, a parameter estimated across a very
  wide range in the literature and the single largest driver of disagreement
  between models.
- **Output effects.** Lower costs raise output, and higher output uses energy
  even at unchanged intensity.
- **Induced innovation.** Cheap energy services change what is worth inventing.
  This is the largest and least tractable channel; it is where the railway comes
  from in Jevons's account, and no model handles it convincingly.

### Saunders's growth-model result

Saunders (1992) embedded energy-augmenting technical change in a neoclassical
growth model with a constant-elasticity-of-substitution production function

$$Y = \left[\alpha (A_K K)^{\rho} + \beta (A_L L)^{\rho} + \gamma (A_E E)^{\rho}\right]^{1/\rho}$$

with elasticity of substitution $\sigma = 1/(1-\rho)$, and showed that an increase
in energy efficiency $A_E$ raises equilibrium energy use $E$ for a range of
plausible $\sigma$ and factor shares. With Cobb–Douglas production ($\sigma = 1$)
the result is backfire in the long run under standard assumptions about capital
accumulation.

The result is real but conditional, and its critics have concentrated on exactly
that conditionality: the conclusion is driven by $\sigma$ and by the treatment of
efficiency gains as costless, and neither is established empirically. This is the
technical core of the disagreement, and it is a disagreement about parameter
values in a model rather than about whether the mechanism exists.

### Empirical magnitudes worth carrying

| Channel | Typical estimate | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct, household space heating | 10–30% | reasonably firm |
| Direct, personal transport | 10–30% | reasonably firm |
| Direct, lighting (developed) | 5–20% | moderate |
| Direct, lighting (developing / historical) | can exceed 100% | case-specific |
| Indirect (re-spending) | 5–15% | weak |
| Economy-wide | 20% to >100% | model-dependent |

These are drawn from Sorrell's review and the subsequent literature. The pattern
is consistent: the better identified the estimate, the smaller and the more
partial it is; the broader the question, the larger the estimate and the weaker
the identification. That relationship is itself the most important thing to know
about this literature.

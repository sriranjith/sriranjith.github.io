## Discover

In ancient Greece there was a famous teacher named Protagoras. What he taught was
how to argue — how to stand up in front of a court and win.

A young man called Euathlus wanted lessons but had no money. So they made a deal.

"Learn from me now," said Protagoras, "and pay me nothing. You pay me the day you
win your first case in court. If you never win a case, you never owe me a thing."

Euathlus agreed. He studied. He finished the course. He was, by all accounts,
very good.

And then he did not take any cases. Not one. He hung around, did other things,
and never once set foot in a courtroom. No cases meant no first win, and no first
win meant no fee — forever.

Protagoras got tired of waiting. So he took Euathlus to court himself, to sue him
for the money. And on the way in he explained, quite cheerfully, that he could not
possibly lose:

"If I win this case, the judges will order you to pay me. And if I lose this
case, then you have won your first case — so our deal says you pay me. **Either
way, I get my money.**"

Euathlus had learned from the best. He stood up and said:

"If I win this case, the judges will say I owe nothing. And if I lose this case,
then I still have not won my first case — so our deal says I owe nothing.
**Either way, I pay nothing.**"

Two men. The same courtroom. The same two possible outcomes. And each of them has
just proved, from those same two outcomes, that he wins.

## Understand

Look carefully at what each man is doing, because they are doing the same trick
in opposite directions.

There are two possible verdicts, and each argument checks both.

| If the court rules… | Protagoras appeals to… | Euathlus appeals to… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Euathlus must pay** | the verdict — so he pays | the contract — he has not won a case, so he owes nothing |
| **Euathlus need not pay** | the contract — he has now won a case, so he pays | the verdict — so he owes nothing |

Read the table across and the trick is naked. On every single row, **the contract
and the verdict say opposite things.** And each man simply picks whichever of the
two happens to favour him on that row.

Protagoras uses the verdict on the top row and the contract on the bottom row.
Euathlus uses the contract on the top row and the verdict on the bottom row.
Neither of them ever says why his chosen authority should win when the two
conflict — because the moment either said it out loud, his own argument would
collapse on the other row.

### So what has actually gone wrong

Nothing is wrong with the logic. Both arguments have a perfectly good shape: two
possibilities, same conclusion from each, therefore the conclusion. That shape is
valid.

What is wrong is a *premise*, and it is the same missing premise in both cases.
Each argument assumes something it never states: that when the contract and the
verdict disagree, mine is the one that governs. They assume opposite things, so at
most one can be right, and as it happens neither is.

The deeper fault is in the contract itself. Protagoras wrote a condition — "when
you win your first case" — and then tried to apply it to the very lawsuit that is
deciding whether the condition has been met. Watch what that does:

- Euathlus owes the money exactly when he wins a case.
- The only case in play is this one, and he wins this one exactly when the court
  decides he does not owe the money.

Put those together and the contract is saying: **he owes the money exactly when he
does not owe the money.** That has no solution, in the same way that "this
sentence is false" has no solution. It is not that the answer is hard to find. It
is that the contract, turned on itself, does not specify one.

### The way out

Courts deal with this sort of thing all the time, and the cure is unglamorous:
**put the clock back into the contract.**

At the moment Protagoras files his suit, how many cases has Euathlus won? Zero.
The condition for payment has not been met. So the court should rule for
Euathlus: nothing is owed *yet*.

Now the world has changed. Euathlus has just won a case — his first. The
condition has now been met. Protagoras walks out of the courtroom, files a second
suit the next morning, and this time he is owed the money and he wins.

Protagoras' mistake was not bad logic. It was suing a day too early. He treated
"has won a case" as though it were a timeless fact about Euathlus, when it is a
fact about Euathlus *at a moment*, and the moment matters. Split the two lawsuits
apart in time and the circle is broken, because the condition is now being checked
at one time and settled at another. Nothing refers to itself any more.

That is the standard resolution, and it is why this entry is marked *dissolved*
rather than *debated*. There is no genuine contradiction here. There is a
carelessly drafted contract and two clever men each pretending not to notice.

## Examples

**The crocodile.** A crocodile snatches a child from the riverbank and says to the
mother: "I will return your child if — and only if — you correctly guess what I
am going to do."

The mother says: "You are not going to return him."

Now the crocodile is stuck in exactly Protagoras' machine. If he keeps the child,
her guess was right, so he owes her the child. If he returns the child, her guess
was wrong, so he was entitled to keep him. The ancient sources treat this as a
companion puzzle to the Court, and the diagnosis is the same: the crocodile made
a promise whose condition is settled by whether he keeps the promise.

**Corax and Tisias.** Almost the same story is told of the rhetoricians Corax and
Tisias — teacher and pupil, unpaid fee, mutually reversing arguments. In that
version the judges throw both of them out with the line "a bad egg from a bad
crow", which puns on Corax's name, the Greek *korax* meaning crow. That two
independent teacher-and-pupil versions circulated in antiquity is a reason to
treat the Protagoras story as a fable about sophists rather than as a court
record.

**What a modern contract would say.** Real contingency-fee agreements have exactly
this structure — "no win, no fee" — and they do not generate paradoxes, because
they are drafted by people who have thought about the edge cases. A working clause
reads something like: *the fee becomes payable upon the client's first judgment in
his favour in any matter other than a dispute between the client and the
attorney, and in no event later than five years from the date of this agreement.*

Two small additions do all the work. The first carves the enforcement suit out of
the condition, so the contract can never be triggered by its own enforcement. The
second sets a deadline, so that never taking a case is not a way of getting free
tuition. Protagoras, who taught argument for a living, failed to draft either.

**The four possibilities, checked.** People often suspect a hidden third option.
There is not one, and it is worth confirming.

| Verdict | Immediate effect | Consequence under the contract | Stable? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Euathlus pays | He pays | He lost, so no fee was due — the verdict was wrong | No |
| Euathlus does not pay | He pays nothing | He won, so a fee is now due — but the verdict said no | No |
| Court declines to rule | Nothing happens | Condition still unmet, so nothing is due | Yes |
| Court rules "not yet", Protagoras refiles | He pays after the second suit | Condition met before the second suit | Yes |

Only the last two rows survive, and only the last one gets Protagoras his money.
Gellius reports that the judges took the third row: they refused to decide and
adjourned the case to a distant date.

## Explore

The interesting disagreements are not about whether there is a real
contradiction — there is not — but about what a court should actually do.

**The sequencing view.** This is the majority position and the one set out above.
The condition is time-indexed; at filing it is unmet; Protagoras loses the first
suit and wins the second. Its attraction is that it requires no new doctrine, only
the observation that a condition precedent is evaluated at a moment.

**The objection from finality.** Legal systems bar re-litigating settled matters.
If the second suit counts as the same claim as the first, *res judicata* blocks
it and Euathlus walks away having paid nothing — which would mean the sequencing
solution works in logic and fails in a courtroom. The usual answer is that a claim
which had not yet accrued when the first judgment was entered is a different
claim, so the bar does not apply. That answer is standard but it is a doctrine of
positive law, not a truth about the situation. **The circle is broken by an
institution, not by an argument**, and if the institution had different rules the
outcome would differ.

**The maturity view.** A minority position holds that the debt matures at the
instant the judgment is pronounced, so a court that has ruled for Euathlus should
in the same breath recognise the fee as now due, and might as well order payment
immediately. This gets Protagoras his money in one hearing at the price of a
verdict that changes the facts it was based on while it is being delivered. Most
commentators think that is worse than making him file twice.

**The void-for-uncertainty view.** A third response is that the agreement simply
fails: a condition that cannot be coherently applied to the case at hand is no
condition at all, and the court should declare the clause unenforceable and send
both men away. Gellius' judges effectively did a version of this. It is tidy, and
it has the disadvantage of rewarding the party who exploited the drafting.

**The implied-term view.** What a modern court would most likely do is read in a
term neither man wrote: that Euathlus undertook to practise in good faith, or that
"his first case" was never intended to include a suit brought by Protagoras
himself. Courts routinely supply the term the parties would obviously have agreed
to had they thought about it. This makes the paradox a drafting failure rather
than a logical event, which is very probably what it is.

**Is it a paradox at all?** Gellius does not present it as one. He files it under
*antistrephonta*, arguments that can be turned back with equal force on the person
who made them, and he treats the genre as a rhetorical curiosity — a set piece for
showing off. On that reading the story is a joke at the sophists' expense: the man
who taught that any case can be argued either way is beaten by a pupil arguing it
the other way. It earns its place among paradoxes because the two arguments look
symmetric and compelling, and because working out precisely why they are not turns
out to be instructive. But nothing here forces anyone to abandon a principle,
which is exactly the difference between an *apparent* contradiction and the real
thing.

## Deep Dive

### Both arguments in standard form

Let $p$ stand for "Euathlus must pay the fee as a result of this suit", and let
$w$ stand for "Euathlus wins this case". The two are complementary by
stipulation: $w \leftrightarrow \neg p$.

The contract is the biconditional

$$C: \quad p \leftrightarrow \text{(Euathlus has won his first case)}$$

Protagoras' argument, as a constructive dilemma over the exhaustive disjunction
$w \vee \neg w$:

1. If $\neg w$ (he loses), the judgment orders payment: $p$.
2. If $w$ (he wins), then he has won his first case, so by $C$: $p$.
3. $w \vee \neg w$.
4. Therefore $p$.

Euathlus' argument, over the same disjunction:

1. If $w$, the judgment orders no payment: $\neg p$.
2. If $\neg w$, he has not won a case, so by $C$: $\neg p$.
3. $w \vee \neg w$.
4. Therefore $\neg p$.

Both are instances of constructive dilemma, which is valid. Two valid arguments
with contradictory conclusions cannot both be sound, so at least one premise set
is inconsistent. In fact both are, and for the same reason.

### The suppressed premise

Each argument uses two distinct sources of obligation — the court's judgment and
the contract — and each horn of each dilemma invokes exactly one of them. Set out
what both sources say on each branch:

| Branch | Judgment says | Contract says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\neg w$ (Euathlus loses) | $p$ | $\neg p$ |
| $w$ (Euathlus wins) | $\neg p$ | $p$ |

On **both** branches the two sources conflict. Protagoras' dilemma is therefore
sound only if the judgment governs on the first branch and the contract governs
on the second; Euathlus' is sound only under the reverse assignment. Neither
argument states its priority rule, and the two rules are inconsistent with each
other. This is the complete diagnosis: the arguments are valid, their premise
sets are inconsistent, and the inconsistency is hidden because the priority
premise is never written down.

### Why the contract has no solution

Suppose we try to make the contract self-applying — that is, let this very suit
count as "his first case". Then the second conjunct of $C$ becomes $w$, and $C$
reduces to

$$p \leftrightarrow w \leftrightarrow \neg p$$

so $p \leftrightarrow \neg p$. This is precisely the Liar's equation, and it has
no solution. The contract, applied to its own enforcement, is not merely silent —
it is unsatisfiable.

The sign matters, and the contrast is illuminating. Had Protagoras written "you
pay me when you **lose** your first case", self-application would give
$p \leftrightarrow \neg w \leftrightarrow p$, a tautology satisfied by both
assignments. That contract is not contradictory; it is *underdetermined*, with two
equally admissible outcomes and nothing to choose between them. The first case is
a Liar sentence; the second is a truth-teller. Both are defective, and they are
defective in different ways.

Viewed this way, the question "who should win?" is a request for a fixed point of
a self-referential condition, and the answer is that the operator has none. No
ruling can be derived from the contract, because the contract does not determine
one.

### Stratification, again

The repair is the same one Tarski applied to truth: forbid the predicate from
applying to its own instance, by introducing levels. Here the levels are
temporal.

Index the condition by time. Let $W(t)$ mean "Euathlus has won at least one case
strictly before $t$", and let the contract read

$$p(t) \leftrightarrow W(t)$$

Let $t_1$ be the filing of Protagoras' first suit. $W(t_1)$ is false — Euathlus
has won nothing before $t_1$ — so $p(t_1)$ is false and the court must rule for
Euathlus. Let $t_2 > t_1$ be any time after that judgment. Now $W(t_2)$ is true,
so $p(t_2)$ holds, and a suit filed at $t_2$ succeeds.

The circularity is gone because the condition is evaluated at $t_1$ using only
facts settled before $t_1$, while the judgment that changes those facts occurs
after. No quantity is defined in terms of itself. This is stratification in the
Tarskian sense, with time playing the role of the metalanguage level, and it is
the general shape of every fix for a self-referential specification: find a
well-founded ordering along which the definition can be unrolled.

Whether the second suit is admissible is then a question of preclusion doctrine
rather than logic. In systems where a cause of action that had not accrued at the
time of the first judgment is not barred by that judgment, Protagoras recovers.
That is the usual rule, and the parallel is worth making explicit: **logic
identifies that the loop must be cut and where; it does not supply the authority
to cut it.** In the Liar there is no such authority, which is why the Liar is
still open and this is not.

### The genre

Gellius' word for the form is $\alpha\nu\tau\iota\sigma\tau\rho\epsilon\phi\omega\nu$ —
*antistrephon*, "turning back" — an argument constructed so that it can be
reversed against its author with the same force. The reversal is not a refutation
in the ordinary sense: Euathlus does not identify Protagoras' error. He builds the
mirror image and lets the symmetry do the work. That is a rhetorical technique
rather than a logical one, and recognising it as such is part of the resolution.
A pair of mutually reversing arguments is evidence that an unstated premise is
being switched, and the productive response is always to go looking for it rather
than to conclude that the world is contradictory.

## History

**What is documented.** Protagoras of Abdera, roughly 490 to 420 BCE, was the
first and most famous of the sophists — professional teachers of rhetoric and
argument. He is the author of "man is the measure of all things", and ancient
sources agree that he was the first Greek to charge for teaching. Diogenes
Laërtius (IX.50–56) reports a fee of a hundred minae, an enormous sum, along with
the tradition that his book on the gods was publicly burned in Athens and that he
was prosecuted for impiety. That last part is doubted by modern scholars.

Euathlus is another matter. Nothing whatever is known about him outside this
story. He may have existed; he functions in the anecdote as a name for "the
pupil", and there is no independent evidence that Protagoras ever sued anyone.

**What is tradition.** The earliest surviving account of the lawsuit is in Aulus
Gellius' *Attic Nights*, Book V chapter 10, written in the second century CE —
around six hundred years after Protagoras died. Gellius does not claim to be
reporting history. He introduces the story as an illustration of the class of
arguments the Greeks called *antistrephonta*, and he says he found it in the Greek
writers, without naming them. Related versions circulated in the later rhetorical
literature.

Gellius' ending is the part most retellings drop, and it is the most sensible
thing in the story: the judges, unwilling to be caught in a case whose verdict
would undo its own basis, declined to decide and adjourned it to a distant date.

**The rival version.** A structurally identical story is told of Corax and Tisias,
the semi-legendary Sicilian founders of Greek rhetoric — again a teacher, a pupil,
an unpaid fee, and a pair of reversing arguments, ending with the judges dismissing
both parties as "a bad egg from a bad crow". The existence of two independent
teacher-and-pupil versions, attached to different founders of the same profession,
is strong evidence that this is a piece of professional folklore rather than a
remembered case. It is the kind of story a discipline tells about itself.

**Afterwards.** The puzzle passed into the standard stock of ancient logical
problems alongside the Liar and the Sorites, and the Megarian and Stoic logicians
who catalogued such things treated them as a group. Medieval logicians discussed
it among the *insolubilia*. In the modern era it is a fixture of introductory logic
courses as the standard illustration of the constructive dilemma and its reversal,
and it appears in legal-writing literature as the standard cautionary tale about
conditions precedent. It has generated no research programme, because unlike the
Liar it stays solved.

## Why It Matters

**It is the clearest demonstration that a valid argument can be worthless.** Both
men argue impeccably. Both reach their conclusion by a rule of inference nobody
disputes. The failure is entirely in a premise that neither of them stated, and
which each needed in a form incompatible with the other's. Anyone who has been
talked into something by an argument they could not fault will recognise the
shape: when two symmetric arguments give opposite results, the productive move is
never to check the reasoning again but to go looking for the premise being
quietly swapped.

**It is why conditions precedent are drafted the way they are.** Every contingent
fee agreement, milestone payment, earn-out clause and performance bonus contains a
condition whose satisfaction has to be determined by someone. If the determination
can itself trigger the condition, the clause eats itself. Commercial drafting
handles this with carve-outs and long-stop dates as a matter of routine, and this
paradox is the reason the routine exists.

**It shows what institutions are for.** The contract, considered in isolation, has
no solution. A court nevertheless produces one, because a judgment is an *act*
rather than a discovery — it does not have to be derivable from the contract to be
binding. Legal systems are full of machinery whose only purpose is to terminate
loops that logic leaves open: finality, limitation periods, the rule that a court
of last resort is right because it is last. The Paradox of the Court is a small,
complete demonstration of why that machinery is not optional.

**It marks the boundary of a real paradox.** Set it next to the Liar. The
structures are nearly identical — a condition whose satisfaction depends on the
verdict about it — but the Liar has no adjudicator, no clock, and no way to
stratify without cost, and it is still argued about. The Court has all three, and
was over as soon as anyone thought about it carefully. Comparing them is the
fastest way to see what makes a paradox genuinely hard rather than merely clever.

## Modern Relevance

**Self-referential legal provisions.** Constitutions that specify how they may be
amended raise the question whether the amendment clause can amend itself, and
whether an entrenched clause can be entrenched by ordinary means. This is
Protagoras' problem at national scale, and it has been litigated seriously in
several jurisdictions. The solutions are the same two: stratify — treat
constitutional and ordinary law as different levels — or vest the decision in an
institution whose ruling is final by fiat.

**Smart contracts and oracles.** Code that executes an agreement automatically has
no judge to adjourn the case. If a contract's payout condition depends on data
that depends on the payout — a common failure in prediction markets, insurance
protocols and governance tokens — the contract has no fixed point and will
deadlock, revert, or resolve arbitrarily depending on implementation details.
Auditors look for exactly this circularity, and the standard fix is Protagoras'
missing clause: exclude the resolution mechanism from the domain of the condition.

**Circular references in computation.** A spreadsheet cell whose value depends on
itself, a build system whose target depends on its own output, a package manager
resolving mutually constraining version bounds — all are the same structure, and
all are handled the same way: detect the cycle, then either break it with a
well-founded ordering or refuse the job. The error message a spreadsheet gives you
is a small automated version of Gellius' judges declining to rule.

**Bootstrapping governance.** A body that votes on the rules for its own votes,
an accreditation agency that accredits itself, a standards committee that
standardises its own procedure — each faces a version of this, and each resolves
it in practice by appealing to some prior authority or by fixing an arbitrary
starting point and moving on. There is no way to derive the first rule from the
rules. That fact is old, and this is where it was first written down clearly.

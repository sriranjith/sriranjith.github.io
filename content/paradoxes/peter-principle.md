## Discover

A school in a mid-sized American city. A teacher who is genuinely exceptional
at her job — clear, patient, inventive — gets promoted to head of department.
She is good at that too: organised, fair, effective at supporting the other
teachers. She gets promoted to assistant principal.

She is a reasonable assistant principal. The work is different — more
administrative, more political, less about teaching — but she manages. She gets
promoted to principal.

She is not a good principal. The skills that made her exceptional in the
classroom — direct engagement with individual students, a teacher's instinct for
when a child is lost — are not what the job requires. What it requires is budget
management, union negotiation, public relations, political navigation. She is
promoted above her competence.

And she stays. Principals do not get demoted. The school now has a mediocre
principal who was once an exceptional teacher — and has lost both.

## Understand

Laurence Peter's observation is elegantly simple. Promotion in a hierarchy is
based on **performance in the current role**. Competence in the current role
does not guarantee competence in the next one, because the two roles require
different skills. The only way the promotions stop is when you reach a role you
are not good at — and then they stop there.

In a hierarchy with unlimited time, every position will eventually be occupied
by someone who is incompetent at it. New positions at the top are filled by
people promoted from below — i.e., by people who were good at a lower job.
They may or may not be good at the new one. If they are, they get promoted
again. The process terminates only at incompetence.

Peter calls this the **Peter Principle** and the terminal position the **Peter
Level**. His corollary: **In time, every post tends to be occupied by an
employee who is incompetent to carry out its duties.**

### Why don't hierarchies collapse?

If every position is filled by someone incompetent, how does anything get done?

Peter's answer: the work is done by employees who have not yet reached their
level of incompetence. Every functioning organisation depends on the people who
are still on their way up — the ones who are currently competent and have not
yet been promoted past it.

### What the principle does not say

It does not say that all senior people are incompetent. Some people are promoted
into roles they are also good at — and some organisations deliberately promote
people into roles adjacent to their skills rather than above them. The principle
describes a *tendency*, not a law. It operates reliably when promotion is based
on individual job performance rather than on predicted performance in the next
role.

| Top sales performer | Sales manager | Managing a team is not making sales |
| Great surgeon | Hospital administrator | Administration is bureaucracy, not surgery |
| Brilliant researcher | Research director | The role is politics and funding, not research |
| Exceptional teacher | School principal | Administration is not teaching |

## Explore

**The empirical evidence.** For decades the Peter Principle was treated as a
clever observation without strong evidence. A 2019 study by Benson, Li, and
Shue, published in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, examined data from
214 companies and 53,035 sales workers promoted to manager. They found that the
best individual salespeople were the most likely to be promoted to manager, but
the worst managers — and that the gap was large and robust. The best salespeople
made managers who scored 7.5% lower on a management quality index than average
salespeople who were promoted. The Peter Principle is real.

**Why organisations tolerate it.** Demoting someone who was promoted is
socially costly. It signals that the original promotion was a mistake, which
embarrasses whoever made it. It is humiliating for the person demoted. Most
organisations find it easier to manage around an incompetent person than to
correct the mistake.

**Lateral promotions as a fix.** One response is to make career advancement
possible without requiring management responsibility — technical tracks, staff
engineering roles, senior individual contributor paths. These let exceptional
performers advance in status and pay without requiring them to manage people.
The assumption is that what made them good at technical work will not necessarily
make them good managers.

**The promotion decision problem.** The root issue is that promotion requires
predicting future performance from past performance in a different role. This is
hard. Research on managerial selection shows that structured interviews and
work-sample tests predict future job performance better than track records in
the previous role — but most organisations still promote primarily on the
basis of past performance.

## History

**1950s–1960s.** Laurence Peter, working as a school administrator and teacher
trainer, observes a pattern: the most effective classroom teachers are promoted
to administrative roles, and some become effective administrators. But the ones
who are worst at administration are often the most distinguished former teachers.
He begins formalising the observation.

**1969.** Peter and Hull publish *The Peter Principle: Why Things Always Go
Wrong*. It is written with humour — Hull was a writer, not an academic — but
the underlying argument is serious. It becomes an international bestseller and
spawns a genre of comic-serious management writing.

**1970s–1980s.** The principle becomes part of general management discourse.
Business schools begin citing it; it appears in discussions of organisational
design and personnel management. Researchers begin looking for empirical tests.

**2019.** Benson, Li, and Shue publish the large-scale empirical confirmation in
the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*. The Peter Principle moves from clever
observation to documented phenomenon.

## Why It Matters

**Personnel decisions.** Organisations that understand the Peter Principle can
design promotion criteria that predict future performance rather than reward past
performance. Instead of "who is best at this job?" the question becomes "who
will be best at the next job?" These are different questions with different
answers.

**Career design.** Individuals can use the principle to make better decisions
about whether to accept promotions. A promotion to a role that requires
different skills is a risk — the new role may be worse at than the old one,
and the old role is gone.

**Organisational resilience.** An organisation aware of the principle can build
in correction mechanisms: trial periods before permanent promotion, lateral
moves, alternative advancement tracks, and cultures where it is acceptable to
return to a role that fits better.

## Modern Relevance

**Startup scaling.** Many successful startups fail at the transition to larger
companies. The founder who was exceptional at building a ten-person company is
not always good at running a five-hundred-person one. The skills required are
genuinely different, and the Peter Principle applies.

**Technology companies.** The tension between individual contributor tracks and
management tracks in software companies is a direct response to the Peter
Principle. The recognition that "the best engineer in the room" is not the same
as "the best manager of engineers" has produced explicit career ladders designed
to let people advance without being forced into roles they will be bad at.

**AI teams.** Researchers who are exceptional at machine learning research are
regularly promoted into roles that require grant writing, team management, and
organisational politics. The promotion was earned; the new role is a different
job. The principle predicts problems, and organisations building AI research
teams are navigating it in real time.

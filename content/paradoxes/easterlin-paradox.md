## Discover

Ask a hundred people in the same town how happy they are, and ask them what they
earn. The pattern is dependable and it is exactly what you would guess: the
richer ones report being happier. This shows up in every country anyone has
checked, in every decade, using every survey.

So here is a prediction that seems to follow. Take that same town and make
everybody in it three times richer over the next forty years. Better houses,
better food, better medicine, better everything. Average happiness should climb.

In 1974 an economist named Richard Easterlin went to look at the data for whole
countries, and found the climb was not there.

The United States between the end of the war and 1970 had become dramatically
richer. Real income per person had risen enormously. Cars, televisions, central
heating, antibiotics — things that had been luxuries became ordinary. And the
share of Americans telling pollsters they were "very happy" was, as far as anyone
could tell, about the same at the end as at the start.

The same result appeared everywhere he looked. Rich countries did not report
being much happier than poor ones, once you got past a certain point. And no
country that got richer seemed to get happier.

**Inside a country, money and happiness go together. Across time, they come
apart.** Both facts are drawn from the same surveys, asking the same question.
Both cannot be a simple story about money buying wellbeing — and fifty years
later, economists are still fighting about which one to believe.

## Understand

The Easterlin paradox is not a single fact. It is a *tension between* facts, and
you need all three of them on the table.

**Fact one.** Within any one country at any one time, income and reported
happiness are positively related. Richer individuals are, on average, more
satisfied with their lives.

**Fact two.** Across countries, the relationship is much weaker than fact one
would lead you to expect — and in Easterlin's original data, close to absent among
countries above a modest income level.

**Fact three.** Over long stretches of time, as a country's income per head rises
substantially, average reported happiness does not rise with it.

Facts one and three are the collision. If money produces happiness, giving
everyone more of it ought to produce more happiness. It appears not to.

### The explanation Easterlin proposed

His answer was that **what matters is not how much you have, but how much you have
compared with something.** Two comparisons in particular.

**Compared with other people.** Income buys status, and status is a fixed pot. If
your income doubles and everyone else's doubles too, you have not moved up the
ladder — you are standing on the same rung of a taller ladder. This is not a new
idea; Thorstein Veblen described conspicuous consumption in 1899 and James
Duesenberry made the relative income hypothesis explicit in 1949. Easterlin's
contribution was to show that it fits the happiness data.

**Compared with what you have got used to.** The other comparison is with your own
past. People adapt. A pay rise is thrilling and then becomes the new normal, and
what was once an aspiration becomes an expectation. Psychologists call this
hedonic adaptation, and it is well documented for many life events.

Put the two together and you get a treadmill. Economic growth raises everyone's
income and everyone's expectations at roughly the same rate, so the difference —
which is what registers as happiness — stays where it was.

### The reason this is still an argument

Three things make the question genuinely hard, and none of them has been settled.

**Measuring happiness is measuring a self-report on a bounded scale.** Surveys ask
people to place themselves on a scale — "very happy / fairly happy / not too
happy", or a ladder from 0 to 10. That scale has a ceiling. If people
reinterpret what a "7" means as society changes, a flat average could hide real
improvement. Conversely, if the scale means what it says, a flat average is a flat
average. There is no external instrument to check against.

**Different questions measure different things.** "How satisfied are you with your
life, all things considered?" and "How do you feel right now?" produce different
answers and behave differently against income. Life *evaluation* — the reflective
judgement — tracks income noticeably better than moment-to-moment emotional
*experience* does. Much of the modern dispute turns out to be about which of these
is being discussed.

**The time series is short and the noise is large.** Detecting whether a slow trend
exists in a noisy annual series over thirty years is statistically hard.
Reasonable analysts using the same data and different specifications reach
different conclusions, and both can be right about their own specification.

### Where the argument stands

This entry does not declare a winner, because the field has not.

- **Easterlin's position** is that the long-run time-series relationship is
  genuinely absent, that short-run correlations reflect business cycles rather
  than growth, and that the accumulated evidence across many countries now
  supports him more strongly than in 1974.
- **Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers** argued in 2008 that with better data —
  the Gallup World Poll, covering far more countries — the relationship between
  wellbeing and *log* income is strong, similar in magnitude within countries,
  across countries and over time, and shows no point at which it flattens out.
  On their reading, the paradox was a data limitation.
- **The 2023 adversarial collaboration** between Daniel Kahneman and Matthew
  Killingsworth, arbitrated by Barbara Mellers, resolved one adjacent dispute and
  showed how such disputes can be resolved: they found that a plateau exists, but
  only for the least happy minority of people, and that for most people wellbeing
  keeps rising with income.

That last result is about the *cross-section* — the relationship between income
and wellbeing among individuals at one time. It is important, and it does not
directly settle Easterlin's claim, which is about what happens to a whole country
over decades. Keeping those two questions apart is the single most useful thing a
reader can take from this entry.

## Examples

**The three countries that carry the argument.** Easterlin and his co-authors keep
returning to these because the income growth is enormous and the time series is
long:

| Country | Period | Growth in real income per head | Change in reported wellbeing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| United States | 1972–2016 | roughly doubled | share saying "very happy" in the General Social Survey stays around 30–34% with no trend |
| Japan | 1958–1991 | roughly six-fold | life satisfaction essentially flat |
| China | 1990–2010 | per capita consumption roughly quadrupled | life satisfaction fell, then partially recovered; no net gain |

Each of these has a documented objection attached to it, and the objections are
part of the evidence rather than a footnote to it.

The Japanese series, the most striking of the three, is assembled from a survey
whose question wording and response categories changed several times over the
period. Stevenson and Wolfers made this criticism directly, and argued that when
the series is broken at the wording changes, the segments show increases. Easterlin
has disputed the reconstruction. The reader should know that the single most
quoted case in this literature rests on a contested splice.

The Chinese case is cleaner in one respect — the growth is beyond dispute — and
messier in another, since the period covers mass layoffs from state enterprises,
the collapse of the social safety net, and a large rise in inequality. Whether the
flat happiness reflects adaptation or reflects real losses offsetting real gains
is not identified by the data.

**What the cross-section looks like.** In the Gallup World Poll, average life
evaluation on the 0–10 Cantril ladder rises close to linearly in **log** GDP per
capita. Roughly:

| GDP per capita (PPP) | Typical average ladder score |
| --- | --- |
| $1,000 | around 3.5 |
| $4,000 | around 4.5 |
| $16,000 | around 5.5 |
| $64,000 | around 6.5 |

These are indicative rather than precise, but they show the shape that matters:
each *quadrupling* of income buys roughly the same increment, so the absolute
dollars needed to move the needle grow without limit. Stevenson and Wolfers's
central claim is that this line does not visibly bend down at the top — there is
no satiation point where extra income stops helping.

Note what the log form already concedes to Easterlin. It means diminishing returns
are enormous. A country going from $40,000 to $50,000 per head, a 25% rise, gains
about a fifth of what a country going from $1,000 to $2,000 gains. A flat-looking
time series in a rich country is exactly what a log relationship with slow growth
predicts, which is one reason the two camps can look at the same data
differently.

**The $75,000 story, and its correction.** This is the clearest worked example of
how the field actually resolves things.

*2010.* Kahneman and Deaton analysed 450,000 responses to the Gallup-Healthways
daily poll of US residents. They separated two measures: **life evaluation** (the
Cantril ladder) and **emotional wellbeing** (reports of happiness, stress, sadness
and worry "yesterday"). Life evaluation rose steadily with log income with no
plateau. Emotional wellbeing rose with log income *up to about $75,000 a year* and
then flattened. The $75,000 figure went around the world.

*2021.* Killingsworth used a different instrument: a smartphone app pinging people
at random moments and asking how they felt right then — 1,725,994 reports from
33,391 employed US adults. He found experienced wellbeing rising log-linearly with
income across the whole range, **including above $75,000**. No plateau.

*2023.* Rather than trading papers, Kahneman and Killingsworth ran an adversarial
collaboration with Barbara Mellers as arbiter: agree in advance on the analysis,
then run it. The result was that both had been partly right.

| Group | What happens to wellbeing as income rises above ~$100,000 |
| --- | --- |
| The least happy ~15–20% | Flattens — more income does not help |
| The middle | Continues to rise |
| The happiest ~30% | Rises, and the slope gets *steeper* |

The earlier plateau was largely an artefact of a ceiling in Kahneman and Deaton's
dichotomous measure combined with the averaging of these different groups.
Suffering that has a non-financial cause — bereavement, depression, chronic pain,
a bad marriage — is not relieved by money, and averaging those people together
with everyone else flattens the curve at the top.

This is a model of how the dispute should be conducted. It is also, again, a
result about individuals in a cross-section, not about nations over time.

## Explore

### The three ways to be right

**Easterlin's position: the paradox is real and has strengthened.** His central
claim has narrowed and sharpened since 1974. It is now specifically about the
**long-run** time series: over periods long enough to average out business cycles
— he uses ten years and more — there is no significant relationship between the
growth rate of income and the growth rate of happiness, in either rich or poor
countries. The 2010 PNAS paper with Laura Angelescu McVey, Malgorzata Switek,
Onnicha Sawangfa and Jacqueline Zweig assembled series for 37 countries, including
17 developing and 11 transition economies, and reported exactly that. Short-run
correlations exist, he argues, because recessions make people miserable and
recoveries relieve them — which tells you about fluctuations, not about growth.

**Stevenson and Wolfers's position: the paradox was a data artefact.** Their 2008
Brookings paper argued that earlier work had too few countries, too short a
period, and measures too coarse to detect a relationship that is real but
logarithmic. With the Gallup World Poll they found: a strong cross-country
gradient in log income; a within-country gradient of similar magnitude; no
evidence of satiation at high incomes; and, in the time-series data available,
positive associations between growth and rising wellbeing in most countries. They
were explicit that this undermines the relative-income story, because a purely
relative account predicts no cross-country gradient at all.

**The reconciling position.** Several researchers — including Deaton, and Easterlin
himself in places — accept that the cross-sectional and cross-national gradients
are real and logarithmic, while holding that something specific about *national
growth over time* prevents the gains from materialising. Candidates: rising
aspirations moving in lockstep with income; growth accruing disproportionately to
the top so that the median household's log income barely moves; and social costs
of growth — insecurity, longer hours, weaker communities, greater inequality —
offsetting the material gains.

That third candidate is worth taking seriously because it makes the paradox a
finding about *how* countries have grown rather than about the limits of money.
It is also much harder to test.

### The methodological fault lines

**Cardinality of the scale.** Comparing "the average ladder score rose 0.2 points"
across countries or decades assumes the scale means the same thing to everyone,
always. Analyses that treat the responses as merely ordinal, and that allow for
different people using the scale differently, can reverse conclusions. This is a
live technical literature and it is not a side issue: the entire dispute is
conducted in units whose interpersonal meaning is assumed rather than established.

**Ceiling effects.** Reported happiness is bounded; income is not. If a large share
of a rich country's population is already at or near the top of the scale, growth
cannot show up. This mechanically produces something that looks like the paradox
without any adaptation or comparison at work.

**Which measure.** Life evaluation, positive affect, negative affect and
domain-specific satisfaction behave differently against income. Studies that
disagree frequently turn out to be using different dependent variables. Reading
any claim in this area requires knowing which question was asked.

**Identification.** Almost none of this evidence is causal. Income and happiness
are correlated for many reasons — healthier people earn more, happier people are
promoted, and both respond to third factors. The small number of quasi-experimental
studies, including lottery-winner studies and cash-transfer trials, generally find
positive but modest effects of income on wellbeing, which is consistent with a
range of positions in the main debate.

**Inequality.** The distributional point deserves separate mention. In the United
States, real median household income grew far more slowly than GDP per capita from
the 1970s onward. If wellbeing tracks the log income of the typical household
rather than the mean, then a large part of the "flat happiness despite growth"
observation is explained without any appeal to adaptation or status. Whether this
rescues the material account or vindicates the social account of the paradox
depends on what you thought the paradox was claiming.

### What both sides agree on

It is worth being explicit, because the disagreement is narrower than the
literature's tone suggests. Both camps accept that:

- Income and wellbeing are positively related in cross-sections, robustly.
- The relationship is approximately linear in **log** income, so absolute gains
  matter less and less.
- Very large income differences correspond to real and substantial wellbeing
  differences — nobody disputes that the average Norwegian reports a better life
  than the average Burundian.
- Relative comparison and adaptation are real psychological phenomena with
  measurable effects.

The dispute is about the magnitude of the time-series relationship in the long
run, and about how much of the cross-sectional gradient survives when everybody
moves together.

## History

**1949.** James Duesenberry published *Income, Saving and the Theory of Consumer
Behavior*, introducing the relative income hypothesis: consumption and
satisfaction depend on income relative to others and to one's own past. The idea
that Easterlin later found in the happiness data was already on the table.

**1965.** Hadley Cantril published *The Pattern of Human Concerns*, with the
self-anchoring ladder scale and cross-national survey data. This is the data
Easterlin used.

**1974.** Richard Easterlin published "Does Economic Growth Improve the Human Lot?
Some Empirical Evidence" in *Nations and Households in Economic Growth: Essays in
Honor of Moses Abramovitz*, edited by Paul David and Melvin Reder (Academic Press),
pages 89–125. The paper is careful and hedged — Easterlin was explicit about the
limitations of the data — and it invented a field.

**1970s–1990s.** The finding was mostly ignored by economists and taken up by
psychologists. Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell's 1971 "hedonic treadmill" and
their 1978 lottery-winners-and-accident-victims study supplied the psychological
mechanism. Ed Diener built the measurement literature. Economics stayed away,
largely because the profession was uncomfortable with self-reported data.

**1995.** Easterlin published "Will Raising the Incomes of All Increase the
Happiness of All?" in the *Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization*,
restating and extending the case. Around the same time Robert Frank, Andrew Oswald
and Richard Layard began bringing the material into mainstream economics.

**2005.** Layard's *Happiness: Lessons from a New Science* took the argument to a
general audience and to policymakers, arguing directly for taxation and policy
reform on the basis of the relative income effect.

**2008.** Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers published "Economic Growth and
Subjective Well-Being: Reassessing the Easterlin Paradox" in the *Brookings Papers
on Economic Activity*. Using the Gallup World Poll and reconstructed national
series, they argued the paradox does not survive better data. This is the paper
that made the question contested rather than settled.

**2010.** Two important papers. Easterlin and colleagues published "The
happiness–income paradox revisited" in *PNAS*, drawing the long-run/short-run
distinction and reporting no long-run relationship across 37 countries. Kahneman
and Deaton published "High income improves evaluation of life but not emotional
well-being" in *PNAS*, splitting the outcome measure and reporting the $75,000
plateau for emotional wellbeing.

**2012.** Easterlin, Robson Morgan, Switek and Fei Wang published "China's life
satisfaction, 1990–2010" in *PNAS*, using the country with the largest income
growth in history as the strongest available test.

**2021.** Matthew Killingsworth published "Experienced well-being rises with
income, even above $75,000 per year" in *PNAS*, using experience sampling from
33,391 people and finding no plateau.

**2023.** Kahneman, Killingsworth and Mellers published "Income and emotional
well-being: A conflict resolved" in *PNAS*, reporting the results of a
pre-registered adversarial collaboration. Kahneman died in 2024; the paper is
among the last of his career and is widely cited as a model for how scientific
disputes should be conducted.

## Why It Matters

**It made subjective wellbeing a legitimate economic variable.** Before Easterlin,
economics inferred welfare from behaviour and treated what people said about their
own lives as unusable. There is now a large empirical literature, a dedicated
journal, national statistical programmes measuring wellbeing in the UK, France,
New Zealand, Bhutan and elsewhere, and an OECD standard for how to ask the
question. Whether or not Easterlin's specific claim survives, the field would not
exist without the paper.

**It supplied the strongest empirical case against GDP as a welfare target.** The
argument that growth is not the same thing as improvement had been made on
theoretical grounds for decades. Easterlin gave it data. The Stiglitz–Sen–Fitoussi
Commission (2009), the OECD Better Life Index, the UK's national wellbeing
measures and the World Happiness Report all descend from that move.

**It sharpened the distinction between a mean and a distribution.** Much of the
force of the modern critique is that "national income rose" and "the typical
household's income rose" came apart in several rich countries after 1970. Working
out which of those wellbeing tracks is now a central question in the economics of
inequality, and it started as a puzzle about happiness surveys.

**It is a case study in how measurement disputes are settled.** The 2023
adversarial collaboration is the concrete payoff. Two researchers with published
opposing findings agreed in advance on a joint analysis with a neutral arbiter,
and discovered that the disagreement was located somewhere neither had specified —
in a subgroup. That method is being adopted in other contested areas precisely
because of how well it worked here.

## Modern Relevance

**National wellbeing statistics.** The UK Office for National Statistics has asked
four wellbeing questions of a large sample since 2011; New Zealand runs a
wellbeing budget; the OECD publishes guidelines on measurement. Whether these
measures should influence policy, and how, is a direct descendant of the argument
on this page.

**Cash transfers and basic income.** Trials of unconditional cash transfers in
Kenya, the United States and Finland measure subjective wellbeing as a primary
outcome. The results — generally positive effects on wellbeing, often larger for
the poorest recipients, sometimes fading over time — are informative for the
paradox because they are experimental. Adaptation shows up in some of these
studies as an effect that decays, which is exactly Easterlin's mechanism observed
directly.

**The satiation question in policy.** Whether there is a level above which extra
income stops improving lives has direct implications for progressive taxation, for
how development aid is targeted, and for the design of poverty thresholds. The
2023 finding that the answer differs by subgroup complicates all of these in a
useful way: it suggests that at high incomes, spending on mental health may
dominate spending that raises incomes further.

**Social media and reference groups.** If wellbeing depends on comparison, then
technologies that widen and intensify the comparison set should reduce it. This
prediction is testable and is being tested; the evidence on social media and
adolescent wellbeing is contested in its own right, but the theoretical connection
runs straight back to Easterlin's relative income mechanism.

**Growth policy after the paradox.** The practical question the paradox poses is
not "should countries stop growing" but "what kind of growth". If insecurity,
long hours and inequality offset material gains, then the composition of growth
matters as much as its rate. That framing is now common in policy discussion, and
it is a direct inheritance from a 1974 book chapter.

## Deep Dive

### The specification at issue

Let $W_{it}$ be mean reported wellbeing in country $i$ at time $t$, and $Y_{it}$
income per head. The three stylised facts correspond to three different
regressions, and conflating them is the source of most confusion.

**Within-country cross-section.** For individuals $j$ in country $i$ at time $t$:

$$W_{ijt} = \alpha_{it} + \beta_{\text{micro}} \ln y_{ijt} + \varepsilon_{ijt}$$

Estimates of $\beta_{\text{micro}}$ are consistently positive and reasonably
stable across countries.

**Between-country cross-section.** For a single period $t$:

$$W_{it} = \alpha + \beta_{\text{macro}} \ln Y_{it} + u_{it}$$

Stevenson and Wolfers's central empirical claim is that
$\beta_{\text{macro}} \approx \beta_{\text{micro}}$, which is what you would
expect if income operates absolutely, and not what a purely relative-income model
predicts. Under a pure relative model, $\beta_{\text{macro}} = 0$ while
$\beta_{\text{micro}} > 0$.

**Within-country time series.** With country fixed effects:

$$W_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_{\text{time}}\ln Y_{it} + \gamma_t + u_{it}$$

Easterlin's claim is that $\beta_{\text{time}} \approx 0$ over long horizons. This
is where the disagreement lives, and it is the hardest of the three to estimate,
because within-country variation in $\ln Y$ over the available sample period is
small relative to measurement noise.

The relative income model in its simplest form makes the distinction explicit.
Suppose

$$W_{ijt} = \theta \ln y_{ijt} - \phi \ln \bar{y}_{it} + \varepsilon_{ijt}$$

where $\bar y_{it}$ is the reference income. Then
$\beta_{\text{micro}} = \theta$ but $\beta_{\text{time}} = \theta - \phi$. The
Easterlin paradox is the hypothesis that $\phi \approx \theta$: reference income
tracks own income one-for-one in the long run, cancelling the gain. Stevenson and
Wolfers's position is that $\phi$ is well below $\theta$.

Stated this way, the paradox is a single-parameter dispute, and its empirical
content is entirely about how completely the reference point adapts.

### Why the log matters so much

Every serious specification uses $\ln Y$, not $Y$. This is not a technicality; it
is doing a great deal of the rhetorical work on both sides.

Under a log specification, wellbeing gains are proportional to *growth rates*, not
to absolute increments. A country growing at 2% per year gains
$\beta \cdot \ln(1.02) \approx 0.0198\beta$ per year. Over forty years that is
$\beta \cdot \ln(2.2) \approx 0.79\beta$. With a within-country micro slope of
roughly 0.3 ladder points per log point — a common estimate — that predicts a rise
of about 0.24 points on a 0–10 scale over forty years.

That is small. It is within the noise of most national time series, and it is
comparable to the drift you would get from changes in survey mode or question
wording. **The relationship Stevenson and Wolfers defend predicts an effect that
Easterlin's data would struggle to detect.** Both camps can therefore be
consistent with the same numbers, and this is the most important thing to
understand about why the dispute has not closed. It is a power problem as much as
a substantive one.

### Ceiling effects and scale use

Let $W^{*}$ be latent wellbeing and $W$ the reported value on a bounded scale
$[0, C]$ with thresholds. If the mapping is
$W = \min(C, \max(0, g(W^{*})))$ for some increasing $g$, then as the population
distribution of $W^{*}$ shifts right, the observed mean rises less than
proportionally and eventually not at all. This is enough to produce a flat
time series from a genuinely rising latent variable.

The 2023 adversarial collaboration is an instance. Kahneman and Deaton's emotional
wellbeing measure was constructed from dichotomous items — did you experience
happiness yesterday, yes or no — which imposes a hard ceiling at "yes". People
already answering yes cannot answer more yes. When Killingsworth's continuous
experience-sampling measure was used, the ceiling disappeared and so did the
plateau, except in the subgroup for which the flattening was substantive rather
than mechanical.

Distinguishing a real plateau from a measurement ceiling requires either an
unbounded measure or an explicit model of scale use. The literature that treats
wellbeing responses as ordinal and estimates the thresholds — including work by
Bond and Lang, and by Kaiser and Oswald — has shown that for many datasets, the
sign of the estimated group difference can be reversed by an admissible choice of
underlying scale. That is a serious result, and it applies to some comparisons in
this literature and not others.

### The subgroup structure found in 2023

The pre-registered joint analysis fitted the relationship between log household
income and experienced wellbeing separately across the wellbeing distribution —
that is, by quantile of happiness rather than only at the mean. The headline
findings:

| Happiness quantile | Slope of wellbeing on log income above ~\$100k |
| --- | --- |
| Bottom ~15–20% | approximately flat |
| Middle | positive, similar to below \$100k |
| Top ~30% | positive and **steeper** than below \$100k |

Averaging a flat segment with a steepening one produces the apparent plateau in
the mean, which is how two competent analyses of overlapping questions reached
opposite conclusions.

The substantive interpretation both authors endorsed: for people whose unhappiness
has a cause money cannot address, more money does not help. For everyone else it
continues to. This is a statement about heterogeneity, not about satiation, and it
does not by itself bear on whether a whole country gets happier as it grows —
though it does suggest that national averages will move sluggishly whenever a
sizeable unhappy minority is included in them.

### What would settle the original question

The honest answer is that the required evidence is close to unobtainable.
Establishing whether long-run national income growth raises wellbeing needs: a
consistent measurement instrument held fixed for many decades; enough countries
with enough variation in growth rates; a credible source of exogenous variation in
national income; and a way to separate the effect of growth from the effects of
everything correlated with it. No study has all four, and the first alone rules
out most of the existing series.

What can be done, and is being done, is to accumulate long consistent series
going forward — the Gallup World Poll has now run since 2005, national statistical
offices since around 2011 — and to test the mechanism directly through
experiments on individuals. That is why the field's centre of gravity has shifted
from national time series to cash-transfer trials and experience sampling. It is a
retreat from the original question toward a tractable one, and it should be
understood as such.

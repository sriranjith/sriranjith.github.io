## Discover

The Athenians kept a ship in the harbour. It was the ship Theseus had sailed
home in after killing the Minotaur, and for centuries they sailed it out once a
year to Delos and back to honour the god.

Wooden ships rot. So every year the carpenters went aboard, found the planks
that had gone soft, prised them out and hammered in fresh ones. One plank. Then
another. Then another.

After a few hundred years of this, not a single piece of the original ship was
left. Every plank, every peg, every rope had been swapped for a newer one.

The Athenians still called it the ship of Theseus. Was it?

If you want to say yes — that a thing can survive having all its parts changed,
one at a time — then here is the question that has been ruining dinner parties
since 1655.

Suppose that all along, a very tidy carpenter has been carrying the old rotten
planks back to a warehouse and stacking them up. And suppose that once the last
original plank comes out, he takes every one of them, scrapes off the barnacles,
and nails them back together in exactly their original arrangement.

Now there are two ships.

One has been sailing to Delos every year without interruption. The other is made
of every single piece of wood that Theseus actually touched.

Point at the ship of Theseus.

## Understand

The trouble is that you have two perfectly good rules for deciding when a thing
is still the same thing, you have used both your whole life, and this case pulls
them apart.

**Rule one: same stuff.** A thing is the same thing if it is made of the same
material. Melt your wedding ring and recast it as a different ring and the gold
is still the same gold. By this rule the ship of Theseus is the reassembled one
in the warehouse, because that is where all of Theseus's wood ended up.

**Rule two: same continuous history.** A thing is the same thing if it carried on
existing without a break — same shape, same job, same place in the world, no gaps.
By this rule the ship of Theseus is the one in the harbour, because it never
stopped being a ship for a single day, and nobody watching it could have told you
which afternoon it stopped being Theseus's.

Both rules are sensible. You would defend either one on an ordinary day. And
almost every ordinary case satisfies both at once, which is why you have never
noticed that you are carrying two rules around.

### Why you cannot just pick the one you like

Here is the part that makes it a genuine problem rather than a matter of taste.

Whatever "the ship of Theseus" refers to, there can only be **one** of it. If the
harbour ship is Theseus's ship, and the warehouse ship is also Theseus's ship,
then the harbour ship and the warehouse ship are the same ship. But they are two
ships. They are in different places. You could burn one and sail the other. They
are obviously not the same ship.

Thomas Hobbes, who added the second ship in 1655, put it exactly this way: you
would end up with two bodies that are numerically one, which he called absurd.

So at least one of your two rules must be wrong, or must not mean what you
thought it meant. And there is no third rule sitting in reserve.

### The move most people make, and why it does not quite work

The usual first response is: *it's just words. There's no fact of the matter.
Call them whatever you like.*

There is something right in that. But notice what happens as soon as anything is
at stake.

Suppose the Athenians insured the ship. Which one does the policy cover? Suppose
a museum paid for the ship Theseus sailed. Which one do they get? Suppose the
ship is you.

That last one is not a joke. Your body replaces its cells continuously. The
material you are made of is not the material you were made of at seven years
old, and yet you have never had the slightest doubt that you are the person who
was seven. You are running rule two, on yourself, right now. And you have views
about whether a copy of you with all your memories, built somewhere else out of
different atoms, would be you — strong views, probably, and views you would act
on.

That is why philosophers keep coming back to a boat. The boat is a way of
getting a clear look at a question about people, before the answer starts to
matter too much to think straight.

### What the puzzle is actually for

The Ship of Theseus proves nothing. It is not an argument with a conclusion. It
is a device for making a disagreement visible.

What it shows is that "same thing" is not one idea. It is several ideas bundled
together — same matter, same form, same function, same continuous path, same
name — that normally travel together and can, with a little carpentry, be forced
apart. Once they come apart, you have to say which of them you meant, and you
will find that different answers suit different purposes.

## Examples

**The everyday cases where the bundle stays bundled.**

| Case | Same matter? | Same continuous history? | Do people hesitate? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Your car after a new tyre | Almost | Yes | No |
| Your car after a new engine | No | Yes | Barely |
| A car rebuilt around a salvaged chassis and its old registration plate | Partly | Contested | Yes, and courts have ruled on it |
| A river | Never | Yes | No — nobody thinks the Thames needs its 1850 water |
| An orchestra after every player retires | No | Yes | No |
| A file copied to a new disk and the original deleted | No | Contested | Yes, as soon as money is involved |

The pattern is that we lean on continuity of form and function almost all the
time, and only reach for continuity of matter when someone hands us the old
parts.

**Trigger's broom.** In a 1996 episode of the British sitcom *Only Fools and
Horses*, a road sweeper proudly announces he has used the same broom for twenty
years — and that it has had seventeen new heads and fourteen new handles. The
joke works because everyone gets it instantly, which is a fair measure of how
natural rule two is.

**The Ise Grand Shrine.** In Japan, the shrine at Ise has been demolished and
rebuilt on an adjacent site roughly every twenty years for over a millennium,
using the same design and traditional techniques. It is the most-visited version
of this puzzle in the world, and the tradition treats the answer as settled:
continuity of practice is what preserves the shrine, and new timber every two
decades is part of the continuity rather than a break in it.

**Your own atoms.** The turnover story is often overstated — neurons in the
cerebral cortex and the lens of the eye largely persist for life — but most of
your body is replaced over years, and none of it is the material you were born
with. You are already the harbour ship.

**The strangler fig.** Software teams rebuild a running system by replacing one
component at a time while it stays live, until nothing of the original remains.
The technique is named after the fig that grows around a host tree; engineers
also call it a Ship-of-Theseus migration, which is one of the rare cases of a
metaphysical puzzle contributing a project plan.

## Explore

The positions people take divide on a prior question: does an object persist by
being wholly present at each moment, or by having a different part at each
moment?

### Endurantism: the object is wholly there each time you look

This is the common-sense view. The ship in the harbour today is not a slice of a
larger four-dimensional object; it is the whole ship, present now, and it was
also wholly present in 300 BCE. Persistence is a matter of one and the same thing
existing at two times.

Endurantists then have to say something about the two ships, and there are
several things to say.

**Matter wins.** Roderick Chisholm defended a strict view on which an object
cannot survive the loss of any part at all, so nothing survives the first plank.
The Athenian ship of any later year is a different object, loosely and
conveniently called by the same name. This is consistent but concedes that almost
all our ordinary identity talk is loose.

**Continuity wins.** The dominant intuition: the harbour ship is Theseus's ship,
and the reassembled ship is a very interesting replica made of authentic
material. The awkwardness is explaining why the warehouse ship is not the
original when it is made of every original piece, and why the answer would change
if the planks had merely been stored rather than reassembled.

**Constitution rather than identity.** Lynne Rudder Baker and others distinguish
the ship from the wood that constitutes it, as a statue is distinguished from the
clay. Two objects can occupy the same place if they have different persistence
conditions: the clay survives being squashed, the statue does not. Applied here,
the harbour ship is the ship, and the warehouse assembly is the original wood
doing a convincing impression. Critics object that this puts two objects in one
place and asks us to believe the difference is not detectable.

**Relative identity.** Peter Geach argued that "same" is always implicitly "same
*F*" — same ship, same collection of planks — and that the two can diverge without
contradiction. This dissolves the puzzle at the cost of abandoning the standard
logic of identity, and few have followed him.

### Perdurantism: the object is a worm in spacetime

Theodore Sider (*Four-Dimensionalism*, 2001) and, earlier, David Lewis take the
other route. Objects are extended in time as well as space, and have *temporal
parts* — the ship-on-Tuesday, the ship-in-300-BCE — as they have spatial parts.
An object persists by having a part at each time it exists, not by being wholly
present at each.

This gives the puzzle a clean answer, and the answer is that the question was
badly posed.

There are **two** ships in the story, and there always were. One four-dimensional
object runs from Theseus's launch to the harbour ship of today. Another runs from
Theseus's launch to the warehouse ship. The two share their early temporal parts
completely — before the first repair, they overlap exactly, the way two roads can
share a stretch of tarmac before diverging. Asking "which one is the ship of
Theseus?" is asking which of two overlapping objects a name picks out, and the
honest answer is that ordinary usage never had to decide.

Sider adds an argument that pushes hard in this direction: identity cannot be
vague, but composition and persistence plainly are, and the only way to have
determinate identity with indeterminate boundaries is to let objects be
four-dimensional and admit far more of them than common sense does.

The cost is a headcount problem. If two ships were in the harbour in 300 BCE,
why did nobody see two? Perdurantists reply that we count by temporal parts, not
by worms — at that moment there was one ship-stage, and one is the number you
would report.

### Persons: where the puzzle stops being academic

**Psychological continuity.** John Locke, in the chapter on identity added to the
*Essay* in 1694, separated the person from the man and from the substance. A
person is constituted by continuity of consciousness — memory, in his
formulation. If you can remember doing it, you are the one who did it. His
prince-and-cobbler case, in which a prince's consciousness moves into a cobbler's
body, makes the point that we follow the mind.

Thomas Reid's *brave officer* objection (1785) is the standard reply: an old
general remembers being a young officer, and the young officer remembered being a
flogged schoolboy, but the general remembers nothing of the boy. Direct memory is
not transitive, so it cannot ground identity. Modern versions patch this with
overlapping chains of psychological connection.

**Biological continuity.** Eric Olson and other animalists reverse the priority:
you are a human organism, you began before you had any psychology and may
continue after it goes, and identity tracks the life of the animal. A brain
transplant, on this view, does not move you — it moves an organ, and you stay
with the body. Animalism handles the persistent vegetative state and the foetus
cleanly, and handles the prince and the cobbler badly.

**Neither.** Derek Parfit (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984) argues that the whole
dispute is a mistake. Consider fission: the two hemispheres of your brain are
separated and transplanted into two bodies, each with your memories and
character. You cannot be identical to both, since they are not identical to each
other. You cannot be identical to just one, since there is nothing to choose
between them. And it is hard to say you died, since you would have survived if
only one hemisphere had been transplanted.

Parfit's conclusion is that identity is not what matters. What matters is
psychological continuity and connectedness, which can hold one-to-many, and which
comes in degrees. Once you know all the physical and psychological facts about
the two ships, or the two people, there is no further fact you are missing. The
question "but which one is *really* me?" is empty.

Whether that is liberating or evasive is exactly where the debate stands. It is
also, straightforwardly, Hobbes's two-ships problem transplanted into a skull.

## Deep Dive

Write $A$ for the ship at launch, $B$ for the fully repaired ship in the harbour
at $t_1$, and $C$ for the ship reassembled from the original planks at $t_1$.

The two intuitions are identity claims:

$$A = B \quad \text{(continuity of form and function)} \qquad A = C \quad \text{(continuity of matter)}$$

Identity is an equivalence relation, so symmetry and transitivity give $B = C$.
But **Leibniz's Law** — if $x = y$ then $x$ and $y$ share every property — refutes
that immediately, because $B$ is in the harbour and $C$ is in the warehouse:

$$\exists P \,\big( P(B) \wedge \neg P(C) \big) \;\Rightarrow\; B \neq C$$

So $\{A = B,\; A = C\}$ is inconsistent. At most one intuition survives, and the
puzzle is the absence of any principle that selects between them.

**The perdurantist reconstruction.** Let an object be a maximal aggregate of
temporal parts. Define two worms:

$$W_1 = \{\, s_t : t \in [t_0, t_1] \,\}\ \text{ending in the harbour ship}, \qquad W_2 = \{\, s'_t : t \in [t_0, t_1] \,\}\ \text{ending in the warehouse ship}$$

with $s_t = s'_t$ for all $t$ before the first plank is replaced. $W_1$ and $W_2$
are distinct objects that share an initial segment. There is no contradiction:
"$A$" was never a name for a worm, only for a stage in the shared segment, and a
stage belongs to both worms. Leibniz's Law is satisfied throughout, since $W_1$
and $W_2$ differ in their later parts and were therefore never identical.

The residual question is semantic, not metaphysical: which worm does the Athenian
name track? Nothing in Athenian practice settled it, so nothing settles it now.

**Stage theory** (Katherine Hawley, and Sider's own preferred variant) goes
further. Ordinary singular terms denote instantaneous stages, and "the ship
existed yesterday" is analysed as: the present stage bears a *temporal
counterpart* relation $R$ to a stage located yesterday. Because $R$ is a
similarity-and-causation relation rather than identity, it need not be
transitive, and it may be one-to-many. Fission is then unproblematic — a stage can
have two future counterparts — and this is the formal version of Parfit's claim
that what matters can branch while identity cannot.

**Sider's argument from vagueness**, compressed:

1. If composition is restricted, there are borderline cases of composition.
2. A borderline case of composition would be a case in which it is indeterminate
   how many objects exist.
3. Numerical sentences contain no vague expressions, so their truth value cannot
   be indeterminate.
4. Therefore composition is unrestricted — any plurality composes something.

Unrestricted composition plus temporal parts yields an ontology containing $W_1$,
$W_2$, and every other aggregate of stages, which is why the four-dimensionalist
can afford to say "both, and there were always two". The argument's second
premise is where most of the resistance lands, and it is worth noticing that this
is the sorites argument doing the work.

**Persons.** Let $R$ be the ancestral of direct psychological connectedness —
overlapping chains of memory, intention and character. Locke's account
effectively proposes $x = y \iff xRy$, which fails Reid's objection unless the
ancestral is taken. Even taken, $R$ can hold one-to-many, so the biconditional
cannot be right, since identity cannot. Standard repairs add a non-branching
clause:

$$x = y \iff xRy \ \wedge\ \neg\exists z\,(z \ne y \wedge xRz)$$

This restores a genuine equivalence relation but makes your survival depend on
whether an unconnected duplicate exists somewhere else — the *only x and y*
problem. Parfit's response is to deny that identity is the thing worth caring
about and to let $R$ carry the practical weight on its own.

## History

**c. 500 BCE.** Heraclitus is reported as saying that different waters flow upon
those who step into the same rivers. The fragments (DK B12, B49a) are terse and
their interpretation is disputed — the "you cannot step into the same river twice"
form is Plato's paraphrase in the *Cratylus*, not Heraclitus's words — but the
question of how something persists while its matter flows through it is
recognisably the same.

**c. 380 BCE.** Plato's *Symposium* (207d–208b) has Diotima observe that a person
is called the same from childhood to old age although hair, flesh, bones and even
memories are constantly departing and being replaced. The observation is offered
as a fact about mortal nature rather than as a puzzle.

**c. 100 CE.** Plutarch, in chapter 23 of the *Life of Theseus*, records that the
Athenians preserved the thirty-oared ship down to the time of Demetrius of
Phalerum — which dates the practice to at least the late fourth century BCE — and
that as timbers decayed they were replaced with new ones. He adds the crucial
line: the ship had become a standing example among philosophers for the disputed
question of things that grow, one party holding it remained the same, the other
that it did not. Plutarch reports the dispute; he did not invent it, does not
name the parties, and does not take a side.

**1655.** Thomas Hobbes, in *De Corpore* Part II chapter 11, adds the move that
turns a disagreement into a paradox: keep the old planks, and build a second ship
from them. He observes that on one criterion the repaired ship is the original
and on the other the reassembled one is, so that there would be two bodies
numerically the same — which he rejects as absurd. Hobbes's own resolution is
deflationary: identity claims are relative to the name under which a thing is
considered, so the same matter can be one body and a different ship.

**1694.** John Locke adds "Of Identity and Diversity" to the second edition of the
*Essay Concerning Human Understanding*. His key move is that identity conditions
depend on the kind of thing in question, and that persons — unlike masses of
matter and unlike living organisms — persist by continuity of consciousness.

**1785.** Thomas Reid publishes the brave officer objection, showing that memory
is not transitive and so cannot by itself constitute identity.

**1971–1984.** Derek Parfit's paper "Personal Identity" and then *Reasons and
Persons* introduce fission cases and the thesis that identity is not what matters
in survival.

**1976.** David Lewis's "Survival and Identity" gives the perdurantist treatment
of fission: two persons sharing earlier stages, counted as one by the practice of
counting stages.

**2001.** Theodore Sider's *Four-Dimensionalism* assembles the case for temporal
parts, including the argument from vagueness, and makes the two-overlapping-worms
answer the standard alternative to the endurantist picture.

The name "the Ship of Theseus" for the whole family of problems is modern.
Plutarch has the ship; Hobbes has the paradox; the label came later.

## Why It Matters

**It shows that "the same thing" was never one question.** Matter, form, function,
continuous path and name are five different criteria that coincide in almost
every real case. Discovering that they can be pulled apart is the beginning of
serious metaphysics, and every subsequent theory of persistence is an attempt to
say which criterion is doing the real work.

**Law has to answer it, and does, arbitrarily.** Vehicle identity is settled by
statute and registration rather than by parts: jurisdictions specify how much of
a car can be replaced before the rebuilt vehicle needs a new identity, and
kit-car and salvage-title rules exist precisely because the metaphysics does not
determine the answer. Corporate law does the same for companies — a firm that has
changed every employee, every product, its name and its headquarters is still the
same legal person, liable for what it did decades ago, because continuity of the
charter is stipulated to be what counts.

**Heritage conservation is a live argument about it.** After the 2019 fire, the
rebuilding of Notre-Dame de Paris turned on whether authenticity lives in the
original material or in the form and the craft. The Nara Document on Authenticity
(1994) was written to accommodate traditions like Ise, where periodic rebuilding
is the preservation, against a European convention that had treated original
fabric as the thing to save. Two continents took opposite sides of Plutarch's
dispute and had to write a treaty about it.

**It sets the terms for questions about people that are becoming practical.**
Advance directives ask whether a person with advanced dementia is bound by the
wishes of the person who wrote the document. Criminal justice asks whether it is
just to punish someone for what they did at nineteen. Both are the Ship of
Theseus with consequences, and both are usually settled by legal continuity of
the body — which is to say, by animalism, adopted without argument.

## Modern Relevance

**Teleportation and uploading.** A device that scans you, destroys the original
and reassembles an identical body elsewhere is Hobbes's second ship, built at
speed. Whether you should step into it is the Ship of Theseus with your life
attached, and the split in public intuition matches the split in the literature
almost exactly.

**Software migration.** The strangler-fig pattern replaces a live system one
component at a time. Teams routinely find that the interesting question is not
technical but nominal — whether the result is version 4 of the old system or a
new system that inherited a name — and the answer determines budgets, ownership
and on-call rotas.

**Digital preservation.** An emulated program running on hardware that did not
exist when it was written, from a file that has been migrated across a dozen
storage formats, raises the question in an unusually crisp form: nothing physical
persists, only the pattern. Archivists have largely settled on the harbour ship,
and preserve behaviour rather than bits.

**Machine learning models.** A model that has been fine-tuned, quantised, pruned
and distilled until none of the original weights survive is still shipped under
the original name and the original licence. Model provenance and licensing law
are currently working out what makes two sets of weights the same model, without
much awareness that Hobbes got there first.

**Bands, teams and institutions.** A touring group with no original members, a
football club that has changed ground, colours and ownership, a university that
shares only a name with its medieval founder: the disputes are fierce, they are
conducted entirely in terms of continuity of matter versus continuity of form,
and they are never settled.

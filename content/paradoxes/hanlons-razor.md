## Discover

A form letter arrives from a bank. It terminates your account with two weeks'
notice. The letter gives no reason. You have done nothing wrong.

The obvious interpretation: the bank is being malicious, or discriminatory, or
has decided to punish you for something you didn't do.

The more likely interpretation: a software filter triggered incorrectly; a new
policy was implemented without adequate exceptions; a compliance team interpreted
a regulation too broadly; someone decided it was easier to close certain account
types than to maintain them.

Nobody at the bank specifically wanted to close your account. The harm is real.
The cause is not malice but a combination of bad policy, inadequate systems,
and the fact that your specific situation was not considered.

Hanlon's Razor: never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by
stupidity (or incompetence, or institutional failure, or indifference).

## Understand

Hanlon's Razor is a heuristic about attribution — about how to explain other
people's harmful actions. It is closely related to Occam's Razor: between two
explanations that fit the evidence, prefer the one that requires fewer
assumptions. Malice is an additional assumption. The malicious explanation
requires:

1. Someone decided to do harm.
2. They carried out that decision.
3. The harm was the intended outcome.

The incompetence explanation requires only:

1. Someone made a mistake (or was negligent, or didn't think it through).

Most harm in the world has the second structure, not the first. This is
not because humans are good, but because:

- **Coordinated malice is expensive.** Deliberate harm against a specific target
  requires identifying the target, deciding to act, and following through.
- **Incompetence is free.** Errors happen automatically. Systems fail by default.
- **Institutions don't have intentions.** A company or government does not
  collectively intend harm even when it collectively causes it.

### The important caveat

The razor is a *default prior*, not a guarantee. Sometimes malice is the correct
explanation. History contains plenty of deliberate harm dressed up as mistake
or policy. The razor says: do not assume malice when incompetence explains it,
but do not rule malice out when the evidence points there.

The razor also does not exonerate. If something causes harm through negligence
or incompetence, those are still causes worth addressing. The point is to
diagnose correctly, not to excuse.

### Where it guides action

The practical difference is large. If the bank closed your account out of
malice, the response is complaint, legal action, and exposure. If it closed
it out of policy error, the response is a specific complaint about the policy,
or finding a different bank. The right response depends on the correct diagnosis.

More generally: attributing harm to malice when incompetence is the cause leads
to conflict, escalation, and adversarial dynamics that are hard to resolve.
Attributing harm to incompetence when malice is the cause leads to inadequate
response and continued harm. Getting the attribution right matters.

## Examples

| Event | Malice attribution | Incompetence attribution | Usually correct |
| --- | --- | --- |--- |
| Government policy harms a community | Deliberate targeting | Bad policy design without adequate analysis | Usually incompetence |
| Software bug loses data | Deliberate sabotage | Engineering failure | Almost always incompetence |
| Doctor gives wrong treatment | Attempted harm | Medical error | Almost always error |
| Colleague takes credit for work | Deliberate theft | Didn't realise; different norms | Depends on evidence |

## Explore

**Hanlon's Razor and conspiracy theories.** Conspiracy theories systematically
violate Hanlon's Razor by attributing to coordinated malice what is usually
explained by coordination failure, incompetence, or independent self-interested
action. The COVID-19 pandemic generated many conspiracy theories that required
implausible levels of coordinated deliberate harm across independent
institutions in many countries. Hanlon's Razor points toward more mundane
explanations.

**When to apply the opposite.** There are domains where assuming good faith is
dangerous. In adversarial security contexts — penetration testing, intelligence
analysis, fraud detection — assuming malice is the professional default.
A penetration tester who assumes the network was designed well and the logs
are accurate is a bad penetration tester. Context determines which prior
is appropriate.

**Institutional malice.** Organisations can have structural effects that harm
specific groups without any individual intending harm. Redlining, discriminatory
algorithms, and unfair hiring processes can be the result of decisions that no
individual intended as harmful. The razor is helpful here: attributing these
to conspiracy obscures the structural nature of the problem. But structural harm
is not the same as no harm — it still requires a structural response.

**The charitable interpretation as principle.** In philosophy of language, the
principle of charity says: interpret an argument in its strongest form before
criticising it. Hanlon's Razor is the behavioural equivalent: interpret actions
in their most benign form before attributing harmful intent.

## History

**1941.** Robert Heinlein's short story "Logic of Empire" contains the line:
"You have attributed conditions to villainy that simply result from stupidity."

**1980.** Robert Hanlon of Scranton submits a version to Arthur Bloch's
*Murphy's Law Book Two*. It is included and cited as "Hanlon's Law" with an
attribution to Robert Hanlon.

**Late 20th century.** The saying circulates widely in technology communities,
particularly in discussions of software design, corporate behaviour, and
government policy. Its appeal is highest in contexts where the alternative
(assuming everything bad is deliberately caused) is clearly unproductive.

## Why It Matters

**Conflict reduction.** Interpreting harm as malice when it is incompetence
creates adversarial dynamics that are harder to resolve than the original
problem. The natural response to perceived malice is retaliation; the natural
response to incompetence is correction. Choosing the latter when it's accurate
reduces unnecessary conflict.

**Better diagnosis.** Correct attribution leads to correct response. If a system
harms people through bad design, the fix is to redesign the system. If it harms
through malicious intent, the fix is to expose the intent and apply consequences.
Applying the malice fix to a design problem, or the design fix to a malice
problem, doesn't work.

**Epistemic humility.** The razor is a reminder that other people's internal
states — their intentions and motivations — are not directly observable. The
malicious interpretation is a strong claim about inner states. The incompetence
interpretation is a claim about external behaviour. The weaker claim requires
less evidence.

## Modern Relevance

**Online discourse.** Social media environments tend to promote the malicious
interpretation: misunderstandings are read as attacks, mistakes are read as
deliberate provocations, and the adversarial interpretation spreads faster than
the charitable one. Hanlon's Razor is a counterweight — not always right,
but a useful corrective to the default escalation dynamic.

**AI alignment.** When an AI system behaves badly — generates harmful content,
gives wrong answers, pursues misspecified goals — the temptation is to treat
it as intentional. It is not. AI systems cause harm through misalignment,
not malice. The correct analogy is incompetence, not villainy. The practical
responses (retraining, realignment, specification improvement) follow from
the correct diagnosis.

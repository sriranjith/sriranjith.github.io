## Discover

London, 1955. The British Colonial Office, which once administered a global
empire, is being wound down as the colonies gain independence. The number of
colonies it administers has dropped dramatically. Logically, its staff should
have shrunk.

Between 1935 and 1954, the Colonial Office's staff grew from 372 to 1,661.
The number of officials grew by 78% while the actual colonies were vanishing.

C. Northcote Parkinson, a naval historian with a gift for observation, noticed
that the British Admiralty had done something similar after World War I: the
number of ships in the fleet fell by two-thirds between 1914 and 1928, but
Admiralty staff grew by 78%. He proposed that these were not coincidences but
instances of the same principle.

His opening sentence, in The Economist in November 1955, became one of the most
quoted observations in management writing:

> **Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion.**

## Understand

The mechanism is not laziness. Parkinson identified two forces that drive
bureaucratic expansion, and they apply equally to individual task management.

**An official wants to multiply subordinates, not rivals.** A bureaucrat who
feels overworked has a simple option: hire two assistants. This gives them status,
removes the risk that a single deputy might challenge them, and is much easier
than simplifying the work. Two assistants will in turn want assistants of their
own. The hierarchy deepens without the underlying workload changing.

**Officials make work for each other.** In a larger organisation, a decision that
one person once made alone now requires memos, consultations, sign-offs, and
reviews — each of which generates more memos. The coordination cost of the
bureaucracy becomes the work.

For an individual — not a bureaucracy — the expansion works differently. Give
yourself an afternoon to write an email and you will draft it, reconsider it,
revise it, worry about its tone, revise it again, look at it the next morning,
and revise it once more. Give yourself five minutes and you will write it.

The underlying dynamics:

- **Anxiety fills available time.** More time means more opportunity to doubt and
  second-guess.
- **Perfectionism scales to deadlines.** When time is short, good enough is good
  enough. When time is ample, perfect beckons.
- **Tasks are rarely pre-committed in detail.** Without a constraint, scope drifts.

### The corollary for individuals

The counterintuitive practical implication: **shorter deadlines often produce
better output than longer ones**, up to a point. The pressure of a genuine
constraint focuses attention and suppresses the anxiety-driven iteration that
fills available time without improving the result.

This is the basis of time-boxing: commit to a fixed time, deliver at the end,
then evaluate. The fixed time does not guarantee perfect work, but it prevents
the recursive perfectionism loop that Parkinson observed.

## Examples

| Context | More time given | What fills it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Writing an email | A week | Multiple drafts, reconsideration of every phrasing |
| Software project | Twelve months | Feature scope expands to consume the budget |
| Government report | Open-ended | Committee formation, sub-reports, endless review cycles |
| Tax return | Until the deadline | Completed the night before regardless of when it starts |
| Essay | A semester | Substantive work begins one week before submission |

## Explore

**Parkinson's Law of Triviality** is the second law he identified (though he did
not give it that name — it was coined later). A committee will spend
disproportionate time on trivial matters. His example: approving a £10 million
nuclear reactor takes three minutes because no one on the committee understands
nuclear reactors. Approving a £350 bicycle shed takes forty-five minutes because
everyone has an opinion about bicycle sheds.

The formal term is **bikeshedding** — spending disproportionate attention on the
small, comprehensible element of a project while the large, consequential
element passes unquestioned.

**The productivity research.** Parkinson stated his law as satire with comic
statistics. Later research found evidence for it. Studies of student assignment
completion found that students with near-identical assignments but different
deadlines used the available time — both groups did similar amounts of work, but
the ones with longer deadlines spread it more thinly and often reported working
harder (because anxiety is effortful).

**Where it breaks down.** Parkinson's Law is not a guarantee. Some tasks
genuinely require a long time because they require thinking that cannot be
forced. Research, creative work, and learning all resist time-boxing. The law is
most reliable for tasks where the scope can be fixed in advance and the work is
execution rather than discovery.

## History

**1955.** Parkinson's essay appears in *The Economist* on 19 November. It is
written as satire — the tone is comic, the statistics are mock-serious — but it
is taken seriously immediately.

**1957.** The essay is expanded into a book, *Parkinson's Law and Other Studies
in Administration*, which becomes an international bestseller. The law is now in
management discourse permanently.

**1960s onwards.** The law enters business school curricula and management
consulting. It is frequently cited in discussions of bureaucratic inefficiency
and project management.

**2007.** Tim Ferriss's *The 4-Hour Workweek* popularises the law for a new
generation, citing it as the justification for aggressive time-boxing and
deadline-setting as productivity tools.

## Why It Matters

**Project management.** Parkinson's Law explains why projects routinely use all
their allocated time — and why adding more time rarely fixes a delayed project.
The additional time is absorbed. The solution is not more time but better-defined
scope and tighter checkpoints.

**Institutional design.** The bureaucratic version explains why organisations
grow even when the work they do does not. Managers who understand this can build
in structural resistance: sunset clauses on teams, zero-based staffing reviews,
explicit headcount constraints.

**Personal productivity.** The individual version is the reason deadline-driven
people often get more done than open-schedule ones. It also explains why
"I'll do it when I have more time" is almost always a statement that the thing
will not get done.

## Modern Relevance

**Software engineering.** Feature scope in software projects reliably expands to
consume the available schedule. Agile methodologies are partly a systematic
response to Parkinson's Law: fix the time (the sprint), scope what fits, ship,
then repeat. The alternative — fix the features and let the time expand —
consistently produces late projects.

**Meetings.** A meeting scheduled for an hour takes an hour. Scheduled for
thirty minutes, it often takes thirty minutes and covers the same ground.
Parkinson's Law is the theoretical justification for aggressive meeting-shortening.

**AI development.** Large AI projects given open-ended timelines tend to expand
their ambitions to match. Benchmarks that were the goal become insufficient once
reached; new benchmarks are invented. The project does not end — it redefines
success to remain just out of reach.

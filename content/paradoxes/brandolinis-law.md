## Discover

A widely shared social media post claims: "Scientists admit COVID vaccines cause heart failure in 1 in 50 people." The claim is false. It misrepresents a study of rare myocarditis cases (estimated incidence 1–4 per 100,000 doses) and ignores that COVID itself causes myocarditis at much higher rates.

To correct this claim fully, you would need to:
- Find and read the original study
- Explain what the study actually measured
- Explain the difference between that and heart failure
- Provide comparative data on COVID-induced myocarditis
- Address the selection of only the "vaccine harm" side of the equation
- Counter the implicit argument that the study shows vaccines are net harmful
- Handle follow-up objections (cherry-picking, claim source bias, etc.)

The false claim took thirty seconds to compose. The full, accurate refutation takes hours of expert time and thousands of words. And it will reach far fewer people than the original claim.

This is Brandolini's Law. The asymmetry is structural, not accidental.

## Understand

Brandolini's Law states that the energy required to refute misinformation is an order of magnitude greater than the energy required to produce it.

The asymmetry arises from several structural features of how claims and refutations work:

**Claim production is cheap.** A false claim requires only internal consistency — it does not have to match external reality. It can be short, vivid, emotionally resonant, and require no evidence. It takes seconds to generate and seconds for an audience to process.

**Refutation is expensive.** A refutation must match external reality, cite evidence for that matching, identify exactly what is wrong with the original claim, and explain the complexity that the original claim glossed over. It is inherently longer, requires more effort to process, and demands that the audience already have the background to evaluate it.

**Complexity asymmetry.** True accounts of complex phenomena are generally more complicated than false ones. A false claim can be "vaccines cause autism." The true account of vaccine safety involves epidemiology, study design, the history of the original fraudulent claim, the mechanism by which vaccines cannot cause autism, and the statistical methods used to study large populations. The true account takes far more words.

**The Gish Gallop.** In debate, the "Gish Gallop" — named after creationist debater Duane Gish — is the technique of making many false or misleading claims rapidly, overwhelming an opponent's ability to refute each one. The asymmetry is weaponised: ten false claims can be made in thirty seconds; ten full refutations take an hour. This is Brandolini's Law deployed as a debate tactic.

### Why refutation is often counterproductive

Research on misinformation correction shows that straightforward refutation has paradoxical properties:

**The backfire effect.** Early research suggested that corrections could sometimes strengthen false beliefs in highly motivated believers. More recent meta-analyses have found this effect is less robust than initially reported, but corrections do sometimes fail and can backfire with specific audiences.

**The illusory truth effect.** Repeating a false claim — even to refute it — increases its perceived credibility through familiarity. The Debunking Handbook recommends "fact then myth" ordering rather than "myth then fact" to mitigate this.

**Bandwidth constraints.** Corrections spread far less virally than false claims. False claims are often emotionally activating (outrage, fear, moral indignation). Corrections are generally not. The information ecosystem systematically rewards false claims with more distribution.

## Examples

| False claim | Energy to produce | Energy to refute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Climate scientists are in it for the grant money" | 10 seconds | Full analysis of funding structures, historical scientific consensus, replication across independent groups |
| "The MMR vaccine causes autism" | 10 seconds (based on a retracted, fraudulent study) | Decades of epidemiological research and science communication effort |
| "We only use 10% of our brain" | 5 seconds | Explanation of neuroscience, neuroimaging, evolutionary cost of brain tissue |
| Election fraud claims (2020 US election) | Continuous production by many actors | 60+ court cases, detailed audit reports, independent investigations |
| Historical revisionism | Single document or video | Comprehensive archival research |

## Explore

**Scale and platform dynamics.** The asymmetry is amplified by social media platforms that optimise for engagement. False claims, particularly those that trigger outrage or fear, are more engaging than careful corrections. The platform's algorithm distributes false claims more widely, reaching more people, generating more engagement, which triggers more algorithmic distribution. Corrections do not produce the same engagement loop.

**Professional fact-checkers.** Major journalism organisations fund dedicated fact-checking operations specifically because Brandolini's Law makes individual refutation impractical. Snopes, PolitiFact, and equivalents are institutional responses to the asymmetry: they amortise the energy cost of refutation across large audiences. But fact-checking organisations are outnumbered by misinformation producers by several orders of magnitude.

**Prebunking.** Research in inoculation theory (Lewandowsky, Cook et al.) suggests that prebunking — warning people in advance about the forms misinformation takes, before they encounter specific instances — may be more effective than post-hoc correction. This is a partial solution to Brandolini's asymmetry: if audiences have schemas for recognising misinformation techniques, the processing cost of correction falls.

**Information operations.** State and non-state actors have operationalised Brandolini's Law as a deliberate strategy: produce overwhelming quantities of false or misleading information, preventing any effective correction. The goal is not to persuade with specific false claims but to pollute the information environment with so much noise that audiences cannot identify reliable signal.

## History

**2007.** Nassim Taleb's *The Black Swan* discusses the asymmetric costs of demonstrating and refuting claims, one of Brandolini's cited inspirations.

**2013.** Alberto Brandolini posts the Bullshit Asymmetry Principle on Twitter. The post circulates virally in technology and media circles.

**2016.** The Brexit referendum and US presidential election focus widespread attention on misinformation at scale. Brandolini's Law becomes a standard reference in discussions of the problem.

**2017–2020.** Multiple academic research programmes on misinformation correction, inoculation theory, and platform responsibility cite the asymmetry as a foundational challenge.

**2020.** The Debunking Handbook 2020 is published as an open-access guide to effective misinformation correction, explicitly addressing the asymmetry Brandolini identified.

## Why It Matters

**Democracy and informed consent.** Democratic deliberation requires an information environment where citizens can distinguish true from false claims. Brandolini's Law describes a structural feature of information environments that works against this requirement. The asymmetry does not merely inconvenience fact-checkers; it undermines a prerequisite of legitimate democratic governance.

**Science communication.** Scientists communicate findings to public audiences after years of research. Those findings can be misrepresented in seconds by motivated actors. The structural asymmetry means that science communication requires persistent institutional investment — in communication training, in media relationships, in public understanding — just to hold ground against misinformation at scale.

**Epistemic self-defence.** Understanding Brandolini's Law is part of epistemic self-defence for individuals. Recognising that a false claim seems much simpler than the true account is not evidence that the simple version is correct — it is evidence that the true account is complex. Simplicity of a claim is not evidence of its truth; it may be evidence of its falseness.

## Modern Relevance

**Generative AI and misinformation production.** Large language models can generate plausible-sounding false claims at industrial scale, at near-zero marginal cost. This extends Brandolini's Law into a new regime: if one person could produce one false claim per hour, now one person with an AI system can produce thousands. The asymmetry widens dramatically.

**Platform design.** Reducing the Brandolini asymmetry through platform design is an active area of research and policy. Friction on sharing (prompting users to read before sharing), visibility of corrections, algorithmic down-ranking of repeated false claims, and fact-check labels are all attempts to reduce the structural advantage of misinformation producers over correctors.

**AI safety and misinformation detection.** Automated fact-checking — using AI systems to detect and correct misinformation at scale — is proposed as a partial solution to the asymmetry: if the energy cost of refutation can be mechanised, the structural advantage of misinformation producers narrows. Current systems are insufficiently accurate and too easily fooled by novel misinformation to fully close the gap.

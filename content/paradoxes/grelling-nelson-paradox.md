## Discover

Some words describe themselves.

The word "short" is itself short — only five letters. So "short" is
*autological*: it applies to itself.

The word "English" is itself an English word. Autological.

The word "polysyllabic" has many syllables. Autological.

Other words do not describe themselves.

The word "long" is not long — it has only four letters. So "long" is
*heterological*: it does not apply to itself.

The word "French" is not a French word. Heterological.

The word "monosyllabic" has five syllables and so does not describe itself.
Heterological.

Now the question: is the word "heterological" itself heterological?

**Suppose it is heterological.** Then the word "heterological" does not apply
to itself. But "heterological" means "does not apply to itself." If "heterological"
does not apply to itself, then it *is* heterological — and it *does* apply to
itself. Contradiction.

**Suppose it is autological.** Then "heterological" applies to itself. But
"heterological" means "does not apply to itself." So if it applies to itself, it
does not apply to itself. Contradiction.

Either assumption leads to a contradiction. The word "heterological" can be
neither heterological nor autological.

## Understand

The paradox has the same structure as Russell's paradox. Russell's version asks
about the set $R$ of all sets that are not members of themselves. The
Grelling-Nelson version asks about the predicate "heterological," defined as the
predicate that applies to all predicates that do not apply to themselves.

In both cases, the self-application generates a contradiction:

- Russell: $R \in R \iff R \notin R$
- Grelling: "heterological" is heterological $\iff$ "heterological" is not heterological

### Why this matters beyond word games

Russell's paradox attacked set theory — a technical mathematical framework. The
Grelling-Nelson paradox shows that the problem is deeper. You do not need set
theory, or any formal system. Ordinary language contains the concept of a word
applying or not applying to itself, and that concept alone generates the
contradiction.

If you try to fix the paradox by restricting language — ruling that a word
cannot be applied to itself — you run into trouble immediately. "Long" is a word
that does not apply to itself. That sentence applies the concept "does not apply
to itself" to a word. The restriction keeps slipping.

## Examples

**More autological words.** "Noun" is a noun. "Readable" is readable (in the
sense that it can be read). "Abridged" — well, it is a fairly short word for
such a large concept. "Sesquipedalian" (meaning "long-worded") is itself
sesquipedalian. These examples show that autology is a real and non-trivial
category.

**More heterological words.** "Verb" is not a verb. "Misspelled" is not
misspelled. "Invisible" is visible on the page. "Unwritten" is written.
"Silent" is not silent — it makes a sound when spoken.

**The borderline cases.** Some words are genuinely hard to classify. Is
"meaningful" meaningful? Most people say yes. Is "useless" useless? Depends on
your theory of use. These borderlines are features, not bugs: they show that the
autological/heterological distinction is not artificial but genuinely tracks a
distinction we care about.

## Explore

**Type theory as a resolution.** Bertrand Russell's own solution to his paradox
was the theory of types: divide all objects into a hierarchy of types, and
restrict predicates to apply only to objects of lower type than themselves. On
this view, a predicate cannot be applied to itself — the question "is
'heterological' heterological?" is not false but *meaningless*, because it
attempts to apply a predicate at its own level rather than a lower one.

This is formally consistent, but it requires giving up a lot of intuitive
reasoning. Many things we want to say — including many self-referential
sentences that are perfectly well-behaved — become formally unutterable.

**Tarski's hierarchy of languages.** Alfred Tarski's approach divides language
into levels: the object language and the metalanguage. Predicates in the object
language can only be applied to objects; to say that a predicate applies to
itself requires moving to the metalanguage. The hierarchy can be extended
indefinitely, and no finite level contains its own truth predicate. This approach
underlies formal theories of truth and avoids the Liar paradox and Grelling's
paradox — at the cost of accepting that there is no single unified language in
which all true statements can be expressed.

**Is the paradox really different from Russell's?** Some logicians regard
Grelling-Nelson as simply a notational variant of Russell's paradox, since the
same substitution — predicate for set, applies-to for membership — transforms
one into the other. Others hold that they are genuinely different, because the
Grelling version is about linguistic meaning and cannot be dissolved by formal
restrictions on set membership. The question of whether the two paradoxes share
a single root or have distinct sources is itself philosophically contested.

## Deep Dive

### The formal structure

Let $P$ be the domain of predicates. Define the predicate $H$ ("heterological")
by:

$$H(x) \iff \neg x(x)$$

(for any predicate $x$, $H(x)$ holds iff $x$ does not apply to itself).

Substituting $H$ for $x$:

$$H(H) \iff \neg H(H)$$

This is a direct contradiction. The derivation uses only the definition of $H$
and the universal generalisation that $H$ applies to all predicates satisfying
its defining condition.

The parallel with Russell's paradox: define $R = \{x : x \notin x\}$. Then
$R \in R \iff R \notin R$. The logical form is identical; only the domain
(sets vs. predicates) and the membership relation (set membership vs. predicate
application) differ.

### What cannot fix it

**Restricting self-application:** If you simply rule that predicates cannot be
applied to themselves, you lose the ability to say "'verb' is not a verb" and
"'long' is not long," which are perfectly meaningful and true sentences. The
restriction is too broad.

**Declaring "heterological" undefined:** This is Tarski's move. It is formally
clean but requires accepting that the predicate "heterological" is not a
legitimate predicate at any level of the hierarchy where it can be applied to
itself. The cost is a stratified, non-unified language.

**Paraconsistent logic:** Some logicians accept that $H(H) \wedge \neg H(H)$
is simply true — that contradictions can be true without trivialising the system.
This requires revising the principle of explosion (ex contradictione quodlibet:
from a contradiction, anything follows), which is a significant departure from
classical logic.

## History

Grelling and Nelson published their paradox in 1908, the year after Russell
published his theory of types as a response to his own paradox. They were
working in the Friesian tradition of German philosophy, interested in the
foundations of logic. The paradox appeared in a paper about Russell's and
Burali-Forti's paradoxes, framed as a demonstration that the same problem could
arise in predicate language.

The paradox is less famous than Russell's because set theory became the central
arena for foundational work in mathematics, and Russell's version was directly
relevant to the major mathematical programmes. Grelling's version is more
relevant to the philosophy of language and linguistics.

Kurt Grelling was murdered at Auschwitz in 1942. Leonard Nelson had died in 1927.
Neither lived to see the paradox become a standard teaching example.

## Why It Matters

**It shows that the crisis in set theory's foundations was not just about sets.**
If you could replace all the set-theoretic language in Russell's paradox with
predicate language and get the same contradiction, the problem was deeper than
the axioms of set theory. It was about the logic of self-reference itself.

**It drives the development of formal semantics.** The need to avoid both the
Liar paradox and Grelling-Nelson motivated Tarski's theory of truth and the
hierarchy of metalanguages. That framework underlies the semantics of formal
languages in logic and computer science.

**It is a clean test case for theories of predication.** Any theory of how
predicates work must say something about whether a predicate can apply to itself.
Grelling-Nelson shows that the most natural answer — that any predicate can be
applied to any predicate — is inconsistent.

## Modern Relevance

**Type systems in programming.** The design of type systems in programming
languages is directly motivated by the need to prevent self-referential
contradictions of exactly this kind. Languages that allow unrestricted
self-application of types generate paradoxes at the level of the type system;
stratified type systems avoid them at the cost of some expressiveness.

**Natural language processing.** Systems that reason about language must handle
predicates that apply to predicates — "this sentence is true," "that description
is accurate." Grelling-Nelson is a standing reminder that naive self-application
of semantic predicates is inconsistent, and that any sufficiently expressive
language must handle the resulting contradictions explicitly.

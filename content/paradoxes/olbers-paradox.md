## Discover

Go outside on a clear night and look up. Nearly all of what you see is black.

That seems like the least surprising fact in the world. The Sun is round the
other side of the Earth. Of course it is dark.

But try this. Imagine the universe goes on forever, and is stocked with stars
all the way out, roughly evenly, and always has been. Now point your finger at
any patch of black sky at all. Follow that line outwards. A hundred light years,
a million, a billion — sooner or later that line has to run into the glowing
surface of *some* star. There is nowhere for it to escape to.

The same is true of the next direction. And the next. And every one.

So every single point on the sky should have a star sitting somewhere behind it.
The night should not be black with dots on it. It should be a solid,
blinding wall — as bright, in every direction you turn, as the surface of the
Sun.

It is not. Something in that story is false.

## Understand

The argument has one step in it that sounds as though it ought to rescue us, and
does not.

**"Surely distant stars are too faint to count?"** They are faint. Put a star
twice as far away and it looks four times dimmer, because its light has spread
over four times the area. But now count how many stars sit at that distance.
Picture the universe as a set of nested shells around you, like the layers of an
onion. Double a shell's radius and its surface goes up fourfold, so it contains
four times as many stars.

Four times as many, each a quarter as bright. **Every shell delivers exactly the
same amount of light as the one inside it.** Add up endlessly many shells and
you get an endless amount of light.

In reality it does not become infinitely bright, because stars are not points —
a near star blocks the one behind it. The sky saturates instead. But it
saturates at the brightness of a stellar surface, which is the same conclusion
wearing a hat: an infinite, eternal, star-filled universe has a sky as bright as
the Sun, everywhere, at all hours.

So one of the assumptions has to go. There are three candidates, and only one of
them works.

### The wrong answer that is still taught

**"Dust and gas in between soak up the light."**

This is what Olbers proposed in 1823, and what de Chéseaux proposed in 1744, and
what a great many textbooks and museum panels still say. It is wrong, and it is
wrong for a reason worth understanding.

Something that absorbs light gets hot. A dust cloud sitting in an infinitely
old, infinitely bright bath of starlight keeps absorbing until it reaches the
same temperature as the light falling on it. At that point it radiates away
exactly as much as it takes in. It is no longer a shade. It is a lamp, glowing
just as fiercely as the stars it was supposed to be hiding.

You cannot dim an eternal furnace by hanging a curtain in it. Given eternity, the
curtain becomes part of the furnace. The second law of thermodynamics forbids the
escape, and Olbers — writing in 1823, three decades before thermodynamics was
assembled — had no way to know that.

### The half-answer

**"The universe is expanding, so distant light is stretched and weakened."**

This one is true. Light from a receding galaxy arrives redshifted: each photon
carries less energy, and the photons arrive less often. Distant sources really
are dimmed beyond what distance alone would do.

But it is nowhere near enough, and it is not the main effect. Calculations from
the 1980s put the suppression from expansion at a factor of roughly two. The
darkness we actually observe needs a factor of about ten trillion.

Redshift is a footnote here. It gets top billing because it is the most famous
fact about the universe, not because it is doing the work.

### The answer

**The universe has not been here long enough.**

Light is fast but not instant. The universe is about 13.8 billion years old, and
stars have been shining for slightly less than that. So when you follow your
finger out along a line of sight, you do not get to travel forever. You run out
of *time* before you run out of *space*. Beyond a certain distance there is
nothing to see, not because nothing is there, but because its light is still on
its way.

How much time would you need? Work out how far a line of sight typically travels
through a universe at the observed density of stars before it strikes one, and
the answer is somewhere near $10^{23}$ light years. For the sky to fill in, the
universe would have to have been shining for about $10^{23}$ years.

It has been shining for about $10^{10}$.

We are short by thirteen orders of magnitude. That is the missing factor of ten
trillion, and it is why the night is dark.

There is a second, independent reason, and it is just as decisive: there is not
enough fuel. Stars shine by converting a fraction of a percent of their mass into
light. Even if you waited $10^{23}$ years, the stars would have burned out long
before the sky filled up. An eternal blaze needs an eternal energy supply, and
the universe does not have one.

### The twist

Point a radio telescope anywhere in the sky and you find that it *is* uniformly
bright after all. The cosmic microwave background fills every direction at a
temperature of 2.7 kelvin — the cooled, stretched-out glow of a universe that
really was, once, hot and opaque in every direction.

The blazing sky of Olbers' nightmare exists. It is just redshifted by a factor of
about 1100, out of the visible and into the microwaves, so it warms your skin by
nothing at all and shows up as a faint hiss on an untuned television. Here — and
only here — the expansion of the universe is genuinely the reason you cannot see
it.

## Examples

**The forest.** Stand in a wood that goes on forever, with trees scattered
evenly. Look horizontally in any direction. Nearby you see gaps between trunks;
further out, the gaps are filled by trees behind; further still, filled again.
Your line of sight is eventually blocked no matter where you aim it. You see a
solid wall of bark.

Now stand in a wood that is very large but not infinite — or, better, one whose
trees were planted only recently and are still saplings a few centimetres tall.
You see the far side. That is our sky.

**The shells, in numbers.** Take shells of thickness one light year at increasing
distance. Suppose the shell at 1000 light years contributes some amount of light,
call it one unit.

| Shell radius | Stars in it (relative) | Brightness of each | Shell's contribution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1,000 ly | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 2,000 ly | 4 | 1/4 | 1 |
| 10,000 ly | 100 | 1/100 | 1 |
| 1,000,000 ly | 1,000,000 | 1/1,000,000 | 1 |

The column on the right never decreases. That is the whole paradox in one table.

**How dark is dark?** The night sky away from the Milky Way is roughly $10^{13}$
times fainter than the surface of the Sun. That number is not a coincidence: it
is approximately the ratio between the age of the universe and the $10^{23}$
years the sky would need to fill in.

## Explore

Calling this a paradox is a slight courtesy. Nothing contradicts anything; a set
of assumptions makes a prediction, and the prediction is false. The interest lies
entirely in **which** assumption fails, and that question has a longer and more
tangled history than the puzzle itself.

**Which assumption is on trial.** The argument needs all of the following: space
is infinite (or at least very large), it is uniformly filled with stars, it is
static, and it is infinitely old. Reject any one and the blaze goes away.
Different centuries rejected different ones.

**The expansion answer had a good reason to exist.** In the steady-state
cosmology of Hermann Bondi, Thomas Gold and Fred Hoyle, proposed in 1948, the
universe genuinely *is* infinitely old and infinitely large. There is no finite
age to appeal to. In that model the redshift really is the resolution — the only
one available. Bondi's 1952 textbook *Cosmology* discussed the problem at length
and fixed the name "Olbers' paradox" in the literature, and it presented the
expansion answer because his cosmology required it.

Steady state lost. The finite-age answer was left standing, but the expansion
answer had already propagated into a generation of textbooks and stayed there.

**Harrison's correction.** Edward Harrison argued from the 1960s onward, and at
book length in *Darkness at Night* (1987), that even in a static universe of
finite age the sky would be just as dark, and that the redshift changes the
answer by a factor of order two against a required factor of $10^{13}$.
Quantitative work in the 1980s by Paul Wesson and collaborators put numbers to
this. The consensus since is Harrison's: **finite age dominates, expansion is a
minor correction, and absorption is no answer at all.**

**The escape nobody takes seriously any more.** You can also break the argument
by giving up uniformity. If stars cluster into galaxies, galaxies into clusters,
clusters into superclusters, and so on without limit — a hierarchical or fractal
universe, proposed by Carl Charlier in 1908 and revived by Benoît Mandelbrot in
the 1970s — then the average density of matter falls to zero at large scales and
the lines of sight escape. It is a real mathematical escape. It is also
contradicted by observation: on scales above a few hundred million light years
the universe looks homogeneous, not fractal.

**Why it stays interesting.** Almost every popular account of this puzzle gets
the resolution wrong, usually by promoting the redshift or by keeping Olbers'
own dust. That the correct answer was written down by a poet in 1848 and by Lord
Kelvin in 1901, and still had to be argued for in the 1980s, says something
uncomfortable about how explanations propagate.

## History

**1576.** Thomas Digges published an English rendering of Copernicus with a
diagram of stars scattered through unbounded space — one of the first
infinite-universe cosmologies in print. He noticed the difficulty and waved it
away: the distant stars, he said, are simply too faint to see. This is the wrong
answer, but he was the first to feel the need for one.

**1610.** Johannes Kepler, replying to Galileo's *Sidereus Nuncius* in his
*Dissertatio cum Nuncio Sidereo*, stated the argument properly for the first
time. If stars filled an endless space the whole sky would shine; it does not;
therefore the realm of stars is bounded and there is a dark wall around it.
Kepler took the darkness as *evidence*, which is precisely the right instinct.

**1721.** Edmond Halley read two papers to the Royal Society on the infinity of
the sphere of fixed stars. He tried to escape by arguing that remote stars
subtend such small angles that their light becomes negligible. The argument does
not work — as we saw, the shrinking is exactly cancelled by the growing number of
stars — and Halley appears not to have noticed.

**1744.** Jean-Philippe Loys de Chéseaux, in an appendix to his study of the
great comet of 1743, gave the first quantitative treatment. He computed how far
one must look before the stars tile the sky, obtained an enormous number, and
proposed that a thin absorbing fluid in space removes the excess light.

**1823.** Heinrich Olbers published *Ueber die Durchsichtigkeit des Weltraums* —
"On the transparency of space" — in Bode's astronomical yearbook. He restated the
problem clearly and proposed, apparently independently of de Chéseaux, the same
absorbing medium. He is the only one of these men to have the puzzle named after
him, and the answer he gave is wrong. Harrison's verdict is that Olbers'
contribution was mostly to be the most-read statement of a problem others had
posed better.

**1848.** Edgar Allan Poe, in *Eureka: A Prose Poem*, wrote the passage that
should have ended it:

> Were the succession of stars endless, then the background of the sky would
> present us a uniform luminosity, like that displayed by the Galaxy — since
> there could be absolutely no point, in all that background, at which would not
> exist a star. The only mode, therefore, in which, under such a state of
> affairs, we could comprehend the voids which our telescopes find in
> innumerable directions, would be by supposing the distance of the invisible
> background so immense that no ray from it has yet been able to reach us at all.

That is the finite-age resolution, correct in outline, written by a poet with no
physics to support it, sixty years before anyone had a reason to believe the
universe had a beginning. Poe was not doing science and should not be credited
with a discovery. He should be credited with the sentence.

**1901.** William Thomson, Lord Kelvin, in an essay on ether and gravitational
matter through infinite space, gave what is essentially the modern quantitative
argument: stars have finite lifetimes and have been shining for a finite time, so
only a limited shell of them can contribute. It was largely overlooked, and
Harrison's rediscovery of it in the 1980s is one of the small pleasures of the
literature.

**1948–1952.** The steady-state cosmology forces attention back to the problem;
Bondi's *Cosmology* names it after Olbers and answers it with expansion.

**1964–1987.** Harrison develops and then publishes the finite-age account,
recovers the Poe and Kelvin priority, and settles the question.

## Why It Matters

**It is cosmology you can do with your own eyes.** Before there were telescopes
capable of measuring redshifts, before anyone had heard of a Big Bang, the plain
fact that it gets dark at night ruled out the simplest imaginable universe:
infinite, unchanging, eternal, evenly filled. That is a real empirical constraint
derived from an observation every human being has made.

**It made the finite age of the universe thinkable.** For most of the history of
Western cosmology an eternal universe was the default, and a universe with a
beginning was a theological claim rather than a physical one. The dark sky is an
argument, available since 1610, that the default cannot be right. It was not
recognised as such until the twentieth century, which is part of its interest.

**It introduced the idea of a horizon.** The modern answer — we see a finite
universe because light is slow and the universe is young — is the first
appearance of the observational horizon that now runs through all of cosmology.
Everything we can ever learn about the universe comes from inside a sphere
defined by how long light has had to travel. Olbers' paradox is the oldest
consequence of that fact.

**It is a case study in a wrong answer surviving.** The dust explanation is
memorable, intuitive, given by the man the paradox is named after, and false. It
outlived its own refutation by roughly a century and a half, and still appears in
print.

## Modern Relevance

**The microwave background is Olbers' sky.** The prediction was right: there
*is* a uniform glow filling every direction. It comes from the hot plasma of the
young universe rather than from stars, and expansion has stretched it from
white-hot to 2.7 kelvin. Measuring the tiny variations in that glow — COBE in
1992, WMAP, Planck — is how the age, geometry and composition of the universe are
now determined. The paradox's answer became its own most productive observation.

**Counting all the starlight ever emitted.** The extragalactic background light
is the integrated output of every star that has ever shone, and it is exactly the
quantity Olbers' argument is about. It is faint and very hard to measure directly
because foreground zodiacal light swamps it. Astronomers now measure it
*indirectly*: high-energy gamma rays from distant blazars are absorbed by
collisions with background photons on the way here, so the attenuation of the
gamma-ray spectrum encodes the background's intensity. The Fermi Gamma-ray Space
Telescope collaboration used this in 2018 to reconstruct the history of star
formation across cosmic time. The night sky is dark, and precisely how dark tells
you when the stars turned on.

**The shape of the argument keeps recurring.** "If X were common, we would
already see it; we do not; therefore something limits X" is now a standard move
in astronomy — used on interstellar civilisations, on primordial black holes, on
Population III stars, on cosmic strings. Olbers' paradox is the first time it
worked, and also the first demonstration that the conclusion can turn out to be
about the observer's horizon rather than about the thing being counted.

## Deep Dive

**The divergence.** Let stars have number density $n$, mean luminosity $L$, and
sit in a static, infinitely old, Euclidean, uniformly filled universe. A shell of
radius $r$ and thickness $dr$ contains $4\pi r^2 n\,dr$ stars, each delivering a
flux $L/4\pi r^2$ at the observer. The total flux received from within radius $R$
is

$$\Phi(R) = \int_0^R \left(4\pi r^2 n\,dr\right)\frac{L}{4\pi r^2} = n L R .$$

The $r^2$ factors cancel exactly. $\Phi$ grows without bound, linearly in $R$.

**The saturation.** Stars are not points. Give each a cross-section $\sigma$. The
probability that a line of sight reaches radius $r$ unobstructed is $e^{-r/\lambda}$
with mean free path

$$\lambda = \frac{1}{n\sigma}.$$

The sky's surface brightness then saturates rather than diverging:

$$\Sigma_{\text{sky}} = \Sigma_\star\left(1 - e^{-R/\lambda}\right),$$

where $\Sigma_\star$ is the surface brightness of a typical stellar surface. For
$R \gg \lambda$ the sky is as bright as the Sun's disc, in every direction. For
$R \ll \lambda$ it is fainter by the factor $R/\lambda$.

**Putting numbers in.** Using the observed density of stars in galaxies and the
observed density of galaxies, Harrison estimates

$$\lambda \sim 10^{23}\ \text{light years}.$$

The horizon set by the finite age of the universe gives $R \sim 10^{10}$ light
years. Hence

$$\frac{\Sigma_{\text{sky}}}{\Sigma_\star} \approx \frac{R}{\lambda} \sim 10^{-13}.$$

The observed extragalactic background is of that order. **This single ratio is
the resolution of the paradox**, and note that no expansion appears anywhere in
it.

**The energy bound.** There is a second, independent ceiling. Stars convert a
fraction $\varepsilon \approx 0.007$ of rest mass into radiation, and only a
small fraction $f$ of baryons have ever been processed through stars. The energy
density of starlight is therefore bounded by

$$u_\star \lesssim f\,\varepsilon\,\rho_b c^2 \ll \rho_b c^2 ,$$

which is far below the $u = aT_\star^4$ required for a sky at stellar
temperature. Even with unlimited time, the universe cannot afford Olbers' sky.
The finite lifetime of stars and the finite age of the universe are two separate
reasons, and either alone suffices.

**What expansion actually contributes.** For a source at redshift $z$, bolometric
surface brightness is suppressed by $(1+z)^{-4}$ — one factor of $(1+z)$ for
photon energy, one for arrival rate, and two from the relation between angular
diameter and luminosity distance. Integrating this over the emission history of a
realistic cosmology reduces the background by a factor of order unity — roughly
two, not thirteen orders of magnitude. Setting the expansion rate to zero while
holding the age fixed leaves the sky dark. Setting the age to infinity while
holding the expansion leaves it bright. That asymmetry is the whole argument for
finite age as the resolution.

**Why absorption fails, formally.** In a static universe of infinite age the
radiation field reaches thermal equilibrium with the matter in it. Kirchhoff's
law requires that a body's emissivity equal its absorptivity at every frequency,
so at equilibrium any absorbing medium radiates exactly what it absorbs. The
opacity redistributes the light; it cannot remove it. The absorbing medium ends
at the temperature of the stellar surfaces and the sky is just as bright, now
including the dust.

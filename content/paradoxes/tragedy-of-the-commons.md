## Discover

Picture a big green field in the middle of a village. It does not belong to
anyone. Everybody's sheep graze on it.

You have ten sheep on the field. So does everyone else. There is just about
enough grass.

Now you think: *what if I put one more sheep out there?*

That extra sheep is entirely yours. Its wool is yours, its lambs are yours. All
the good of it comes to you.

The cost is that the field gets a little more crowded and the grass gets a little
thinner. But that thinner grass is shared out among every sheep in the village.
You bear a tiny sliver of it. Everyone else bears the rest.

One extra sheep: all of the gain, a sliver of the cost. Obviously you add the
sheep.

Here is the difficult part. Everyone in the village is standing at the edge of
the same field, doing the same sum, and getting the same answer.

Add the sheep. Add another. Add another.

By autumn there is no grass, and everybody's sheep are thin.

Not one person in the village did anything foolish. Every single decision was
sensible when it was made. And the field is bare.

## Understand

The trap has a precise shape, and once you see it you will see it everywhere.

**An action is privately profitable and socially costly, and the person deciding
gets the profit while the group pays the cost.** In economics the leftover cost
is called an *externality* — a real consequence of your choice that lands outside
your accounts.

Nothing in the story depends on greed. It works just as well with a village of
kind, thoughtful people. Restraint by one herder does not save the field; it just
leaves more grass for whoever is less restrained. That is what makes the situation
genuinely hard rather than merely a moral failing. Being good, on its own, does
not help. It only helps if everyone else is good too, and nobody can guarantee
that from inside their own farmyard.

Garrett Hardin, writing in *Science* in 1968, put it starkly: "Freedom in a
commons brings ruin to all." He argued the outcome was inescapable, and that the
only two escapes were to privatise the commons — divide the field into fenced
plots, so each person bears their own costs — or to have an outside authority
impose limits.

### The part Hardin got wrong

He was right about the incentive structure. He was wrong that the outcome is
inevitable, and the evidence against him is now overwhelming.

Elinor Ostrom, a political economist at Indiana University, spent decades doing
something Hardin never did: she went and looked. She and her collaborators
assembled hundreds of case studies of real shared resources — Swiss alpine
pastures grazed in common since the thirteenth century, Japanese village forests,
irrigation networks in Spain and the Philippines, inshore fisheries in Turkey and
Maine, groundwater basins in California.

Many of these had been managed sustainably for centuries. Not by privatisation.
Not by a state. By the users themselves, who had built their own rules, their own
ways of watching each other, and their own penalties for cheating — and had kept
them working across generations.

Some commons *do* collapse; Ostrom studied failures too, and was careful about
them. But the collapse is not a law of nature. It is what happens when the
governing arrangements are absent, undermined, or destroyed. Ostrom received the
Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2009 for this work.

Historians added a second correction. Hardin's illustration invokes the English
commons, but the actual medieval English and Welsh commons were not open to all.
Grazing rights were attached to particular holdings, the number of animals each
commoner could pasture was capped — the practice was called *stinting* — and
manor courts enforced the caps and fined violators. The historical example that
gave the idea its name was a regulated resource, not a free-for-all. Susan Jane
Buck Cox laid out the documentary evidence in 1985 in a paper titled, pointedly,
"No Tragedy of the Commons".

The distinction that survives all of this is between a **commons**, which has
identified users and rules, and **open access**, which has neither. Hardin
described open access and called it a commons, and the mislabelling has cost the
idea a great deal of accuracy. Ostrom's own view, stated plainly in *Science* in
1999, was that Hardin's analysis is correct for open-access resources and
misleading for everything else.

### What Hardin's essay was actually arguing

The pasture occupies about one page. The essay is about human population.

Hardin's thesis is that population growth is a problem with "no technical
solution", that the freedom to have children is the freedom that must be
withdrawn, and that the mechanism should be "mutual coercion, mutually agreed
upon". He wrote that "the freedom to breed is intolerable". These are his central
claims, not asides, and they are what the pasture illustration was constructed to
support.

Hardin's later writing extended the argument to immigration and famine relief. In
"Lifeboat Ethics" (1974) he argued that wealthy nations are lifeboats that must
refuse to take on the people in the water, and opposed food aid on the grounds
that it would let poor populations grow. He was a founder of, and adviser to,
organisations campaigning to restrict immigration to the United States, and wrote
of the need to preserve a particular ethnic and cultural character in the
population. Scholars who have examined this body of work describe it as nativist
and racist; the Southern Poverty Law Center maintains a file on him on that basis.

None of this makes the incentive analysis of a shared pasture false. It is stated
here because the 1968 essay is still routinely assigned to students as a neutral
piece of ecology, with the pasture excerpted and the argument it was built to
serve left out. A reader should know what they are reading.

## Examples

**The arithmetic of one extra animal.** Ten herders share a pasture, with equal
flocks. An animal is worth £100 a year when 100 animals graze, and every animal
added beyond that thins the grass enough to knock £1 off the yearly value of
*every* animal on the field. So with $N$ animals grazing, each is worth
$200 - N$ pounds.

When a herder adds one animal, they gain that animal's value but lose £1 on each
animal they already own. Everyone else simply loses £1 per animal.

| Animals on the field | Each flock | Value per animal | Total village income | Net gain to a herder who adds one more | Cost pushed onto the other nine |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | 10 | £100 | £10,000 | **+£89** | −£90 |
| 120 | 12 | £80 | £9,600 | **+£67** | −£108 |
| 150 | 15 | £50 | £7,500 | **+£34** | −£135 |
| 180 | 18 | £20 | £3,600 | **+£1** | −£162 |
| 181 | 18.1 | £19 | £3,439 | −£0.10 | — |

The village's total income is highest at exactly 100 animals — £10,000. Every
herder nonetheless has a positive private reason to add animals right up to
around 181, where the last column finally turns negative and the adding stops.

At that point the pasture yields £3,439. **The village has destroyed roughly
two-thirds of its own income, and every single decision along the way was
correct.** The tragedy is arithmetic, not psychology.

**Where it appears, and whether it actually collapsed.**

| Shared resource | Individually rational act | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| North Atlantic cod | Catch one more tonne | Canadian Grand Banks fishery collapsed in 1992; a moratorium put roughly 30,000 people out of work and the stock has still not recovered |
| Atmosphere | Emit one more tonne of CO₂ | Ongoing; the archetypal global open-access resource with no enforcing community |
| Antibiotics | Prescribe for one more borderline case | Rising resistance, a shared resource being depleted prescription by prescription |
| Swiss alpine pastures (Törbel) | Graze one more cow | Did *not* collapse. Rules dating to a 1483 written charter cap each villager's summer grazing at the number of animals they can overwinter on their own land |
| Spanish huerta irrigation (Valencia) | Take a little more water | Did *not* collapse. The Tribunal de las Aguas has met weekly outside the cathedral to arbitrate water disputes for roughly a thousand years |

The bottom two rows are the ones missing from most retellings, and they are the
reason this entry's status is "debated" rather than "resolved".

**Ostrom's eight design principles.** Extracted in 1990 from the long-enduring
cases, and the most useful practical residue of the entire literature:

1. Clearly defined boundaries — who the users are, and where the resource ends.
2. Rules fitted to local conditions, not imported wholesale.
3. Those affected by the rules can participate in changing them.
4. Monitors who are accountable to the users, or are the users themselves.
5. **Graduated** sanctions — a first offence costs little, repeat offences escalate.
6. Cheap, accessible conflict-resolution mechanisms.
7. Recognition by higher authorities of the community's right to organise.
8. For large systems, governance in nested layers.

Principle 5 is the one people find least intuitive and it is among the best
supported. Systems that punish a first offence severely tend to break down,
because the punishment feels unjust and enforcement stops being applied.

## Explore

**The three-way disagreement.** Hardin's own conclusion was that a commons
requires either privatisation or coercive external authority. Ostrom's is that a
third option — self-governance by the users — is not merely possible but common,
and often outperforms the other two because local users hold information that a
distant regulator does not. A third position, associated with the property-rights
economics of Harold Demsetz, holds that the interesting question is why property
rights emerge when they do, and predicts that communities will define rights once
the resource becomes valuable enough to be worth the trouble of defining them.
These are not merely academic. Which one you hold determines whether you respond
to an overfished bay by auctioning quotas, sending inspectors, or funding the
fishermen's own cooperative.

**Where Hardin's analysis genuinely bites.** Ostrom's design principles have
prerequisites: a bounded group, repeated interaction, the ability to observe each
other, and some authority permitting local rule-making. Take those away and the
picture darkens sharply. Deep-sea fisheries in international waters, the
atmosphere, and orbital debris are close to pure open access — billions of
anonymous users, no boundary, no monitoring, no enforcement. Ostrom herself said
so in 1999: the local lessons do not simply scale to global commons, and the
scaling problem is unsolved. Anyone citing Ostrom to suggest that climate change
will resolve itself through community management is misusing her.

**Can the essay be separated from the man?** There are two defensible positions
and both are held by serious people. One: the incentive structure is a piece of
mathematics with no politics in it, and it is legitimate to teach the mechanism
while discarding the author. Two: Hardin's framing is not incidental to his
politics — the specific choices to define the commons as unregulated, to declare
the ruin inevitable, and to conclude that coercion is the only remedy are the
choices a eugenicist argument requires, and teaching the frame smuggles in the
conclusion. What is not defensible is the common third option of assigning the
essay without mentioning any of this.

**A note on terminology.** Much of the confusion in public discussion of this
topic would evaporate if people used "open-access resource" for what Hardin
described and reserved "commons" for a resource with a defined community and
rules. The field made this distinction decades ago. Popular usage has not
followed.

## History

**1833.** William Forster Lloyd, a lecturer in political economy at Oxford,
published *Two Lectures on the Checks to Population*. In it he asks why cattle on
a common are "so puny and stunted" and the common itself "so bare-worn" compared
with adjacent enclosed fields, and gives the incentive answer. Lloyd's context,
like Hardin's, was Malthusian anxiety about population.

**1954.** The economist H. Scott Gordon published a formal model of the
common-property fishery, showing that open-access entry dissipates the entire
economic rent of the resource. The mathematics of the tragedy was in the
economics literature well before it had a memorable name.

**December 1968.** Hardin, then professor of human ecology at UC Santa Barbara,
published "The Tragedy of the Commons" in *Science*. It began as his presidential
address to the Pacific Division of the American Association for the Advancement
of Science. It became one of the most cited papers the journal has ever
published, and the phrase entered general English.

**1975–1990.** Field researchers repeatedly failed to find Hardin's predicted
collapse in real common-property regimes. Anthropologists studying inshore
fisheries, historians of the English open-field system, and development
economists working on irrigation all reported the same thing: the users had
rules. In 1985 Susan Jane Buck Cox published the historical rebuttal. In 1986 the
US National Research Council convened a panel on common property that gathered
much of this evidence in one place.

**1990.** Elinor Ostrom published *Governing the Commons*. Its method was what
made it decisive: rather than presenting counterexamples, it compared long-lived
successes with documented failures and asked what distinguished them,
generating the eight design principles as an empirical result rather than a
recommendation.

**1999.** Ostrom, with Burger, Field, Norgaard and Policansky, published
"Revisiting the Commons" in *Science* — the same journal, thirty-one years later
— distinguishing open access from common property and posing the global-commons
scaling problem.

**2009.** Ostrom was awarded the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, the
first woman to receive it. The prize committee's citation named the challenge to
"the conventional wisdom that common property is poorly managed and should be
either regulated by central authorities or privatised".

**2003–present.** Hardin died in 2003. Reassessment of his wider work has
intensified since, with a substantial literature examining the relationship
between the commons argument and his advocacy on population, immigration and
race.

## Why It Matters

**It supplied the default frame for environmental policy for forty years.** If
ruin is inevitable without ownership or enforcement, then the policy menu has two
items. A great deal of fisheries management, forestry policy and land reform in
the second half of the twentieth century was designed on that assumption, and
some of it did real damage — nationalising forests in Nepal and parts of Africa
dispossessed communities that had been managing them successfully, and in several
documented cases the forests deteriorated faster afterwards, because the
functioning local institution was destroyed and the replacement state authority
could not monitor what the villagers had monitored for free.

**Ostrom's correction changed how institutions get designed.** The eight design
principles are now used directly in fisheries co-management, irrigation projects,
community forestry and the governance of digital commons. The practical shift is
from asking "who should own this?" to asking "who are the users, can they see
each other, and can they make and enforce their own rules?"

**It gave the externality a story.** Economists had the formal concept of an
external cost since Pigou in 1920, but "the tragedy of the commons" is the version
that a legislator, a journalist or a fisherman can hold in their head. Concepts
travel on their illustrations, which is precisely why the accuracy of the
illustration matters.

**It is a case study in how ideas get simplified in transmission.** A one-page
illustration inside a eugenics argument became a stand-alone law of social nature,
was refuted empirically by a body of fieldwork that won a Nobel prize, and is
still taught in its refuted form. Very few ideas offer such a clean record of the
gap between what a field knows and what the public has been told.

## Modern Relevance

**Climate.** Greenhouse gas emissions are the closest thing to a true global
open-access problem, and it is exactly the case where Ostrom's solutions are
hardest to apply: no boundary, no way to exclude, weak monitoring, no shared
enforcer. Ostrom's own late work proposed a *polycentric* response — cities,
firms, regions and states acting at many scales at once rather than waiting for a
single global agreement — on the grounds that the global commons will never have
the single community that the local commons has.

**Antibiotic resistance.** The most direct medical instance, and one where the
Ostrom prescription is actually being applied: hospital antimicrobial stewardship
programmes are, structurally, monitoring plus graduated sanctions applied to a
bounded community of prescribers.

**Groundwater.** Aquifers under the US High Plains, northern India and the
Central Valley of California are being drawn down by individually rational
pumping. Some basins — notably several in southern California — have escaped
through negotiated adjudication among pumpers, which is an Ostrom outcome and one
she personally studied for her doctorate.

**Digital and informational commons.** Wikipedia, open-source software and public
scientific datasets are shared resources vulnerable to depletion of a different
kind: not overuse, but under-contribution and abuse. Their governance
arrangements — reputation, graduated blocks, elected arbitration, maintainer
hierarchies — were invented independently and map onto Ostrom's principles
closely enough that she and Charlotte Hess wrote a book about it.

**Orbital debris.** Low Earth orbit is a shared resource with rapidly growing
use, no property rights, no enforcement, and a physical mechanism for
catastrophic collapse in the Kessler syndrome, where debris collisions cascade.
It is the newest large commons, and it currently has none of the eight design
principles in place.

**Free riding on the open web.** Web publishers bear the cost of
serving crawlers that train models they do not benefit from, and the individually
rational response — blocking crawlers, paywalling, poisoning content — degrades
the shared corpus that everyone including the publishers draws on. The structure
is familiar; the governing institutions do not yet exist.

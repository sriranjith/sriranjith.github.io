## Discover

2003. Photographer Kenneth Adelman is documenting the California coastline for
the California Coastal Records Project — 12,000 aerial photographs of the coast,
publicly available, part of an environmental monitoring effort.

One of the photographs includes the Malibu estate of Barbra Streisand. The
photograph has been downloaded six times since the archive went online. Twice
by Adelman himself.

Streisand's lawyers file a $50 million lawsuit demanding the photograph's
removal. The lawsuit generates news coverage. Journalists write about it. People
search for the photograph. In the month following the lawsuit, the photograph is
downloaded 420,000 times.

The lawsuit fails. The photograph remains online. It has now been seen by several
orders of magnitude more people than it would have been if the lawsuit had never
been filed.

## Understand

The Streisand Effect is an instance of **reactance** — the psychological
principle that people value things more when they believe access to them is being
restricted. The urge to see the forbidden thing is partly independent of interest
in the thing itself; the suppression is the provocation.

In the pre-internet era, suppression could sometimes work. A newspaper could
be pulled from newsstands. A book could be pulped. A photograph could be
confiscated. The suppression had to scale to the number of copies.

The internet broke this. A legal demand to remove content from one location
generates news coverage that links to that content and explains exactly what it
is and why it is worth finding. Search interest spikes. Archives and mirrors
appear faster than legal action can follow them. The Streisand Effect is
reactance multiplied by network effects and decentralised storage.

### The anatomy of a backfire

The pattern is reliable:
1. A powerful party attempts to suppress information they find damaging or
   embarrassing.
2. The suppression attempt becomes public — through court filings, news coverage,
   social media, or just the target's announcement that they are being suppressed.
3. The suppression attempt itself becomes the news. Outlets that would not have
   covered the original story cover the suppression story.
4. Interest in the original content spikes, and the content spreads far beyond
   its original reach.
5. The suppression fails, and the original content is now permanently associated
   with the suppression story.

### When suppression works

The effect is not universal. Suppression can work when:
- The information is not yet public and can be kept that way without legal action
  becoming news.
- The suppression is of practical access (a private communication, a proprietary
  document) rather than published content.
- The content has no inherent interest other than the fact of suppression — and
  even then, the suppression must be kept quiet.

The error in most high-profile cases is that the suppression attempt is itself
public, making it a better story than the original content.

## Explore

**The forbidden fruit mechanism.** The psychological literature on reactance
(Jack Brehm, 1966) predicts that restriction increases desire. "Do not press
this button" makes people want to press the button. The Streisand Effect is the
public information version of this: "this information is suppressed" makes
people want the information.

**Attacker's dilemma.** From a game-theoretic perspective, the party attempting
suppression faces an asymmetric problem: they must succeed at suppression across
all channels simultaneously, while the person sharing the information only needs
to find one channel that works. The internet has made the number of potential
channels effectively unlimited.

**Legal amplification.** Court cases create public records. Serving a
takedown notice creates a paper trail. Demanding removal from a major platform
generates coverage in news articles about the demand. Each step in the legal
process that is intended to suppress is also a step that creates documentary
evidence of the suppression attempt that can itself be shared.

**The Streisand Effect and authority.** When a powerful party suppresses
information that reflects badly on them, the suppression adds an additional
layer of story: not just "this bad thing happened" but "they tried to hide it."
The second story is often more damaging than the first would have been.

## History

**2003.** The Streisand aerial photograph lawsuit. The lawsuit is dismissed;
Streisand is ordered to pay Adelman's legal fees of $177,107.

**2005.** Mike Masnick of Techdirt coins the term "Streisand Effect" in a blog
post about a different case (a singer trying to suppress a critical website).

**2007.** The AACS encryption key controversy: when the DVD Copy Control
Association issued DMCA takedowns against websites publishing a cryptographic
key, the key spread rapidly across the internet, including onto Wikipedia, in
what became a celebrated example of the effect.

**2010s.** UK superinjunctions — court orders preventing any reporting of the
injunction's existence — are routinely undermined by social media disclosures
and parliamentary speeches, which are protected by privilege.

**2020s.** The effect is now well-understood by communications professionals
and regularly cited as a reason not to issue public legal threats. Many PR
crises begin not with the original event but with an attempt to control coverage
of it.

## Why It Matters

**Power and information.** The Streisand Effect is a practical demonstration
that controlling information is fundamentally different in a networked world than
it was in a broadcast one. Power over information once required controlling
production and distribution. On the internet, neither is possible.

**Institutional communication.** Public figures and organisations that
understand the effect will choose responses other than suppression when
something embarrassing becomes public. Silence, correction, or getting ahead of
the story are all more effective than legal action that produces news coverage.

**Transparency as strategy.** The effect creates a counterintuitive incentive
toward openness: organisations with nothing to hide (or that behave as if they
have nothing to hide) are immune to it. The vulnerability is created by the
attempt to suppress, not by the existence of the information.

## Modern Relevance

**Platform governance.** Content moderation decisions that become public generate
coverage of the content being moderated. Banning a user or removing a video is
a public act that gets reported. Platforms increasingly face a version of the
Streisand Effect: the decision to moderate can amplify the thing being moderated.

**Corporate reputation management.** Legal threats against journalists,
reviewers, or critics reliably generate more coverage of the original criticism
than the criticism itself would have. Communications professionals now routinely
advise against them.

**Whistleblowing.** Aggressive suppression of whistleblower disclosures tends to
amplify them. The suppression becomes evidence of what was worth suppressing.
Edward Snowden's disclosures, and the government response to them, are a
large-scale instance of the dynamic.

**Misinformation.** Debunking misinformation by widely repeating it can amplify
it — a variant of the effect where the content is false and the amplifier is
fact-checking. The communication research on this ("the backfire effect") is
more contested, but the practical problem of whether to correct misinformation
by repeating it is real.

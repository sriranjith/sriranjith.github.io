## Discover

Here is a rule so obvious it feels like arithmetic: when something gets more
expensive, people buy less of it.

Petrol goes up, people drive less. Coffee goes up, people switch to tea. This is
the first thing anyone learns about prices, and it is right almost all of the
time.

Now picture a family with very little money in a very poor village. Almost
everything they eat is rice. Once a week, if it has been a good week, there is a
small piece of meat.

The price of rice goes up by half.

They still have to eat. Rice is still the cheapest way to fill a stomach that
exists for them — nothing else comes close. And now that rice costs more, the
weekly meat has become an impossible luxury. So they give up the meat.

But giving up the meat means giving up its calories. Those calories have to come
from somewhere.

They come from rice.

**The price of rice went up, and this family bought more rice.** Not out of
confusion, not out of panic, not because they misread the label. Because it was
the only sensible thing left to do.

## Understand

A good like this — one whose demand rises when its price rises — is called a
**Giffen good**. It is the textbook exception to the law of demand, and it has
been in economics textbooks for more than a century.

The odd thing about it is that until 2008, nobody had ever convincingly found
one.

### Two things happen when a price rises

Whenever the price of something changes, the change does two separate jobs at
once. Pulling them apart is the whole explanation.

**The first job: it changes what is cheap relative to what.** Rice has become
dearer compared with everything else, so at the margin rice looks less
attractive. This effect always pushes in the same direction — a higher price
always makes you want to lean away from that good. Economists call it the
**substitution effect**, and it is the reason demand curves normally slope
downward.

**The second job: it changes how well off you are.** If a large part of your
spending goes on rice, then a rise in the rice price is a cut in your real
income. You can afford less of everything. Economists call this the **income
effect**.

For most goods the income effect reinforces the first. You are poorer, so you buy
less rice, on top of leaning away from rice. Both effects point down, and demand
falls.

### The one case where they fight

Now suppose that as people get *richer* they buy *less* of the good. That sounds
strange until you name examples: bus tickets, instant noodles, own-brand
margarine, the cheapest cut of meat. Get a pay rise and you buy fewer of these,
not more, because you upgrade. Economists call such things **inferior goods**.

For an inferior good, the income effect runs backwards. A price rise makes you
poorer, and being poorer makes you buy *more* of it.

Now the two effects are pulling in opposite directions:

- Substitution effect: buy **less** rice, because rice is relatively dearer.
- Income effect: buy **more** rice, because you are now poorer and rice is what
  poor people eat.

Normally the substitution effect wins comfortably. A Giffen good is what you get
in the rare case where the income effect wins.

### Why the case is so rare

For the income effect to overwhelm the substitution effect, three things have to
be true at once, and they pull against each other.

1. **The good must be strongly inferior.** People must actively move away from it
   as they get richer.
2. **It must eat a large share of the budget.** A price rise in something you
   spend 1% of your income on barely dents your real income. To generate a big
   income effect the good needs to be a big line in the household accounts —
   which in practice means a staple food for a very poor household.
3. **There must be nothing much to substitute towards.** If a cheaper source of
   calories existed, the household would switch to it and the substitution effect
   would win.

The tension is that conditions 1 and 3 are close to contradictory. A good that
takes up most of your budget is usually a good you cannot easily do without,
which is usually a good you keep buying as you get richer — a *normal* good, not
an inferior one. Finding something that is simultaneously the bulk of a budget
and strongly inferior is genuinely hard, and that is why real Giffen goods are so
scarce.

There is also a ceiling. If the price rises far enough, the household runs out of
luxuries to give up. Once the weekly meat is gone there is nothing left to
sacrifice, and further price rises simply mean less rice. **The Giffen behaviour
appears in a window of poverty and disappears on both sides of it** — the
comfortably-off never had the budget share, and the desperately poor have already
cut everything else.

## Examples

**A household in arithmetic.** Take a family with 30 yuan a week for food that
needs 14,000 calories a week to get by. It has two options:

| Food | Price | Calories per kg |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rice | ¥2 per kg | 3,600 |
| Meat | ¥20 per kg | 2,500 |

Rice buys 1,800 calories per yuan; meat buys 125. Rice is by far the cheaper way
to survive, and meat is what the family actually enjoys. So the family buys as
much meat as it can while still hitting 14,000 calories on its 30 yuan.

Work it through and it buys **3.06 kg of rice and 1.19 kg of meat**, spending
¥6.12 on rice and ¥23.88 on meat. Calories: 11,015 from rice, 2,985 from meat.
Exactly 14,000.

Now the price of rice rises 50%, to ¥3 per kg. Redo the arithmetic under the same
rules and the family buys **3.18 kg of rice and 1.02 kg of meat**.

Rice cost half as much again, and the family bought 3.9% *more* of it. Meat fell
by 14.3%. The reason is visible in the numbers: the family had to drop 0.17 kg of
meat, which was carrying 425 calories, and the only place to replace them was
rice.

**Push the price further and watch it turn around:**

| Rice price | Rice bought | Meat bought | Calories still reachable? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ¥2 / kg | 3.06 kg | 1.19 kg | yes |
| ¥3 / kg | 3.18 kg | 1.02 kg | yes |
| ¥5 / kg | 3.45 kg | 0.64 kg | yes |
| ¥7 / kg | 3.76 kg | 0.18 kg | yes |
| ¥7.71 / kg | 3.89 kg | 0.00 kg | only just |
| ¥9 / kg | 3.33 kg | 0.00 kg | **no** |

Rice consumption climbs all the way to ¥7.71 per kg. At that price the last gram
of meat is gone and the family is spending its entire budget on rice just to
reach 14,000 calories. Past that point there is nothing left to give up, the
household simply buys all the rice it can afford, and consumption falls with
price like any ordinary good. The demand curve slopes *up* over one stretch and
*down* on either side of it.

This is a deliberately stark model — a hard calorie floor, exactly two foods —
but the shape it produces is the shape Robert Jensen and Nolan Miller found in
the field.

**The field experiment.** Jensen and Miller (2008) randomly assigned vouchers
that subsidised the price of the household staple to poor households in two
Chinese provinces: rice in Hunan, wheat in Gansu. Because the subsidy was
randomised, they could measure how consumption responded to a price change
cleanly, without the usual worry that price and demand move together for some
third reason.

For poor households in Hunan, subsidising rice — that is, making rice cheaper —
**reduced** rice consumption. Removing the subsidy raised it back. That is Giffen
behaviour, established by experiment rather than inferred from a demand curve.

Two details matter for honesty about the result. First, the evidence was strong
for rice in Hunan and considerably weaker for wheat in Gansu, where the staple's
budget share and the availability of alternatives differed. Second, and exactly
as the theory predicts, the effect was concentrated in the middle of the poverty
range. The very poorest households showed no Giffen behaviour, because they had
no meat left to cut.

## Explore

### The attribution is a mess

Alfred Marshall introduced the phenomenon in the third edition of his *Principles
of Economics* (1895):

> There are however some exceptions. For instance, as Mr Giffen has pointed out,
> a rise in the price of bread makes so large a drain on the resources of the
> poorer labouring families and raises so much the marginal utility of money to
> them, that they are forced to curtail their consumption of meat and the more
> expensive farinaceous foods: and, bread being still the cheapest food which
> they can get and will take, they consume more, and not less of it.

That is the whole of it — one paragraph, no citation, no data. Sir Robert Giffen
was a distinguished statistician: head of the statistical department of the Board
of Trade, president of the Royal Statistical Society, knighted the same year
Marshall's third edition appeared. He was exactly the kind of authority whose
name would settle a question. But no one has found the passage.

George Stigler went looking in 1947, in "Notes on the History of the Giffen
Paradox", and reported that nothing in Giffen's published writings states the
proposition Marshall credits to him. Giffen wrote a great deal about working-class
budgets and about the price of bread, and some of it gestures in the vicinity, but
the specific claim is not there. The most likely explanation is that Marshall was
reporting something Giffen said in conversation or in a lecture, which was normal
practice at the time and leaves no record.

The honest position: **the phenomenon is named after a man who may never have
asserted it in print.** This is worth stating plainly, because textbooks have
repeated the attribution for over a century without the qualification.

### The Irish potato story is probably false

The most commonly repeated illustration — that during the Irish famine of the
1840s the rising price of potatoes caused the poor to eat more potatoes — appears
to be wrong on its own terms.

Gerald Dwyer and Cotton Lindsay laid out the case in 1984. The famine was caused
by potato blight, which destroyed the crop: there were dramatically *fewer*
potatoes to eat, so consumption fell for reasons that have nothing to do with
demand curves. Sherwin Rosen reached a similar conclusion in 1999. Whatever the
Irish famine demonstrates, it is not a Giffen good.

The story survives in teaching because it is memorable, which is a good reason to
be suspicious of memorable economic anecdotes generally.

### Was it ever more than a theoretical curiosity?

For most of the twentieth century, serious economists split into three camps, and
the split is worth understanding because it is a general one about how theory and
evidence interact.

**The possibility is fine, the example is missing.** The majority view. Giffen
goods are perfectly consistent with utility maximisation — the Slutsky equation
permits them and no axiom rules them out — so they belong in the theory, and the
absence of examples is an empirical fact about the world rather than a defect in
the analysis.

**The absence is itself informative.** A minority, including some who took the
"as if" methodology seriously, argued that a phenomenon with no confirmed
instance in a century of looking should be dropped from teaching, whatever its
logical status. Textbook space is scarce and students overweight what they are
shown.

**The search was badly designed.** A third view, which Jensen and Miller
essentially vindicated, was that nobody had looked in the right place with the
right method. Giffen behaviour requires very poor households, a staple with a
huge budget share, a real fallback good to give up, and a price change you can
attribute cleanly. Historical price series from rich countries satisfy none of
those conditions.

### What was found before 2008

Two earlier results deserve credit, and neither is a field observation.

Raymond Battalio, John Kagel and Carl Kogut demonstrated a Giffen good in the
laboratory in 1991, using rats choosing between quinine-flavoured water and root
beer under a budget of lever presses. The design was clean and the upward-sloping
demand was real. What it establishes is that the mechanism works in a system with
the right structure — not that human markets contain such goods.

Various historical studies claimed to detect upward-sloping demand in price
series for bread, potatoes and rice. These were consistently vulnerable to the
same objection: in observational data, price and quantity are jointly determined,
and a supply shock that raises price while reducing quantity looks nothing like a
demand curve. Identification, not economics, was the binding constraint. This is
precisely why the randomised subsidy in Jensen and Miller's design was decisive.

## History

**1890.** Alfred Marshall published the first edition of *Principles of
Economics*. It contains no Giffen passage.

**1895.** The third edition adds the paragraph quoted above. Marshall attributes
the observation to Robert Giffen without a citation. Later editions kept it, and
it entered the standard curriculum through Marshall's near-total dominance of
English-language economics teaching for the next thirty years.

**1915.** Eugen Slutsky, working in Kyiv, published the equation that separates
the substitution and income effects in "Sulla teoria del bilancio del
consumatore" in an Italian journal. The paper was ignored, partly because of the
war and partly because of where it appeared.

**1934.** John Hicks and R. G. D. Allen rediscovered and popularised the
decomposition in "A Reconsideration of the Theory of Value". From this point the
Giffen good ceased to be a puzzle and became a corner of a general theory: an
exception you can locate exactly, on a specific sign condition.

**1947.** George Stigler published "Notes on the History of the Giffen Paradox"
in the *Journal of Political Economy*, documenting that the attribution to Giffen
cannot be traced to Giffen.

**1984 and 1999.** Dwyer and Lindsay, and later Rosen, dismantled the Irish
potato illustration.

**1991.** Battalio, Kagel and Kogut published the laboratory demonstration in the
*American Economic Review*.

**2008.** Robert Jensen and Nolan Miller published "Giffen Behavior and
Subsistence Consumption" in the *American Economic Review*, reporting the
randomised subsidy experiments in Hunan and Gansu. It remains the standard
citation for the claim that Giffen goods exist.

## Why It Matters

**It is the cleanest case of a "law" that is really a strong tendency.** The law
of demand is not a theorem of consumer theory. It follows from utility
maximisation only with an extra assumption — that the good is not too inferior
and not too large a share of spending. The Giffen good marks exactly where the
assumption bites. Knowing that boundary is more useful than believing the law is
unconditional.

**It sharpened how economists think about welfare for the poor.** The mechanism
is not a curiosity about demand curves; it is a statement about how households
near subsistence actually make decisions. Jensen and Miller's practical point was
this: subsidising a staple can *reduce* consumption of that staple among the poor,
because the subsidy makes them richer and they spend the gain on better food.
Whether that is a good or a bad outcome depends on what the programme was for. If
the goal is calories, the subsidy may be self-defeating; if the goal is nutrition,
it may be working exactly as it should. Either way, designing food policy without
the income effect in view will produce surprises.

**It is a case study in the cost of a missing counterexample.** For 113 years the
profession taught a phenomenon with no confirmed instance. That is defensible —
the theory permitted it and the theory was right — but it also meant a century of
textbook diagrams whose empirical content was unknown. The resolution came from a
change in method: a randomised price change in a population selected because
theory said the effect should be there. The lesson generalises well beyond Giffen
goods.

**It is why identification matters.** Every pre-2008 attempt failed for the same
reason: observational price and quantity data cannot distinguish a demand curve
from a supply shock. That the definitive answer came from an experiment rather
than from better econometrics on old data is part of why the credibility
revolution reshaped empirical economics.

## Modern Relevance

**Food policy and subsistence.** Staple price shocks in low-income countries —
the 2007–08 and 2010–11 food price spikes among them — raise exactly this
question: does a rise in the price of the staple reduce staple consumption, or
increase it while destroying dietary diversity? The measured answer determines
whether a price shock shows up as a calorie crisis or a nutrition crisis, and
those call for different responses.

**Cash transfers versus in-kind subsidies.** The Giffen mechanism is one reason
the two are not interchangeable. An in-kind subsidy changes relative prices and
therefore triggers substitution; a cash transfer changes income only. Where the
income effect on a staple is strongly negative, the two policies move staple
consumption in opposite directions.

**Behavioural pricing generally.** Upward-sloping demand appears in modern markets
for reasons that are *not* Giffen effects and should not be confused with them:
Veblen goods, where price signals status; quality signalling, where price is read
as evidence of quality in the absence of other information; and speculative
assets, where a rising price is read as a forecast of further rises. All three
work by changing what the price *means* to the buyer. A Giffen good involves no
such reinterpretation. Preferences are fixed, the good is understood perfectly,
and the arithmetic of the budget does all the work. Keeping these apart is the
main practical use of the concept today.

## Deep Dive

### The Slutsky decomposition

Let the consumer choose a bundle $x \in \mathbb{R}^n_+$ to maximise $u(x)$
subject to $p \cdot x \le m$. Write $x_i(p,m)$ for the Marshallian demand and
$h_i(p,u)$ for the Hicksian (compensated) demand. The **Slutsky equation** is

$$\frac{\partial x_i(p,m)}{\partial p_i} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial h_i(p,u)}{\partial p_i}}_{\text{substitution effect}} \;-\; \underbrace{x_i(p,m)\,\frac{\partial x_i(p,m)}{\partial m}}_{\text{income effect}}$$

The substitution term is the own-price derivative of compensated demand. Because
the Slutsky matrix is negative semi-definite — a consequence of the weak axiom of
revealed preference, not of any further assumption — its diagonal entries satisfy

$$\frac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_i} \le 0$$

So the substitution effect can never be positive. **Every Giffen good must
therefore get its upward slope entirely from the income term**, which requires

$$x_i \frac{\partial x_i}{\partial m} < \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_i} \le 0$$

and in particular $\partial x_i / \partial m < 0$: the good is inferior. Inferior
is necessary, not sufficient.

### The elasticity form

Divide through by $x_i / p_i$ and let $s_i = p_i x_i / m$ be the budget share.
Writing $\varepsilon_{ii}$ for the uncompensated own-price elasticity,
$\varepsilon^h_{ii}$ for the compensated one, and $\eta_i$ for the income
elasticity:

$$\varepsilon_{ii} = \varepsilon^h_{ii} - s_i \eta_i$$

A Giffen good is one with $\varepsilon_{ii} > 0$, which requires

$$s_i\,\eta_i < \varepsilon^h_{ii} \le 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s_i \, |\eta_i| > |\varepsilon^h_{ii}| \;\text{ with }\; \eta_i < 0$$

This makes the empirical requirement precise and shows why the search was so
hard. The compensated elasticity for a staple food is small in magnitude but not
zero — say $|\varepsilon^h_{ii}| \approx 0.1$ to $0.3$. So Giffen behaviour needs
the product of budget share and the magnitude of a *negative* income elasticity
to exceed that. With a budget share of 0.5, an income elasticity of about $-0.3$
or steeper would do it. Both numbers are extreme, and they must occur together.

### Why the effect vanishes at both ends

Model the household as choosing rice $r$ and a preferred fallback food $f$
subject to a budget $p_r r + p_f f \le m$ and a subsistence calorie constraint
$c_r r + c_f f \ge \bar{c}$, with rice the cheaper source of calories
($c_r/p_r > c_f/p_f$), maximising consumption of the preferred food.

When both constraints bind, the solution is

$$r = \frac{m c_f - p_f \bar{c}}{p_r c_f - p_f c_r}, \qquad f = \frac{p_r \bar c - c_r m}{p_r c_f - p_f c_r}$$

Differentiating $r$ with respect to $p_r$ over the region where $f > 0$ gives a
positive derivative: rice demand rises with the rice price. The upward slope is
not an approximation — it is exact in this model, and it is driven entirely by
the calorie constraint forcing a substitution back into rice as the fallback is
surrendered.

The behaviour terminates when $f$ hits zero, at the price
$p_r^{*} = c_r m / \bar c$. Above $p_r^{*}$ the calorie constraint can no longer
be met, the household spends everything on rice, and $r = m/p_r$ falls
hyperbolically. In the worked example, $p_r^{*} = 3600 \times 30 / 14000 \approx 7.71$
yuan per kg, which is where the table above turns over.

At the other end, as $m$ grows the budget constraint stops binding, the calorie
floor is met with room to spare, and rice demand behaves normally. Jensen and
Miller emphasised this non-monotonicity in poverty as a testable prediction of
the theory, and it is the pattern they found: Giffen behaviour among the poor,
but not the poorest.

### Aggregation

A final constraint worth stating: not everything can be a Giffen good at once.
Adding up the budget constraint gives the Engel aggregation condition
$\sum_i s_i \eta_i = 1$, so the budget-share-weighted income elasticities must
average to one and cannot all be negative. Giffen goods are necessarily a small
part of any consumer's spending in the aggregate, however large a share the
single good takes.

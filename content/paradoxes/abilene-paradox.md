## Discover

It is a July afternoon in Coleman, Texas, and it is 104 degrees outside.

Four people are on the porch: Jerry, his wife, and her parents. There is an
electric fan, cold lemonade, and a game of dominoes on the table. Nobody is
doing anything, and nobody minds.

Then the father-in-law says: "Tell you what. Why don't we drive to Abilene and
get dinner at the cafeteria?"

Abilene is fifty-three miles away. The car has no air conditioning. Jerry
thinks this is a terrible idea. But before he can say so, his wife says, "Sounds
like a great idea." So Jerry says, "Sounds good to me. I just hope your mother
wants to go." And the mother-in-law says, "Of course I want to go. I haven't
been to Abilene in a long time."

They drive. The heat is brutal, the dust blows in through the windows, and by
the time they arrive everyone is sticky and cross. The cafeteria food is bad.
They drive fifty-three miles back and collapse in front of the fan, exhausted,
four hours older.

Somebody says, insincerely, that it was a great trip.

And then the mother-in-law says no, actually, she would rather have stayed home
— she only went because the other three were so enthusiastic. Jerry's wife says
she never wanted to go either; she only agreed because everyone else seemed
keen. Jerry admits he only went along to keep the others happy. Everyone turns
to the father-in-law, the man whose idea it was.

He says he never wanted to go to Abilene at all. He thought the other three
looked bored, and he was afraid they weren't enjoying themselves.

Four people, all agreeing, had just driven a hundred and six miles across a
Texas heatwave to eat food they didn't want, in a town none of them wished to
visit — and every single one of them had been against it the whole time.

## Understand

Nothing went wrong with anybody's thinking. Each of the four made a perfectly
sensible decision given what they believed. The trouble is that what each of
them believed about the other three was false.

That is the paradox: **a group can take an action that every single member
privately opposes, without anyone lying, bullying, or making a mistake in
reasoning.**

### How four "yes" votes appear from four "no" opinions

Look closely at what actually got said on that porch. Nobody said "I want to go
to Abilene." What they said was:

- "Sounds like a great idea." — meaning *you seem to want this*.
- "Sounds good to me. I just hope your mother wants to go." — meaning *I'm
  agreeing to what I think you two want*.
- "Of course I want to go." — meaning *I don't want to be the one who spoils it*.

Every reply was a guess about somebody else's preference, dressed up as a
preference of one's own. Once the first guess was made, the second person was
now reading a fake signal, and their reply produced another fake signal for the
third. The false picture built itself in about fifteen seconds.

The technical name for the resulting state is **pluralistic ignorance**:
everybody privately holds one view, everybody wrongly believes almost everybody
else holds the opposite view, and so everybody keeps quiet. It is the situation
where the majority is silent because each member of it thinks they are the
minority.

### Why nobody speaks up

Harvey identified three reasons, and they are worth naming because they are
extremely ordinary.

**Action anxiety.** Speaking up is an action, and actions feel risky in a way
that silence does not. Saying nothing has no visible author. Saying "I don't
want to go" makes you the person who said it.

**Negative fantasies.** Before you object, you imagine what will happen if you
do — the awkward pause, the disappointed faces, being thought difficult. These
imagined consequences are vivid, immediate and almost always far worse than
what actually happens. Nobody imagines the far more likely outcome: three people
saying, "Oh, thank God, neither did I."

**Fear of separation.** Underneath both is something older and simpler: the fear
of being cut off from the group. Being the lone dissenter feels like standing
outside the circle. People will accept a genuinely bad afternoon rather than
risk that, and they do not usually notice they are making the trade.

Add to this a fourth thing that costs nothing and does enormous damage:
**silence gets read as agreement.** Someone who says nothing is counted as a
yes. So the quieter the room, the more unanimous it looks, and the more
unanimous it looks the harder it is to be the one who breaks it.

### This is not groupthink

The two are constantly confused, and keeping them apart is the whole point of
the idea.

**Groupthink**, described by Irving Janis in 1972, is what happens when a group
develops a real shared position and then defends it — dissent is suppressed,
doubts are self-censored, and members who genuinely disagree are pulled into
line with a view that a majority actually holds.

The Abilene paradox has no such view. **There is no majority to conform to.**
Everybody already agrees — they agree that this is a bad idea — and the
agreement is invisible to all of them. Harvey's title is exact: this is a
failure to *manage agreement*. The problem is not that people disagree and can't
say so. It is that people agree and don't know it.

The practical difference matters. If your problem is groupthink, the fix is to
protect dissent: appoint a devil's advocate, invite outside criticism, get the
leader to speak last. If your problem is Abilene, those fixes miss entirely,
because there is no dissenting minority to protect. You need the opposite move:
you need to find out what everyone actually thinks.

### The remedy

Harvey's prescription is blunt and slightly uncomfortable, which is why it
works: **confront the agreement directly.**

Instead of asking "does anyone object?" — a question whose honest answer
requires volunteering to be the difficult one — you go round the room and
require each person to state their own private preference before any discussion
of what the group should do. Not their assessment of the plan. What they
personally want, and what they think will happen.

That single change removes the guessing. Nobody has to infer anyone's view from
a polite noise, because everyone's view has been said out loud. And it removes
the cost of dissent, because dissenting is no longer an act of courage — it is
just answering the question you were asked, like everyone else.

Harvey's own recommendation for a group already suspecting it is on the road to
Abilene is to say so: "I want to raise something. I think we may all be going
along with a decision none of us wants. Let's each say where we actually stand."
This is awkward exactly once. It saves the drive every time.

## Examples

**The porch, laid out.** What each person said, and what each of them meant.

| Person | What they said | What they actually wanted | Why they said it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Father-in-law | "Why don't we drive to Abilene?" | To stay on the porch | Thought the others were bored |
| Jerry's wife | "Sounds like a great idea" | To stay on the porch | Her father had suggested it |
| Jerry | "Sounds good to me" | To stay on the porch | Two people had now agreed |
| Mother-in-law | "Of course I want to go" | To stay on the porch | Didn't want to be the spoiler |

Four unanimous yeses. Four private noes. Zero liars.

**Harvey's six stages.** In the 1974 article he set the pattern out as a
sequence, which is useful because it shows the damage does not stop at the bad
decision.

1. Members privately agree about what the situation is.
2. Members privately agree about what should be done.
3. Members fail to say either of these things out loud, and instead give each
   other misleading signals.
4. On the strength of that false information, the group takes an action
   contrary to what its members want.
5. Members are angry and frustrated at the result, and — crucially — blame each
   other, the leader, or another department for it.
6. Because the actual cause is never named, the cycle repeats, usually harder.

Stage five is the one people underestimate. The trip to Abilene does not end
with a bad meal; it ends with four people quietly resentful of each other over
something none of them wanted. In an organisation this is where the real cost
lands: a project nobody believed in, followed by months of recrimination aimed
at everybody except the mechanism that produced it.

**Three everyday drives to Abilene.**

| Situation | The fake signal | The real state of opinion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A team keeps a weekly meeting nobody finds useful | Everybody attends, so it must be valued | Everyone assumes the others need it |
| A family holds a large annual gathering that exhausts them all | Nobody has ever said they'd rather not | Each keeps it going for the others' sake |
| A committee approves a reorganisation it privately doubts | Silence in the room after "any concerns?" | Each member assumes their doubts are unique |

**The distinguishing question.** If you want to know which failure you are
looking at, ask what would happen if everybody's private view were written on a
card and read aloud. Under groupthink you would find a genuine majority and a
suppressed minority. Under the Abilene paradox you would find that the cards all
say the same thing, and it is not what the group decided.

## Explore

**Is it a paradox at all?** It sits in the same family as the Prisoner's Dilemma
— individually reasonable choices producing a collectively terrible outcome —
but the mechanism is different in an important way, and the difference is where
the interest lies. In the Prisoner's Dilemma, the incentives really are opposed;
each prisoner gains from betraying. Fix the information and the trap remains. In
Abilene, nothing whatsoever is opposed. Everyone wants the same thing, and
communication alone would resolve it completely. The whole disaster is made of
misinformation that the participants generated themselves, for free, out of
politeness.

**How solid is the evidence?** Harvey's article is a piece of essayistic
management writing built on a family anecdote and two organisational cases, not
an experiment, and it should be read as one. What has held up is not Harvey's
particular framework but the underlying phenomenon, which was described in
social psychology well before him. Floyd Allport introduced pluralistic
ignorance in the 1920s; Daniel Katz and Floyd Allport documented it in 1931 among
students who each privately disapproved of a campus norm they all believed the
others endorsed. Later work found the same pattern in college drinking, where
students substantially overestimate how comfortable their peers are with heavy
drinking, and in surveys of racial attitudes in the mid-twentieth-century United
States, where private opinion had shifted considerably ahead of what people
believed their neighbours thought. Harvey's contribution was to give the
organisational version a name and a story that people remember.

**Pluralistic ignorance or preference falsification?** These are two different
diagnoses and the distinction changes what you should do. In pluralistic
ignorance, people are simply *mistaken* about what others think, and telling
them the truth fixes it. In preference falsification — the economist Timur
Kuran's term, from his 1995 book *Private Truths, Public Lies* — people
misrepresent their views because there is a real social cost to honesty, and
telling them the truth changes nothing while the cost remains. Harvey's porch is
clearly the first kind: the cost was imagined. Many organisational cases are the
second kind, where the person who objects to the executive's pet project pays a
genuine price. Calling a preference-falsification problem "an Abilene paradox"
is comforting and useless, because it implies the fix is a candid conversation
when in fact the fix is changing what happens to people who speak.

**How would you ever know?** The paradox is defined by private states that, by
construction, nobody expressed at the time. That makes it awkward to
demonstrate. Every account of it, including Harvey's, is reconstructed after the
fact from people who have just discovered they wasted an afternoon — and people
in that position have an obvious motive to remember themselves as having been
sceptical all along. Anonymous polling before the decision is the only way to
catch it live, which is part of why anonymous pre-voting is both a good remedy
and the main source of decent evidence that the phenomenon is real.

**The label is over-applied.** Not every bad group decision is a trip to
Abilene. The Challenger launch decision of 1986 is often cited as an example and
mostly is not one: Thiokol's engineers, Roger Boisjoly among them, objected
clearly and in writing, and were overruled. That is a failure to act on stated
dissent, which is a different and in some ways worse problem. The Abilene test is
strict — the objection has to be *unspoken by everyone*. If somebody said it out
loud and was ignored, you are looking at something else.

## History

**July, in the 1970s, Coleman, Texas.** Jerry B. Harvey, then a professor of
management science at George Washington University, was visiting his wife's
parents. The afternoon happened as described: the porch, the fan, the dominoes,
the father-in-law's suggestion, the fifty-three miles each way in an
unairconditioned Buick, the disappointing cafeteria, and the conversation
afterwards in which the whole thing came apart. Harvey has always presented it
as a true account of his own family, and it is by some distance the most
effective piece of writing in the management literature.

**1974.** Harvey published "The Abilene Paradox: The Management of Agreement" in
*Organizational Dynamics*, volume 3, issue 1, pages 63–80. The article opens
with the drive, then works through two organisational cases and sets out the six
symptoms and his diagnosis — action anxiety, negative fantasies, and fear of
separation from the group. The central claim is stated flatly: organisations
fail at least as often from an inability to manage agreement as from an
inability to manage conflict, and the second failure gets all the attention.

**The context it landed in.** Irving Janis had published *Victims of Groupthink*
two years earlier, in 1972, and the idea that groups make bad decisions was
suddenly fashionable. Harvey's article is in part an argument against being
absorbed into that fashion. His insistence that the Abilene paradox is *not*
groupthink is the reason the entry is worth having separately, and it is also
the reason the two have been conflated ever since.

**1984 onwards.** A training film based on the story, produced for corporate
audiences, carried the idea far beyond people who read journals; it became one of
the most widely used management training films of its era and was remade in the
1990s. "Are we on the road to Abilene?" entered boardroom vocabulary as a way of
raising the question without accusing anybody.

**1988.** Harvey collected and extended the argument in *The Abilene Paradox and
Other Meditations on Management*, pushing it into harder territory: organisational
collusion, the treatment of whistleblowers, and what he considered the ethical
weight of going along with something you believe to be wrong. The later essays
are less comfortable than the original article, and deliberately so — Harvey's
view was that the polite silence on the porch and the polite silence around
serious misconduct are the same behaviour at different stakes.

## Why It Matters

The lasting contribution is a distinction that changes what you do in a room.

Before Harvey, the standard diagnosis of a bad group decision was that dissent
had been suppressed, and the standard fix was to protect dissent. That fix is
correct — for the cases it fits. Harvey identified a large and previously
unnamed category where it does nothing at all, because there was no dissent to
protect. Everybody was already on the same side. What failed was not the group's
tolerance for disagreement but its ability to discover an agreement it already
had.

Once you have the distinction, you can see how much of ordinary organisational
machinery is built to solve the wrong one of the two problems. Devil's advocates,
red teams and "any objections?" all assume the risk is a majority steamrolling a
minority. None of them help a room where every person is privately hoping
somebody else will say it.

The second contribution is the reframing of silence. It is normal to treat a
quiet room as a settled room, and the quieter it is the more settled it seems.
The Abilene paradox says the opposite can be true: unanimity that arrives
quickly, with no friction and no questions, is not evidence of agreement. It is
evidence of nothing at all, because agreement and universal private objection
produce exactly the same sound.

There is also a small, useful piece of self-knowledge in it, which is probably
why the story has travelled so well. Most people, reading about the drive to
Abilene, recognise themselves immediately — not as the victim of a group that
went somewhere silly, but as one of the four who said "sounds good to me". The
cost of being the first to speak is consistently overestimated, and the relief
of everyone else in the room is consistently underestimated. Those two errors,
running in every head at once, are enough to move a group anywhere.

## Modern Relevance

**Meetings that approve things nobody believes in.** The commonest modern form
is a project sign-off where each attendee assumes the enthusiasm is real and
located elsewhere in the room — with the sponsor, or with the executive, or with
"the business". The tell is a decision that passes with no questions and is
privately grumbled about within the hour. Where the pattern is chronic, the cause
is usually structural rather than personal: the meeting is designed for
ratification, so the only available inputs are assent and awkwardness.

**Anonymous pre-voting.** The most direct remedy in current practice, and a
straight implementation of Harvey's prescription. Before any discussion, each
participant privately records their position — a number, a confidence level, a
would-you-fund-this — and the distribution is revealed all at once. Nobody is
identifiable, nobody has to go first, and the distribution frequently looks
nothing like the room. It is the same logic as blind bidding: you cannot anchor
on a signal you have not seen.

**Premortems.** Gary Klein's technique, popularised by Daniel Kahneman: before
committing, the group is told to imagine it is a year later and the project has
failed badly, and each person writes down why. This works on Abilene for a
reason worth noticing. It does not ask anyone to object — objecting is what
people will not do — it asks them to explain a failure that has been stipulated.
The social cost of being the difficult one is removed by the framing, and the
private doubts come out as an assignment rather than as dissent.

**"Disagree and commit."** Widely adopted in technology companies, and a genuine
partial fix, since it makes disagreement a normal contribution rather than an
act of obstruction and promises the objector that raising it will not stall the
work. Its failure mode is exactly the one to watch: in practice the phrase is
frequently deployed at the commit end, to close a discussion, rather than at the
disagree end, to open one. Used that way it becomes a faster road to Abilene
with better vocabulary.

**Remote and asynchronous work.** Written, asynchronous decision-making cuts both
ways here. A comment thread makes silence even cheaper and even more ambiguous
than silence in a room — a document with no comments looks endorsed. But
written-proposal cultures, where reviewers are required to record a position
rather than merely attend, remove the guessing that the paradox runs on. The
mechanism is the same as the anonymous poll: the failure mode is inferred
consent, so any process that makes each person state something is an
improvement.

**Safety-critical work.** Aviation crew resource management and the surgical
safety checklist both, in part, exist to break the assumption that a junior
person's silence means agreement. Requiring each team member to speak — name,
role, concerns — before an operation or a departure is not ceremony. It is the
recognition that a person who has said nothing has not been consulted.

## Discover

Hawthorne Works, Cicero, Illinois, 1924. Western Electric is running a factory
here that makes telephone equipment. Researchers from the National Research
Council want to understand how lighting affects worker productivity.

They increase the lighting. Productivity goes up.

They decrease the lighting back to its original level. Productivity goes up
again.

Every time the researchers change anything — the lighting, the break schedule,
the work hours, the temperature — productivity improves. Even when they change
things back, it improves. Nothing they try makes productivity worse.

The researchers eventually conclude that the changes themselves are not the
cause. The workers, aware they are being studied, are working harder because
someone is paying attention to them.

This became one of the most famous findings in industrial psychology — and one
of the most misunderstood.

## Understand

The Hawthorne Effect is the tendency for people to change their behaviour when
they know they are being observed. The name comes from the factory; the
phenomenon is broader.

The mechanism has several plausible components:

**Social motivation.** Being watched signals that someone cares about what you
are doing. Most people, most of the time, perform better when they believe
their performance matters to someone who is paying attention.

**Demand characteristics.** Participants in any study pick up cues about what
the researchers expect, and many tend to try to provide those responses — not
consciously, but as a result of wanting to be helpful or to seem capable.

**Self-consciousness and effort.** Observation makes people more aware of their
own actions, which can increase effort and care, at least temporarily.

**Novelty effects.** Any change to a routine produces a temporary boost in
attention and effort, which decays as the novelty fades.

### Why this is a problem for research

The Hawthorne Effect is a specific instance of **reactivity**: the phenomenon
that the act of measurement changes what is being measured. In physics, this
is sometimes unavoidable (observing a particle affects the particle). In social
science, it is pervasive: people who know they are in a study behave differently
from people who do not.

This is one of the main reasons for **randomised controlled trials** with
**blinding**: if neither the participant nor the researcher knows who received
the treatment, demand characteristics and observer effects are distributed
equally between treatment and control groups, cancelling out.

### The mythology and the reality

The original Hawthorne studies have been heavily scrutinised. When Steven Levitt
and John List went back to the original data in 2011, they found that the
effects were much more modest than the mythology suggested — and that several
confounding variables (day of the week, weather, the fact that some manipulations
happened on Mondays after the weekend) had been ignored. The "Hawthorne Effect"
as commonly described may be substantially an artefact of how the original
findings were reported and retold.

The underlying phenomenon — that observation changes behaviour — is, however,
real and replicated in many settings. The specific claim that workers at Hawthorne
became more productive because they were being watched is more contested than the
general claim that being watched changes behaviour.

## Examples

| Context | Hawthorne manifestation |
| --- | --- |
| Medical drug trials | Placebo group often improves significantly — partly because of the attention of being in a study |
| Performance management | Employees being evaluated often perform better during evaluation periods |
| Educational assessment | Students perform differently on tests they know are being observed |
| Traffic safety | Accident rates often fall when cameras are installed, even before speed enforcement begins |
| Healthcare | Hand-washing compliance improves dramatically when staff know they are being monitored |

## Explore

**The placebo effect.** The placebo effect is a close relative: receiving
treatment — even an inert one — changes how people feel and behave. The attention
and care of being treated is itself therapeutic, independent of the specific
treatment. This is why placebo-controlled trials are essential in medicine: the
act of giving someone a pill does something, regardless of what is in the pill.

**Hawthorne vs. observer effects in physics.** The Hawthorne Effect is sometimes
called the "observer effect" in social science, but it should not be confused
with the quantum mechanical observer effect. In quantum mechanics, observation
physically disturbs the system; in social science, observation changes the
social and psychological context. The mechanisms are completely different.

**Ecological validity.** The Hawthorne Effect is one reason laboratory psychology
findings sometimes fail to replicate in the real world. In the lab, people know
they are being observed. In the real world, they may not. Behaviour in a lab may
not predict behaviour in natural settings.

**Deception in research.** Because the Hawthorne Effect undermines research on
humans, social psychology has a history of using deception — not telling
participants what is actually being studied. This raises ethical questions that
the field is still working through.

## History

**1924–1932.** The Hawthorne studies are conducted by various researchers
including Elton Mayo, Fritz Roethlisberger, and William Dickson. The full
picture is complex: multiple different studies, different factories, different
researchers over nearly a decade.

**1939.** Roethlisberger and Dickson publish *Management and the Worker*, the
main account of the studies. The social interpretation — that attention and
relationships matter more than physical conditions — becomes influential in
management theory.

**1958.** Henry Landsberger coins the term "Hawthorne Effect" while reanalysing
the original data.

**1966.** The Hawthorne Effect becomes a standard item in psychology textbooks
and research methods courses.

**2011.** Levitt and List re-examine the original raw data, finding that the
effects were more modest and more confounded than the standard account. The
mythology of the Hawthorne Effect is substantially revised in academic literature,
though it remains widely taught as originally described.

## Why It Matters

**Research design.** Any study of human behaviour must account for the
possibility that study participants are behaving differently because they are
being studied. Control groups, blinding, and naturalistic designs are responses
to this problem.

**Management.** The social interpretation of the Hawthorne studies — that
workers respond to being cared about, not just to specific incentives — had a
large influence on management theory in the mid-twentieth century. Whether or
not the original data support it, the claim that attention and recognition
affect performance is broadly supported by subsequent research.

**Technology and surveillance.** Systems that monitor employee performance —
email surveillance, keylogging, productivity tracking — may improve measured
performance without improving actual performance. The Hawthorne Effect predicts
that monitored behaviour and unmonitored behaviour will diverge, and that
measurement artefacts will contaminate the data.

## Modern Relevance

**Remote work monitoring.** Software that tracks keystrokes, mouse movements,
and application usage to monitor remote workers generates a textbook Hawthorne
Effect: workers perform differently when they know the software is running. The
monitored data does not measure what it claims to measure.

**A/B testing.** Online experiments (A/B tests) typically use much larger
samples and more automated measurement than traditional studies, reducing but
not eliminating observer effects. Users who realise they are in an experiment
may behave differently.

**Clinical trials.** The Hawthorne Effect is part of the justification for
blinded trials: placebo groups often show substantial improvement simply because
they are receiving care and attention. Without blinding, it is impossible to
separate the specific treatment effect from the effect of being treated.

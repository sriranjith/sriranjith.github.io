## Discover

Imagine a club with exactly one rule:

**Everyone is welcome. We never turn anyone away.**

It is a good rule. It is generous, it is simple, and everyone likes it.

Then one day a group joins whose stated plan is to take over the club and start
turning people away.

Now the club has a problem it cannot solve with its own rule. If it lets them in
and lets them do it, the club stops welcoming everyone — the rule is gone. If it
turns them away to protect the rule, then it has turned someone away — the rule
is gone.

**The one rule cannot survive being applied to everyone, including the people
who want to abolish it.**

Karl Popper noticed this in 1945 and put it in a footnote. The footnote is now
one of the most quoted, and most misquoted, passages in political philosophy.

## Understand

The tension is real, but it is not a genuine contradiction. It comes from an
ambiguity in what "tolerance" is meant to be.

Read it as a **rule with no exceptions** — tolerate absolutely everything — and
the rule is self-undermining. It licenses its own destruction, and a rule that
does that cannot be a stable foundation for anything.

Read it instead as a **reciprocal arrangement** — a mutual agreement to settle
disagreements by argument rather than by force — and the tension dissolves.
Someone who refuses the arrangement is not being excluded from it; they have
declined to enter it. The rule was never "put up with anything"; it was "we
argue instead of fighting."

That is the standard resolution, and it is why this entry is classified as an
apparent contradiction rather than a real one.

What remains genuinely difficult is not the logic but the practice. If the
principle permits some limits, *which* limits, decided by *whom*, and how do you
stop that power being used against ordinary disagreement? This is where serious
disagreement actually lives, and no one has a clean answer.

### What Popper actually wrote

This matters, because the popular version is not what he said.

Popper's footnote makes three moves, and the middle one is almost always
dropped:

> I do not imply, for instance, that we should always suppress the utterance of
> intolerant philosophies; as long as we can counter them by rational argument
> and keep them in check by public opinion, suppression would be most unwise.

He then adds the condition under which the claim applies:

> But we should claim the right to suppress them if necessary even by force […]
> if they are not prepared to meet us on the level of rational argument, but
> begin by denouncing all argument.

So Popper's position is:

1. Unlimited toleration destroys toleration — the paradox.
2. **Therefore argument and public opinion are the normal remedy, and
   suppression would usually be unwise.**
3. The right to suppress is claimed only as a reserve, for those who have
   refused argument outright.

The version that circulates online usually keeps (1) and (3) and drops (2).
That inversion turns a cautious, argument-first position into a licence, which
is close to the opposite of what the footnote says.

## Explore

The interesting disagreements are not about whether the logical tension exists.
They are about what follows from it.

**The reciprocity reading.** Toleration is a bargain, not a gift. Its
protections extend to those who accept the bargain. This is the cleanest
resolution and the most widely held, and it is essentially Popper's own.
Its weakness is that "refuses to accept the bargain" is much harder to identify
in practice than in a footnote.

**Rawls's version.** In *A Theory of Justice* (§35), Rawls argues from a
different starting point and lands somewhere stricter than Popper. On his
account an intolerant group has no title to complain of intolerance, but it does
not follow that a just society should restrict them: it should do so only when
it sincerely and with reason believes that its own constitutional security is
genuinely in danger. Rawls sets a higher bar and states the trigger more
precisely.

**The free-speech objection.** The most serious criticism is not that the
paradox is unreal but that the criterion is unusable. Any authority empowered to
decide who has "refused argument" can apply that label to ordinary opponents,
and historically such powers have been used that way far more often than against
the cases they were designed for. On this view the answer to bad argument is
more argument, and building the exception into the rule is more dangerous than
the problem it solves.

**The scope objection.** A related position holds that the paradox is real but
that its remedy belongs to social rather than legal authority — that refusing to
platform, associate with, or fund a position is different in kind from
prohibiting it, and that conflating the two is what makes the debate go badly.

There is no consensus resolution here, which is why the status of this entry is
"debated". What is not in dispute is the logical point that a completely
unrestricted rule of toleration cannot guarantee its own survival.

## Examples

**The self-undermining vote.** A group campaigns, entirely within the rules, on
a platform of abolishing future elections. Every step is procedurally legitimate.
The outcome removes the procedure. Constitutions that contain
unamendable clauses — Germany's Basic Law protects its core articles from
amendment, and several other constitutions do the same — are an explicit
institutional answer to exactly this shape of problem.

**A rule that exempts itself.** Compare with a simpler case. A sign reading
"ignore all signs" cannot be obeyed. The trouble is the same: a rule whose scope
includes itself can issue instructions that cancel it. Toleration is unusual
only because we care about the outcome.

**Where the analogy breaks.** A club can identify a hostile takeover; a society
often cannot agree on who counts as hostile. The abstract argument is easy and
the application is hard, and treating the second as though it were as settled as
the first is the most common error in discussions of this footnote.

## History

**1945.** *The Open Society and Its Enemies* was published in two volumes, written
between 1938 and 1943 while Popper taught at Canterbury University College in
Christchurch, New Zealand. He described it as his "war effort" — a defence of
liberal democracy written in response to the rise of fascism, aimed at what he
saw as the authoritarian strands running from Plato through Hegel to Marx.

The paradox of tolerance is not in the main text. It is note 4 to chapter 7,
which concerns Plato. It is a few sentences long, and Popper never developed it
into a chapter, an essay, or a system.

**A precedent.** The structure was not new. Debates about whether a free society
must tolerate those who would end it recur through liberal political thought,
and Popper's contribution was the compressed formulation rather than the
observation itself.

**1971.** John Rawls addressed the same question in *A Theory of Justice*, in a
section titled "Toleration of the Intolerant". Rawls arrived independently at a
qualified conclusion, and his formulation of the triggering condition is more
precise than Popper's.

**2010s onwards.** The footnote acquired a second life on the internet, usually
as an image or a short quotation. In that form the qualifying clause is
routinely omitted. It is a striking case of a passage becoming famous in a
version its author did not write — which is worth knowing before citing it in
either direction.

## Why It Matters

The immediate lesson is narrow and worth having on its own: **read the primary
source.** A famous position summarised in one line is often not the position.
Popper's footnote is short enough to read in under a minute, and doing so changes
what it supports.

The wider lesson is structural. **A rule that applies without exception to
everything, including itself, can license its own abolition.** That is the same
shape as the Liar sentence and as Russell's paradox, and the fixes available are
also the same in outline: restrict the rule's scope, or accept that it must
contain an exception clause. Constitutions with unamendable articles are the
practical version of a type restriction.

The third lesson is the one this entry has tried hardest to make. **A valid
abstract argument does not carry its own application.** The logical point here is
solid, and it tells you almost nothing about any particular case. Most of the
work — and all of the danger — is in the step from "unlimited toleration is
unstable" to "therefore this specific thing should be restricted." Treating that
step as automatic is a mistake regardless of which conclusion you reach.

## Modern Relevance

**Content moderation.** Every platform with rules faces this directly, and every
one of them has landed on a version of the reciprocity answer: participation is
conditioned on accepting the rules of participation. The hard problems are all
in the application — who decides, on what evidence, with what appeal — which is
exactly what the abstract argument does not settle.

**Constitutional design.** Unamendable clauses, judicial review, and
supermajority requirements are all mechanisms for stopping a system from being
used to dismantle itself through its own procedures. They are engineering
responses to a logical problem.

**Institutional norms.** Scientific and academic communities operate a version
of the reciprocity reading: any claim may be argued for, provided it is argued
for. Refusing to engage with evidence does not get you excluded by decree; it
gets you excluded by irrelevance. That mechanism is closer to what Popper
actually recommended than the version usually attributed to him.

**Protocol design.** Distributed systems face a structurally identical problem
and solve it explicitly. A network that accepts every message including messages
that shut it down does not survive; so real protocols specify which messages are
in-protocol and reject the rest, and do it in advance rather than case by case.
The lesson transfers: the time to decide the boundary is before you need it, in
public, with the rule visible.

## Discover

San Francisco, 1964. Psychologist Robert Rosenthal and school principal Lenore Jacobson conduct an experiment at a California elementary school they call Oak School.

At the start of the year, they give all students a standard IQ test. Then they randomly select 20% of the students and tell their teachers that these children have been identified by the test as "intellectual bloomers" — students who can be expected to show unusual intellectual development over the coming year.

The test had actually identified nothing of the sort. The "bloomers" were chosen completely at random.

Eight months later, the researchers return and retest all the children. The randomly-designated bloomers showed significantly greater gains in IQ than their classmates — particularly in the younger grades, where children in the experimental group gained 27 IQ points more than the control group.

The teachers had expected more. And somehow — without anyone intending it, without anyone knowing the experiment was happening — more had arrived.

## Understand

The Pygmalion Effect describes how expectations create the outcomes they predict, through the changes they make in the behaviour of the person holding the expectation.

When a teacher believes a student is capable, they behave differently in dozens of small ways:

- They call on that student more often.
- They wait longer for an answer before moving on.
- They provide more detailed feedback on the student's work.
- They smile and nod more when the student speaks.
- They offer more challenging material.
- They interpret ambiguous performance as evidence of underlying ability.

The student, in turn, responds to this different treatment. More opportunities means more practice. More patience means more time to think. Better feedback means faster learning. More challenge means more growth. The student who was randomly told to expect success receives an education that produces success.

### The four channels

Rosenthal later identified four mechanisms through which expectations travel:

**Climate**: teachers create a warmer emotional environment for expected high achievers — more eye contact, more smiling, more encouragement.

**Input**: teachers give expected high achievers more material, more challenging content, more of their time.

**Output**: teachers give expected high achievers more opportunities to respond, more time to answer, and more chances to correct errors.

**Feedback**: teachers give expected high achievers more informative and more positive feedback on their performance.

All four channels are largely below the teacher's awareness. Teachers in the Oak School experiment did not know they were being studied. They were not consciously giving the "bloomers" special treatment. The expectation operated through their behaviour without their knowledge.

### The Golem Effect

The mirror image — negative expectations leading to underperformance — is called the **Golem Effect**. Students labelled as low achievers receive less time, less challenge, less feedback, fewer second chances. The expectation that they will underperform creates the conditions under which they do. The Golem Effect is the more damaging of the two in most real-world educational settings, because negative expectations are embedded in many standard classroom practices.

## Examples

| Domain | Positive expectation | Observed outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Education | Teachers told students are "bloomers" | Significant IQ gains at end of year |
| Military training | Officers told recruits have high potential | Recruits performed significantly better on tests |
| Management | Managers told employees have high potential | Employees received more development, performed better |
| Clinical psychology | Therapists told patients were likely to respond well | Patients showed better therapeutic outcomes |
| Sports coaching | Coaches told athletes had untapped potential | Athletes showed measurable improvement in performance |

The effect has been replicated across contexts, though the size of the effect varies considerably across studies and settings.

## Explore

**The controversy over the original study.** Rosenthal and Jacobson's 1968 experiment attracted immediate criticism from methodologists. The IQ test they used (Flanagan's Tests of General Ability) was not well-validated for the age groups studied. The IQ gains for the youngest children were implausibly large. Several attempted replications produced smaller or null effects. The debate over whether the original data are reliable continues.

**The meta-analytic picture.** Despite the controversy over the original study, a 1978 meta-analysis by Rosenthal and Rubin of 345 studies found a consistent positive effect: teacher expectations do predict student outcomes beyond what baseline ability would predict, even after controlling for prior performance. The effect is real, though smaller than the original study suggested.

**Self-fulfilling prophecy in finance.** George Soros and others have argued that financial markets exhibit a closely related phenomenon: expectations about prices shape the behaviour of market participants in ways that cause prices to move toward the expectation. A belief that a currency will fall triggers actions (selling, hedging, short positions) that cause it to fall. Soros called this **reflexivity**. The mechanism is identical to Pygmalion: the expectation changes behaviour, the behaviour changes reality, the reality confirms the expectation.

**Stereotype threat.** The negative Pygmalion (Golem Effect) has a specific documented form in Claude Steele's stereotype threat research. When members of stereotyped groups are reminded of the stereotype before a test, they perform worse — not because the stereotype is accurate, but because the awareness of negative expectations disrupts performance. The expectation, internalized, becomes a cognitive burden.

## History

**Antiquity.** The myth of Pygmalion — the sculptor who falls in love with his statue and wills it to life — appears in Ovid's *Metamorphoses*. George Bernard Shaw's 1913 play *Pygmalion* (later adapted as *My Fair Lady*) updates the myth: Professor Higgins transforms flower-girl Eliza Doolittle into a duchess not through any inherent change in Eliza, but through sustained expectation and instruction that she can pass as one.

**1948.** Robert Merton coins the term **self-fulfilling prophecy** in his sociology paper of that name, describing how a false belief can cause actions that make the belief true. The bank run is his canonical example: a rumour that a bank is insolvent causes depositors to withdraw, which makes the bank insolvent, which vindicates the rumour.

**1963–1964.** Robert Rosenthal conducts studies on experimenter expectancy effects — finding that research subjects in psychological experiments perform differently depending on what the experimenter expects — before shifting his attention to teacher expectations.

**1968.** *Pygmalion in the Classroom* is published. It is an immediate sensation, widely covered in the press, and immediately controversial in the academic literature.

**1994.** Rosenthal publishes a theoretical synthesis identifying the four channels (climate, input, output, feedback) through which expectations travel. This framework becomes the standard reference for explaining the mechanism.

## Why It Matters

**Who gets written off.** The most consequential application of the Pygmalion Effect is not in how we cultivate high performers, but in how we limit those we have written off. When a teacher, manager, or institution has decided — consciously or not — that someone is unlikely to succeed, they withdraw the resources, time, challenge, and feedback that would give that person a chance. The decision becomes self-confirming. And because these decisions disproportionately follow class, race, and gender lines, the Pygmalion Effect is a mechanism through which social inequality reproduces itself.

**Training and performance.** In military and corporate training, the Pygmalion Effect has been deliberately exploited: instructors who are told their trainees are exceptional produce better trainees, even from identical raw material. This is not about faking things — it is about what high expectations do to the quality of instruction.

**Medicine.** Research on the placebo effect suggests that physician expectations about treatment outcomes influence patient recovery, through similar channels: more encouraging communication, more careful monitoring, more responsive adjustment of treatment. The expectation travels through clinical behaviour.

## Modern Relevance

**AI and automated screening.** When hiring algorithms trained on historical data systematically lower expectations for candidates from certain backgrounds, they reproduce the Golem Effect at scale. The algorithm does not express doubt — but it assigns lower scores, which means fewer callbacks, which means less practice at interviews, which means lower performance scores, which trains future algorithms to assign lower scores. The feedback loop is slower than a teacher's nod, but structurally identical.

**Performance management.** Research on manager expectations in corporate settings finds persistent Pygmalion dynamics: employees whose managers hold high expectations for them develop faster, receive more stretch assignments, and perform better — even controlling for their starting performance. This has implications for how stretch assignments are distributed, who is placed under high-expectation managers, and how expectations are communicated in performance reviews.

**Growth mindset.** Carol Dweck's research on fixed versus growth mindsets is a close cousin of the Pygmalion Effect. Students who believe their intelligence is fixed (fixed mindset) interpret challenges as evidence of their limitations. Students who believe intelligence is developable (growth mindset) interpret challenges as opportunities. Teachers who communicate — through their feedback, their assignments, their expectations — that intelligence is developable are, in effect, deploying the Pygmalion Effect deliberately.

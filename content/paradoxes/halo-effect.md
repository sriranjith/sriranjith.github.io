## Discover

You are interviewing two candidates for a job. The first walks in with confident posture, makes immediate eye contact, and shakes your hand firmly. The second is quieter, takes a moment to settle, answers your questions thoughtfully.

You probably already know who you are leaning toward.

But here is the question worth sitting with: is your preference based on the job they would do? Or is it based on the handshake?

Psychologist Edward Thorndike asked a version of this question in 1920, using a population he could check: US Army soldiers, rated by their commanding officers. He compared ratings across traits — physique, intelligence, leadership, character, personal qualities. If officers were accurately observing independent qualities, the ratings should correlate loosely, if at all. Instead he found correlations so high that officers seemed to be starting from a general impression and then filling in the specifics to match. One glowing quality lit up all the others. Thorndike called this the **halo** effect, and noted it was an error.

## Understand

The halo effect is the tendency for one positive (or negative) trait to distort our evaluation of all other traits in the same direction.

When we form an overall impression of someone — they are warm, attractive, confident, successful — that impression acts like a **filter**. Subsequent information passes through it and gets nudged toward consistency. We notice things that fit the impression. We remember things that fit. We interpret ambiguous things in a way that fits.

The mechanism is well-documented across perception, memory, and judgment:

**Attractiveness and competence.** Studies across multiple countries find that physically attractive people are rated as more intelligent, more moral, more honest, and more competent, even by people who know about the effect. The correlation between attractiveness and rated intelligence in some studies approaches the correlation between actual intelligence and rated intelligence.

**The horn effect.** The same mechanism runs in reverse. A single negative trait — a nervous tic, a stumble early in a presentation, an unfashionable haircut — can contaminate every subsequent judgment. The horn effect is the halo turned upside down.

**Order effects.** The halo effect is particularly powerful for *first impressions*, because early information sets the frame through which all later information is processed. A confident opening to a presentation shapes how the audience hears everything that follows, even if the content is technically identical to a presentation with a tentative opening.

### Why the bias is so hard to escape

The halo effect operates largely below the threshold of awareness. You do not experience yourself thinking "that person shook my hand confidently, therefore their financial forecasting is probably accurate." You experience yourself noticing that their forecasting is accurate — because the halo has already done its work before the conscious assessment begins.

This is why knowing about the effect provides only partial protection. Kahneman's research showed that even experienced professionals who understand cognitive bias show the halo effect in their own judgments at rates close to the general population.

## Examples

| Context | The halo trait | What it distorts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Job interviews | Physical attractiveness | Rated intelligence, competence, leadership |
| Political elections | Height | Perceived authority and electability |
| Medical consultations | Apparent friendliness | Perceived accuracy of diagnosis |
| Student evaluation | Legible handwriting | Rated quality of content and argument |
| Criminal trials | Physical attractiveness | Sentences handed down (attractive defendants receive shorter sentences) |
| Wine tasting | Expensive-looking label | Ratings of taste, complexity, and quality |

In the wine case, the same wine received significantly higher taste ratings when served from an expensive-looking bottle than from a plain one. Crucially, subjects were confident their rating reflected taste, not presentation. The halo operates without announcing itself.

## Explore

**The "beautiful is good" stereotype.** The overlap between attractiveness and moral/intellectual virtue ratings has been documented so consistently that psychologists have a name for it: the beauty-is-good stereotype. It appears from early childhood — children as young as three prefer faces their culture considers attractive and attribute more positive qualities to them. The bias is not learned; it seems to emerge from something more fundamental about how humans build representations of other people.

**The halo in organisations.** Phil Rosenzweig's 2007 book *The Halo Effect* extended the concept to business analysis: when companies are performing well, analysts and journalists attribute their success to strong leadership, good strategy, and clear culture. When the same companies decline, the same leadership is described as arrogant, the same strategy as hubristic, the same culture as dysfunctional. Nothing changed except the financials — and the halo reversed.

**Structured assessment as partial remedy.** The most effective response to the halo effect in hiring and evaluation is **structured interviews**: the same questions in the same order for every candidate, with each answer rated before moving to the next. This prevents the overall impression from forming before individual assessments are made. Research consistently finds that structured interviews predict job performance better than unstructured ones, partly because they interrupt the halo mechanism.

**Implicit association tests.** The IAT (Implicit Association Test) can measure halo-type associations between concepts — attractive/good, dark skin/negative — that subjects explicitly deny holding. The test reveals associations operating below the level of conscious report, which is consistent with the halo mechanism being pre-conscious.

**The Halo Effect vs. correlation.** It is worth noting that some of the correlations the halo effect produces are not entirely spurious. Physically attractive people may, on average, have received better educational opportunities, more positive social feedback, and higher expectations from teachers (see Pygmalion Effect) — so there is sometimes a weak real relationship between attractiveness and outcomes. The halo effect inflates these correlations far beyond any real signal, but the correlations are not pure artefact. This makes the effect harder to detect and correct.

## History

**1920.** Edward Thorndike publishes "A Constant Error in Psychological Ratings" in the *Journal of Applied Psychology*. He does not use the term "halo effect" in the title but introduces it in the text to describe the systematic inflation of correlated ratings.

**1950s–1960s.** Research on the halo effect expands into personnel psychology and performance evaluation. Studies find the effect in supervisor ratings, teacher evaluations of students, and jury decisions.

**1972.** Karen Dion, Ellen Berscheid, and Elaine Walster publish "What Is Beautiful Is Good" in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, documenting the beauty-is-good stereotype systematically and naming it explicitly. The paper becomes one of the most cited in social psychology.

**1977.** Nisbett and Wilson publish "The Halo Effect: Evidence for Unconscious Alteration of Judgments," showing that people systematically misattribute their global impressions to specific qualities — and deny that the irrelevant quality influenced them, even when it demonstrably did.

**2007.** Phil Rosenzweig's *The Halo Effect* applies the concept to business analysis and management research, showing how attributions of organisational success and failure follow company performance rather than preceding it.

## Why It Matters

**Hiring and evaluation.** Unstructured job interviews are notoriously susceptible to the halo effect. Attractive candidates, candidates from prestigious universities, and candidates who make a confident first impression are routinely rated higher on all subsequent dimensions — including technical skills that the interviewer may not be qualified to evaluate. The result is that organisations systematically select for social performance and appearance in roles where these predict nothing.

**Justice.** Research on mock trials and sentencing data finds that physical attractiveness systematically reduces sentences and increases acquittal rates. The halo distorts what should be a purely evidence-based judgment. This has motivated calls for procedural reforms that reduce the prominence of appearance — screens, transcripts, anonymised submissions — in at least some phases of legal process.

**Medical care.** Studies find that patients rate attractive physicians as more competent and are more likely to follow their advice. Physicians rate attractive patients as more cooperative and better historians of their own symptoms. Both distortions affect the quality of care.

**Self-knowledge.** The halo effect runs inward as well. People who are performing well in one area of their life tend to rate themselves more highly on unrelated dimensions — fitness, relationships, financial health. The success halo makes accurate self-assessment harder precisely when things are going well.

## Modern Relevance

**Online profiles and photographs.** In hiring, dating, and social media, photographs shape all subsequent evaluation. Profile photographs on job application platforms influence callback rates significantly, even when reviewers are explicitly told to focus on qualifications. Studies in which CV photographs are varied while all other content is held constant find large, persistent effects of attractiveness on callback probability.

**AI training and bias.** Machine learning systems trained on human evaluation data inherit human halo effects. If a training set was produced by evaluators who applied halo-inflated ratings, the model learns those distortions as if they were signal. Attempts to detect and correct bias in AI hiring tools have found systematic patterns consistent with inherited halo effects in training data.

**Influencer culture.** Social media amplifies the halo effect at scale. Influencers who are perceived as attractive or stylish are rated as more knowledgeable about unrelated domains — finance, nutrition, science — even when their expertise is nonexistent. The mechanism is the same as Thorndike's 1920 soldiers; the audience is global.

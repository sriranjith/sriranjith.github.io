## Discover

On the table in front of you is a heap of sand. Ten thousand grains. Nobody
would argue about it: that is a heap.

Take one grain away and drop it on the floor.

Still a heap? Of course. One grain is nothing. A single grain of sand cannot be
the difference between a heap and not-a-heap — that would have to be a very
special grain, and sand does not come with special grains.

So take another. Still a heap. And another. Still a heap.

Keep going. Every time, the same reasoning applies, and every time it looks
completely safe. Nine thousand grains, heap. Five hundred grains, heap. Twenty
grains — well, hold on.

Eventually you are holding one grain of sand between your finger and thumb. That
is not a heap. Anyone can see it is not a heap.

But you never took the grain that destroyed the heap. There was no moment when
you looked down and thought: *that one did it.* Every step was harmless, and the
harmless steps added up to something that cannot be right.

## Understand

The trap is built out of two things you already believe, and it is very hard to
stop believing either of them.

**The first thing you believe: there is a heap.** Ten thousand grains of sand,
piled up, is a heap. That is not a matter of opinion or a trick of language. It
is what the word means.

**The second thing you believe: one grain never matters.** If a pile is a heap,
then that pile minus one grain is still a heap. Philosophers call this
*tolerance*: the word "heap" tolerates tiny changes. It has to, or the word would
be unusable. Nobody learns "heap" by being told a number.

Now put them together and turn the handle ten thousand times. You are forced to
conclude that a single grain is a heap. It is not. So the two things you believe
cannot both be true, and the reasoning that connects them is nothing more
exciting than "if A then B, and A, therefore B" repeated.

That is why this counts as a real contradiction rather than a mere surprise.
Something you are sure of has to go.

### It is not about sand

Sand is just a convenient stage. The same machine runs on almost every word you
own.

A man with a full head of hair is not bald. Pulling out one hair cannot make a
non-bald man bald. Therefore no number of hairs pulled out ever makes anyone
bald. Eubulides ran this version too, and it is why the paradox is sometimes
called the Bald Man.

Or: a person who is one second old is a child. Someone one second older than a
child is still a child. Therefore everyone is a child, forever.

Or run it in the other direction. One grain is not a heap. Adding one grain to a
non-heap cannot make a heap. Therefore ten thousand grains are not a heap — and
neither are ten million.

The words that resist this are the exact, made-up ones: *prime*, *divisible by
seven*, *a legal adult in this jurisdiction*. Everything you learned by being
shown examples rather than given a rule is vulnerable.

### Where the exits are

There are only three places to push back, because the argument has only three
moving parts.

1. **Deny that there is a heap to begin with.** Say the word "heap" is broken and
   refers to nothing. This sounds absurd until you notice that it is the only
   option that leaves the reasoning entirely alone.
2. **Deny that one grain never matters.** Say there is an exact number — 1,847
   grains, perhaps — where the heap stops. This sounds absurd too, because you
   have no idea what the number is and neither does anyone else.
3. **Deny that the reasoning is valid.** Say that "if A then B" arguments,
   chained ten thousand times, do not preserve truth the way they do in
   mathematics. This means changing logic itself.

Every serious position on the sorites is a variation on one of those three, and
each one costs something people would rather not pay. That is why the paradox is
two and a half thousand years old and still open.

## Examples

**A forced march.** Get someone to answer a single question ten thousand times:
"is this a heap?" Start at ten thousand grains, remove one before each question,
and forbid them from saying "sort of". They must switch from yes to no at some
point — the questions run out otherwise. Ask them afterwards to defend the grain
they switched on. Nobody can. The forced march makes the paradox something you
do rather than something you read.

**Colour.** Print a strip of a thousand patches running from pure red to pure
orange, each patch one thousandth of the way along. No two neighbours are
distinguishable by eye. The first is red; the last is not. There is no first
non-red patch that you can point at, and no adjacent pair you would call
different colours.

**Where the line has to be drawn anyway.**

| The vague thing | The sharp line society uses | What the line is doing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grown up | 18 years old | A decision, not a discovery |
| Too drunk to drive | 0.08 % blood alcohol | A stipulation, chosen for enforceability |
| Poor | A published income threshold | An administrative cut in a continuous quantity |
| Dead | Absence of brain-stem activity | A convention refined as medicine changed |
| A tumour | A measured diameter on a scan | A trigger for treatment, not a fact about biology |

Every one of these is an admission. We know the underlying property has no sharp
edge, so we bolt one on, and then we argue about the bolt. The sorites is what
you get when you are not allowed to bolt one on.

**A famous non-example.** "Prime" is not vague. There is no borderline prime, no
pair of adjacent numbers where you cannot say which is which, and no forced
march to run. This is the control condition: vagueness is a property of some
words, not an inevitable feature of language.

## Explore

The four surviving families of answer split on which of the three exits they
take. What follows is who says what, and what each has to give up.

### Epistemicism: the line is real and you cannot find it

Timothy Williamson (*Vagueness*, 1994) takes the option nearly everyone else
rules out on sight. There *is* an exact number of grains at which the heap stops
being a heap. There *is* a last hair. "Heap" draws a perfectly sharp boundary;
we simply do not know where it is, and we cannot know.

The attraction is enormous: classical logic survives untouched, every sentence is
true or false, and the paradox is dissolved by rejecting the tolerance premise
outright. The cost is a claim that sounds like magic. If nobody has ever fixed
the boundary, what fixes it?

Williamson's answer is that meaning is determined by the whole tangle of how a
word is used across a community, and that this tangle settles a boundary in the
way a huge computation settles an answer nobody has computed. Our ignorance is
then a principled consequence of how knowledge works: knowledge requires a margin
for error, and there is no margin available at a boundary. Roy Sorensen defends a
version of the same position.

The recurring objection is not that epistemicism is inconsistent — it is not —
but that it buys logical tidiness with a metaphysical promissory note.

### Supervaluationism: true on every way of sharpening it

Kit Fine ("Vagueness, Truth and Logic", 1975) offers the most popular middle
road. Take a vague language and consider all the *admissible* ways of making it
precise — every way of stipulating a cut-off that respects the clear cases and
the relations between terms. A sentence is true if it comes out true on every
sharpening, false if false on every one, and neither if the sharpenings disagree.

This has a lovely consequence. "There is a number of grains at which the heap
stops" is true, because it is true on every sharpening. But no particular
statement of the form "the heap stops at 1,847 grains" is true, because each is
true on only one sharpening. You get the existence of a boundary without any
particular boundary. Classical tautologies survive; bivalence does not.

Two costs. First, "admissible" is itself vague, so the vagueness reappears one
level up — this is the *higher-order vagueness* problem, and it is the standard
weapon against every non-epistemicist theory. Second, familiar reasoning patterns
like reductio and argument by cases stop working once you add an operator for
"definitely". Fine himself later moved away from the view.

### Degree theory and fuzzy logic: truth by amounts

Joseph Goguen ("The Logic of Inexact Concepts", 1969), building on Lotfi Zadeh's
fuzzy sets (1965), replaces true-or-false with a number between 0 and 1. Ten
thousand grains: truth value 1. One grain: truth value 0. Five thousand grains:
somewhere in between.

Each step of the sorites is then *almost* true rather than true, and the tiny
shortfalls accumulate. Run ten thousand nearly-perfect inferences in a row and
you can end up with something worth nothing at all. The argument is not valid
after all, and it fails in a way that explains exactly why it looked so
convincing.

The complaint is artificial precision. Saying that "this is a heap" is true to
degree 0.5 rather than 0.51 replaces one sharp boundary with an infinite
continuum of them. Where did those decimals come from? Higher-order vagueness
bites here too.

### Nihilism: there are no heaps

Peter Unger ("There Are No Ordinary Things", 1979) does the thing that makes
people put the paper down. He accepts the tolerance premise, accepts that a
single grain is not a heap, and runs the argument backwards: no heaps. Then he
notices that the same series can be built for *stone*, *table*, *cloud* and
*chair*, and concludes that ordinary things are incoherent posits. In a companion
paper the argument reaches people.

Nobody believes this, including, arguably, Unger, who later disowned it. Its use
is as a measuring stick: it shows precisely what the price of keeping both the
premises and the logic is, and it makes every other theory look reasonable by
comparison.

### The rest of the field

**Contextualism** (Diana Raffman and others) holds that the extension of "heap"
shifts as attention moves along the series, so the reasoner never faces two
adjacent cases under one fixed standard. **Subvaluationism** (Dominic Hyde) is
supervaluationism's mirror image: borderline sentences are both true and false,
which requires a paraconsistent logic. **Incoherentism** holds that our rules for
"heap" are jointly unsatisfiable and that this is a fact about us, not the world.

What no one has produced is a theory that keeps classical logic, keeps
bivalence, keeps tolerance, and keeps the heaps. The consensus is only about the
shape of the bill, not who should pay it.

## Deep Dive

Fix a vague predicate $F$ and a **sorites series** $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_n$: a
finite sequence of cases in which $a_0$ is a clear $F$, $a_n$ is a clear non-$F$,
and adjacent members differ only marginally in whatever respect $F$ is sensitive
to. For the heap, $a_i$ is a pile of $n - i$ grains.

### The tolerance principle

The intuition that one grain never matters is the **tolerance principle**: for a
small enough difference $\delta$ in the relevant dimension,

$$\forall x\, \forall y \;\big[ \left| x - y \right| \le \delta \;\rightarrow\; (Fx \leftrightarrow Fy) \big]$$

Restricted to the series, this is

$$\text{(T)} \qquad \forall i < n \;(Fa_i \rightarrow Fa_{i+1})$$

Michael Dummett's formulation makes the source of tolerance explicit:
observational and communally taught predicates must be tolerant, because
competence with them is acquired from clear cases and preserved under
indiscriminable change. If $F$ were intolerant, two indistinguishable objects
could differ in $F$, and nothing in how we learned $F$ could account for the
difference.

### Three ways to state the argument

The three standard presentations are classically equivalent but come apart under
non-classical logics, which is why the choice of presentation matters.

**Conditional form.** Premises $Fa_0$ and the $n$ conditionals $Fa_i \rightarrow Fa_{i+1}$;
conclusion $Fa_n$ by $n$ applications of modus ponens. Only modus ponens is used.

**Inductive form.** Premises $Fa_0$ and (T); conclusion $\forall i \le n\, Fa_i$
by mathematical induction on $i$. Here (T) is the induction step and $Fa_0$ the
base case; the derivation is the ordinary induction schema

$$\big[ Fa_0 \wedge \forall i (Fa_i \rightarrow Fa_{i+1}) \big] \rightarrow \forall i\, Fa_i$$

**Line-drawing form.** The denial that a sharp cut-off exists:

$$\text{(L)} \qquad \neg \exists i \,(Fa_i \wedge \neg Fa_{i+1})$$

In classical logic (T) and (L) are interderivable, since $\neg(p \wedge \neg q)$
is equivalent to $p \rightarrow q$. This equivalence is the hinge of the whole
debate: it is what forces anyone who denies tolerance to assert a sharp cut-off,
and it is what several non-classical treatments break.

Because $\neg Fa_n$ is as secure as $Fa_0$, the same premises also run backwards
by modus tollens, delivering $\neg Fa_0$. The paradox therefore has two
conclusions, each contradicting a datum, and no asymmetry to exploit.

### How each position formalises its answer

**Epistemicism.** Rejects (T) and (L); asserts $\exists i (Fa_i \wedge \neg Fa_{i+1})$.
Logic and bivalence are untouched. The explanatory burden shifts to why the
witness is unknowable, discharged by a **margin-for-error principle**: knowledge
of $Fa_i$ requires $F$ to hold in all cases sufficiently similar to $a_i$, so

$$K(Fa_i) \rightarrow Fa_{i+1}$$

Near the cut-off this is unsatisfiable, so the boundary is knowledge-resistant by
construction. Note the contrast: the epistemicist's $K(Fa_i) \rightarrow Fa_{i+1}$
is a claim about knowledge, whereas the sorites premise $Fa_i \rightarrow Fa_{i+1}$
is a claim about truth. Williamson's thesis is that speakers systematically
mistake the first for the second.

**Supervaluationism.** A *precisification* $s$ maps $F$ to a classical extension,
subject to admissibility constraints: clear cases keep their verdicts, and
penumbral connections are preserved — if $a_i$ is redder than $a_j$, no admissible
$s$ makes $a_j$ red and $a_i$ not. Then

$$\phi \text{ is } \textbf{supertrue} \iff s \models \phi \text{ for every admissible } s$$

Now $\exists i (Fa_i \wedge \neg Fa_{i+1})$ is supertrue, since every precisification
supplies a witness. But for each particular $k$, $Fa_k \wedge \neg Fa_{k+1}$ is
neither supertrue nor superfalse, because the witness moves between
precisifications. A true existential with no true instance is exactly the
combination the paradox needs, and it comes at the price of bivalence.

(T) comes out superfalse, so the argument is unsound rather than invalid — the
logic is preserved at the level of which sentences are valid. The known casualty
is the metarules. Adding a determinacy operator $D$, with $D\phi$ supertrue iff
$\phi$ is supertrue, one gets $\phi \models D\phi$ but not
$\models \phi \rightarrow D\phi$, so conditional proof fails; contraposition,
reductio and argument by cases fail with it. And $D$ reintroduces the problem:
"definitely a heap" is itself tolerant, generating a second-order sorites over
$DFa_i$, and a third over $DDFa_i$, without end.

**Degree theory.** Let $v(Fa_i) \in [0,1]$ decrease monotonically with $v(Fa_0) = 1$
and $v(Fa_n) = 0$; take the simplest case $v(Fa_i) = 1 - i/n$. Using the
Łukasiewicz conditional

$$v(\phi \rightarrow \psi) = \min\!\big(1,\; 1 - v(\phi) + v(\psi)\big)$$

each premise has value $v(Fa_i \rightarrow Fa_{i+1}) = 1 - 1/n$: very nearly true,
which is why the premises are so persuasive. But validity in this setting means
preserving degree, and iterated modus ponens does not. With the Łukasiewicz rule,
$n$ chained inferences from premises of value $1 - \epsilon$ bound the conclusion
only by

$$v(Fa_n) \ge v(Fa_0) - n\epsilon$$

With $\epsilon = 1/n$ the bound is $0$: the argument is invalid, and invalid by
exactly the accumulated margin that made each step look free. Fuzzy logic thereby
explains the *seductiveness* of the sorites, not merely its failure.

The standing objection is that $v$ is a real-valued function. Asking why
$v(Fa_{5000})$ is $0.5$ rather than $0.501$ is the sharp-boundary question
returning in continuous dress, multiplied.

**Nihilism.** Keeps classical logic and keeps (T), and takes $\neg Fa_n$ as the
fixed point rather than $Fa_0$. From (T) and $\neg Fa_n$, $n$ applications of
modus tollens yield $\neg Fa_0$. Generalising over predicates for which sorites
series can be constructed — which is nearly all sortal predicates for
medium-sized objects — Unger concludes the extensions are empty. The position is
formally impeccable and universally rejected, which is a useful demonstration
that formal impeccability is not the binding constraint here.

### What any solution must do

A solution has to say which of the following it denies, and pay the associated
bill:

| Denies | Position | Bill |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tolerance (T) | Epistemicism | An unknowable sharp boundary fixed by use |
| Bivalence | Supervaluationism | Metarule failures; higher-order vagueness |
| Classical validity | Degree theory | Artificial numerical precision |
| $Fa_0$ | Nihilism | There are no heaps, stones or people |
| Nothing | — | Contradiction |

Higher-order vagueness is the test that keeps failing candidates. Any theory that
introduces a new category — borderline, indeterminate, degree $0.5$ — must say
where *that* category begins, and the question is as hard as the one it replaced.
Epistemicism's advantage is that it never introduces a new category. That is also
the reason people distrust it.

## History

**Fourth century BCE.** Diogenes Laertius (II.108) credits Eubulides of Miletus
with seven puzzles, among them the Liar, the Bald Man (*phalakros*) and the Heap
(*sōritēs*, from *sōros*, a heap). Eubulides belonged to the Megarian school and
was a contemporary and critic of Aristotle. Nothing he wrote survives, so the
exact wording he used is unrecoverable; what survives is the name and the shape.
The puzzle was almost certainly a weapon in live debate rather than a private
worry — the Megarians used it to embarrass opponents who claimed to possess
knowledge.

**Third century BCE.** The Stoics took it seriously enough to develop a doctrine
of response. Chrysippus advised the sage to "fall silent" — to stop answering as
the series approaches the unclear cases, rather than assert something false.
Cicero reports the strategy in the *Academica* (II.92–94) and treats it as an
evasion: the Academic sceptics regarded the sorites as evidence that the Stoic
theory of secure comprehension could not be sustained. The debate mattered
because the Stoics had staked their epistemology on the existence of
self-certifying impressions, and the heap is precisely a case where impressions
do not certify anything.

**Second century CE.** Galen, in *On Medical Experience* (chapters 16–17), gives
the fullest ancient treatment and the most exasperated. His objection is
professional: medicine builds knowledge by accumulating cases, and if no single
additional case can convert insufficient evidence into sufficient evidence, then
no amount of experience ever amounts to knowledge. He describes being asked, one
grain at a time, where the heap begins, and refusing to play.

**Medieval period.** Vagueness lived on in the *insolubilia* and in scholastic
discussions of *latitudo formarum* — the intension and remission of qualities —
but the sorites was mostly treated as a sophism to be classified rather than a
problem to be solved.

**1923.** Bertrand Russell's paper "Vagueness" restarts the modern discussion. He
argues that vagueness is a feature of representations rather than of the world,
and that logic applies strictly only to precise languages — a position that
concedes much of what later theorists try to save.

**1937.** Max Black's "Vagueness: An Exercise in Logical Analysis" introduces
consistency profiles and the systematic study of borderline cases, moving the
topic from a curiosity to a technical subject.

**1965–1969.** Lotfi Zadeh's fuzzy sets and Joseph Goguen's logic of inexact
concepts supply degrees of truth. The engineering applications arrive long before
philosophical agreement does.

**1975.** Kit Fine's "Vagueness, Truth and Logic" gives supervaluationism its
mature form, including penumbral connection. Michael Dummett's "Wang's Paradox",
published the same year, argues that observational predicates are inherently
tolerant and therefore that our concepts are genuinely incoherent.

**1979.** Peter Unger publishes the nihilist reading, and the field acquires its
outer boundary.

**1994.** Timothy Williamson's *Vagueness* makes epistemicism a serious
contender rather than a curiosity, principally by supplying the margin-for-error
account of why the boundary is unknowable. Since then the literature has been an
argument between four camps rather than a search for a fifth.

## Why It Matters

The sorites is not a puzzle about sand. It is the discovery that ordinary
language and classical logic do not fit together, and it is the oldest
unresolved problem in either subject.

**It is why logic has alternatives.** Multi-valued logics, supervaluation
semantics, paraconsistent logics and fuzzy set theory were all developed or
sharpened with vagueness as a target. If classical logic had accommodated
vagueness, several twentieth-century research programmes would not exist.

**It explains why law is written the way it is.** Statutes speak of "reasonable
care", "undue delay" and "substantial harm" — irreducibly vague, because a
precise rule would be gamed and would produce absurd results at the margin. But
courts must return a verdict, so the vagueness is resolved case by case rather
than in the text. Legal theorists distinguish the *core* of a rule from its
*penumbra*, borrowing the vocabulary directly from this debate. Every
bright-line rule in a statute book — the drinking age, the speed limit, the
threshold for a capital gain — is a decision to bolt a sharp edge onto something
that has none, and the arbitrariness is not a drafting failure but the price of
having enforceable rules at all.

**It sets the terms of arguments that will not otherwise end.** When a foetus
becomes a person, when a patient becomes dead, when a country becomes a
democracy, when a model becomes dangerous: each is a sorites series with a
decision attached. Recognising the structure does not settle the argument, but it
does rule out one bad move — the demand that an opponent name the exact grain.
Nobody can name the grain. That is the point of the paradox, not a weakness in
the opponent.

**It clarifies what tolerance costs.** The reason "heap" is useful is exactly the
reason it is paradoxical. A word learnable from examples must be insensitive to
tiny differences, and a word insensitive to tiny differences cannot have a sharp
extension. Usefulness and logical good behaviour are in tension, and natural
language chose usefulness.

## Modern Relevance

**Machine learning thresholds.** A classifier outputs a continuous score and a
threshold converts it to a decision. The threshold is a bolted-on sharp edge, and
every dispute about false positives versus false negatives is a dispute about
where to bolt it. Calibration curves are, formally, degree-theoretic membership
functions; the philosophical objection to fuzzy logic — that the exact decimals
are unmotivated — is the practical objection to threshold tuning.

**Content moderation.** "Hate speech", "misinformation" and "harassment" are
tolerant predicates enforced at scale by systems that must return a binary
verdict millions of times a day. Written policies function as attempted
precisifications; appeal processes are the forced march, run in public, by people
who did not consent to the experiment.

**Fuzzy control.** Zadeh's sets found their commercial home in controllers for
trains, cameras and washing machines, where rules like "if the load is somewhat
heavy, spin somewhat faster" outperform sharp thresholds because real inputs are
noisy. The philosophy remains contested; the engineering shipped decades ago.

**Clinical cut-offs.** Hypertension, obesity, anaemia and diabetes are diagnosed
against numerical thresholds in continuous distributions, and moving a threshold
by a small amount reclassifies millions of people overnight without any of them
changing. The recurring controversies over redefining these boundaries are
sorites arguments conducted with public health consequences.

**Species and genomes.** Biological kinds shade into one another across
populations and across evolutionary time; the ring species that interbreed at
every adjacent step but not at the ends of the ring is a sorites series with
feathers on. Taxonomy handles it the way the law handles reasonableness — by
stipulating, and then arguing about the stipulation.

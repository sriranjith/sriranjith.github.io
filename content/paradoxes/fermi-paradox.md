## Discover

One summer day in 1950, four physicists walked to lunch at Los Alamos. They had
been joking about a run of flying-saucer sightings in the newspapers, and about a
cartoon blaming aliens for the disappearance of New York City's rubbish bins.
Then the conversation moved on to something else.

Some minutes later, in the middle of the meal, Enrico Fermi looked up and said:

> **"Where is everybody?"**

Everyone at the table knew exactly what he meant.

Look at the numbers. Our galaxy holds something between one hundred and four
hundred billion stars. It is about thirteen billion years old. The Sun is a
comparative newcomer, formed only four and a half billion years ago — so there
were stars with planets, and time to spare, for billions of years before Earth
existed. And we now know, thanks to the Kepler space telescope, that planets are
not rare at all. Most stars have them. Rocky planets at comfortable temperatures
are common.

Now suppose that just *one* civilisation, anywhere, at any point in those
thirteen billion years, decided to send ships to a few nearby stars. And suppose
that after settling in, some of those settlements did the same. Even at a
sluggish one per cent of the speed of light, with a thousand-year pause between
each hop, that spreading wave crosses the entire galaxy in a few tens of millions
of years.

A few tens of millions of years sounds enormous. Against the age of the Galaxy it
is less than one per cent. It is the blink of an eye. It has had time to happen
hundreds of times over.

So the Galaxy should be full. Every promising star should have been visited, or
settled, or at the very least littered. The sky should be busy with the
by-products of somebody's engineering.

Instead: nothing. No ships. No ruins. No radio calls. No stars wrapped in
machinery. Seventy-five years of increasingly careful looking, and the sky is
silent.

## Understand

The first thing to be clear about is that this is not a logical contradiction. It
is a **collision between an expectation and an observation**, and the honest form
of the question is: *which of the things we assumed is false?*

### The argument, stated properly

Fermi asked a question over lunch and never wrote anything down. The argument
that carries his name was built twenty-five years later by the astrophysicist
Michael Hart, in a 1975 paper in the *Quarterly Journal of the Royal Astronomical
Society*. It runs like this:

1. Interstellar travel is difficult but not impossible — no law of physics
   forbids a slow ship.
2. A civilisation that settles a few nearby systems, whose settlements do the
   same, produces an expanding frontier.
3. That frontier crosses the Galaxy in something between a million and a hundred
   million years.
4. The Galaxy is ten thousand times older than that.
5. Therefore, if any such civilisation ever arose in our galaxy, its descendants
   should be here.
6. They are not here.
7. Therefore no such civilisation ever arose.

Hart called step 6 "Fact A", and it is the pivot of the whole thing. Note what he
is *not* relying on: he is not relying on our radio telescopes being sensitive
enough to hear anyone. He is relying on nobody being physically present in the
Solar System. That makes the argument much harder to dodge than the older
complaint about SETI's null results, because it does not depend on our
instruments at all.

Frank Tipler sharpened it in 1980 by adding von Neumann's idea of a
self-reproducing machine. A civilisation does not need a great fleet or any
lasting enthusiasm for exploration. It needs to launch, once, a single probe
capable of building copies of itself out of the material it finds. After that the
Galaxy fills whether anyone is still interested or not.

### Two different silences

It helps to keep two observations apart, because they have different strengths.

**Nobody is here.** No artefacts, no probes, no settlements, no evidence of past
visits. This is Hart's Fact A. It is strong evidence *if* you accept that a
spreading civilisation would leave traces we could recognise.

**Nobody is calling.** No radio or optical signals of artificial origin. This is
much weaker evidence than it sounds, because the volume of the search space we
have covered is small. Jason Wright and colleagues estimated in 2018 that all
SETI conducted to date, measured across the relevant dimensions of frequency,
sensitivity, sky position and time, amounts to roughly a hot tub's worth of the
Earth's oceans.

Most proposed resolutions address one silence and not the other, and conflating
them is the most common way to reach a confident wrong conclusion.

### The candidate answers

Nobody knows which of these is right. Several could be partly right at once. They
are presented here in the order that they attack the argument's premises, not in
order of plausibility, because there is no agreed order of plausibility.

**Rare Earth.** Life may be common; complex, technological life may not be. Peter
Ward and Donald Brownlee argued in 2000 that Earth's history depended on a long
chain of contingencies — plate tectonics recycling carbon, a large moon
stabilising the axial tilt, Jupiter clearing debris, a quiet position in the
Galaxy, and multicellularity arising at all, which on Earth took some three
billion years. Attacks premise 7's assumption that "arising" is easy.
*The weakness:* every item on the list is an inference from a single example, and
we have no way to know which were necessary and which were merely what happened.

**The Great Filter.** Robin Hanson's 1998 formulation is the most careful way to
hold the uncertainty. Somewhere along the road from dead chemistry to a visible
interstellar civilisation there is at least one step that is *wildly* improbable —
improbable enough to account for a Galaxy with nobody in it. The question is
where. If the filter is behind us — in abiogenesis, or in the origin of the
eukaryotic cell, or in the emergence of intelligence — then we are an
extraordinary fluke and the future is open. If it is ahead of us, then the reason
nobody expands is that civilisations reliably fail to.

Hanson's corollary is the uncomfortable part, and it is genuinely
counterintuitive: **discovering independent life elsewhere would be bad news.**
Every discovery that an early step is easy pushes probability mass towards the
later steps, which are the ones still in our future.

**The zoo hypothesis.** John Ball proposed in 1973 that they are here, know about
us, and are deliberately not interfering — that Earth is set aside, whether as a
preserve, an experiment, or a quarantine. Attacks premise 6: they are here, we
just cannot tell. *The weakness:* it requires near-perfect uniformity of motive
across every civilisation, every faction within every civilisation, and millions
of years. One defector ruins it. Arguments that need unanimity from a large,
diverse and long-lived population are weak arguments.

**They are transient.** Civilisations may last only briefly — destroying
themselves, exhausting their planet, or simply losing interest in expansion and
settling into something small and quiet. This is the $L$ term in the Drake
equation doing all the work. Attacks premise 2. *The weakness:* it needs to hold
universally and permanently. Out of billions of stars over billions of years, if
even a tiny fraction of civilisations went expansionist, the Galaxy fills.

**The dark forest.** Everyone is silent on purpose, because broadcasting is
dangerous: any civilisation that reveals its position risks destruction by
another that cannot verify its intentions and cannot afford to guess wrong. The
idea has roots in David Brin's 1983 survey of the Great Silence and in
deterrence theory; Liu Cixin's 2008 novel *The Dark Forest* gave it its name and
its popularity. Attacks the "nobody is calling" silence. *The weakness:* it
explains quiet, not absence. A civilisation that expands is visible whether or
not it broadcasts — you cannot hide a settled galaxy — so the dark forest does
nothing about Hart's Fact A.

**The paradox may be a statistical artefact.** This is the newest and most
disruptive line. Anders Sandberg, Eric Drexler and Toby Ord argued in 2018 that
the standard treatment commits a basic error: the Drake equation is a product of
seven factors, several of which are uncertain over *many orders of magnitude*,
and the usual practice is to plug in a best guess for each and multiply. Doing so
gives an answer near "there should be lots". But multiplying point estimates
tells you roughly about the *mean* of the resulting distribution, and for a
product of highly uncertain quantities the mean can be enormous while the typical
outcome is a fraction of one.

When they propagate honest uncertainty distributions instead — drawing on the
actual scientific literature for each factor, especially the probability of
abiogenesis, which is uncertain across something like two hundred orders of
magnitude — the resulting distribution for the number of civilisations has a
great deal of its mass below one. On their priors the probability that we are
alone in the Milky Way is of order one half, and the probability that we are alone
in the observable universe is far from negligible.

If that is right, an empty sky is not surprising, and therefore is not strong
evidence for a Great Filter or for anything else. The paradox does not get
resolved; it gets **dissolved**, on the grounds that we were never entitled to
expect company in the first place.

*The weakness:* the conclusion is driven almost entirely by the prior chosen for
abiogenesis, which is exactly the quantity nobody knows. The paper's honest
achievement is to show how much of the apparent paradox was manufactured by bad
arithmetic. Whether it shows that we are probably alone is a separate question
and remains contested.

### Where this leaves things

**No proposed answer is established.** Anyone who tells you the Fermi paradox has
been solved is telling you which explanation they prefer. The observational
situation is genuinely thin: one example of life, zero examples of contact, and a
search that has covered a small fraction of the relevant space.

What has changed since Hart wrote is the exoplanet count. In 1975 nobody knew
whether planets were common. They are. That removes one of the escape routes and
sharpens the question rather than answering it.

## Examples

**The colonisation arithmetic.** This is the calculation that makes the argument
bite, and it is worth doing yourself.

The Galaxy's disc is about 100,000 light years across. Suppose a civilisation
sends ships to stars within 10 light years, they establish themselves over
1,000 years, and then repeat.

| Cruise speed | Travel time per 10 ly hop | Time per hop incl. settling | Hops to cross | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0.10\,c$ | 100 yr | 1,100 yr | 10,000 | 11 million yr |
| $0.01\,c$ | 1,000 yr | 2,000 yr | 10,000 | 20 million yr |
| $0.001\,c$ | 10,000 yr | 11,000 yr | 10,000 | 110 million yr |

Even the slowest row — ships at three hundred kilometres per second, which is
within sight of current propulsion concepts — finishes in a hundred and ten
million years. That is under one per cent of the Galaxy's age. Making the ships
ten times slower still, or the settling pause a hundred times longer, does not
rescue the argument; it only pushes the crossing time to a few billion years,
which is still less than the head start available.

**Where the Drake equation goes wrong.** Suppose seven factors, each of which you
believe is somewhere between $0.001$ and $1$, uniformly in the logarithm.

- Multiply the *midpoints* (about $0.032$ each): $0.032^7 \approx 3\times10^{-11}$.
- But the distribution of the product is very wide. Its logarithm is a sum of
  seven uniform variables, so the product spans from $10^{-21}$ to $1$.

The mean of a wide log-uniform product is dominated by the rare high-end draws
and is much larger than the median. Multiply point estimates and you compute
something close to a mean, then interpret it as though it were a typical case.
That is the specific mistake Sandberg, Drexler and Ord identified, and it is
enough on its own to turn "there should be thousands of civilisations" into "the
expected number is large but the most likely number is zero."

**One data point, and what it can support.** We know of exactly one biosphere,
and we are standing on it. Any inference of the form "life arose quickly on
Earth, therefore life arises easily" has to survive the objection that we could
only ever have found ourselves on a planet where it did. Brandon Carter made this
point in 1983, and it is the reason the Fermi question keeps drifting into
anthropic reasoning, where it becomes entangled with problems nobody has solved
either.

## Explore

**Fermi's question was not the Fermi paradox.** This matters for the history and
it matters for the argument.

Eric Jones, at Los Alamos, wrote in 1985 to the three surviving lunch companions —
Emil Konopinski, Edward Teller and Herbert York — and printed their recollections
in a laboratory report. What they remember is a conversation about flying saucers
and faster-than-light travel, and Fermi asking Teller for the probability that we
would obtain evidence of superluminal travel within ten years. Teller said one in
a million; Fermi said more like one in ten. Then, later in the meal, out of
nowhere: "Where is everybody?" York recalled that Fermi went on to make a rapid
estimate, in his characteristic style, and concluded that we should have been
visited long ago and many times over.

Their memories differ in detail, and they were recalling a lunch thirty-five
years earlier. What is clear is that the topic was **whether interstellar travel
is feasible**, and that Fermi published nothing about it and appears never to
have returned to it.

**Hart deserves the credit.** The argument as it is now used — the timescale
calculation, the systematic elimination of alternatives, the treatment of absence
as evidence — is Michael Hart's, from 1975. Robert Freitas made this point
explicitly in a 1985 *Icarus* paper pointedly titled "There Is No Fermi Paradox",
and some authors write "the Fermi–Hart paradox" for this reason. The habit of
naming it after Fermi has had a real cost: it lends the argument the authority of
a great physicist who never made it, and it obscures that the argument has
specific, examinable premises which are Hart's.

**Is it even a paradox?** By the standards used on this site, not in the strict
sense — nothing contradicts anything. It is classified here as an open problem
because the disagreement is real and current, and because presenting any single
resolution as settled would be false. It is worth noticing that the label
"paradox" itself does some of the persuasive work: calling something a paradox
implies that a resolution is owed, and part of what Sandberg, Drexler and Ord
argue is that no resolution is owed, because the expectation was never justified.

**The methodological quarrel underneath.** Two camps have been arguing past each
other for fifty years.

*The astronomical camp* treats the question as one about physical processes:
how likely is abiogenesis, how likely is complex life, how fast can you actually
travel. Progress comes from telescopes and laboratories, and the honest answer is
that we do not yet know enough.

*The probabilistic camp* treats it as a question about inference under deep
uncertainty: how should a reasoner update on a null observation, how do you
handle a quantity uncertain over two hundred orders of magnitude, what does your
own existence entitle you to conclude. Progress comes from being careful about
priors.

The 2018 paper is the clearest statement of the second view, and its central
claim — that much of the apparent force of the paradox comes from treating
uncertain quantities as though they were known — is now hard to ignore even for
people who reject its conclusion.

**Why nobody should be confident.** Every proposal here requires an assumption
about the motives, longevity or technology of beings we have never observed. The
sample size is one, the observer is inside the sample, and the search has been
shallow. The appropriate epistemic state is genuine uncertainty, and the entries
that claim otherwise — in either direction — are claiming more than the evidence
supports.

## History

**Summer 1950.** The lunch at Los Alamos. Fermi with Emil Konopinski, Edward
Teller and Herbert York.

**1959.** Giuseppe Cocconi and Philip Morrison publish "Searching for Interstellar
Communications" in *Nature*, proposing that microwave frequencies near the
21-centimetre hydrogen line would be the natural channel. Modern SETI begins as a
research proposal.

**1960.** Frank Drake conducts Project Ozma, pointing an 85-foot dish at Tau Ceti
and Epsilon Eridani. Nothing.

**1961.** Drake writes his equation on a blackboard at Green Bank, not as a
predictive formula but as an agenda for a three-day meeting — a way of organising
what would need to be known.

**1973.** John Ball publishes the zoo hypothesis in *Icarus*.

**1975.** Michael Hart's paper appears in the *Quarterly Journal of the Royal
Astronomical Society*. It is the first rigorous statement of the argument, and its
conclusion is blunt: there are no extraterrestrial civilisations in the Galaxy,
and searching for radio messages is probably futile.

**1980.** Frank Tipler, in the same journal, adds von Neumann probes under the
title "Extraterrestrial Intelligent Beings Do Not Exist".

**1983.** Carl Sagan and William Newman reply with "The Solipsist Approach to
Extraterrestrial Intelligence", arguing that the colonisation models assume
exponential growth that real populations do not sustain. Also in 1983, David
Brin's survey "The Great Silence" catalogues the possibilities and introduces
the deterrence-based reasoning that later became the dark forest.

**1985.** Eric Jones publishes the recollections of the lunch. Robert Freitas
publishes "There Is No Fermi Paradox" in *Icarus*.

**1996–1998.** Robin Hanson formulates the Great Filter, initially in response to
claims about fossil life in the Martian meteorite ALH84001.

**2000.** Ward and Brownlee's *Rare Earth*.

**1995 onward.** The exoplanet era. 51 Pegasi b in 1995; the Kepler mission from
2009 establishes that planets outnumber stars and that small planets are the most
common kind. One of the Drake equation's seven factors moves from unknown to
measured.

**2008.** Liu Cixin's *The Dark Forest* is published in Chinese, reaching English
in 2015 and putting one particular hypothesis into general circulation far more
effectively than any academic paper had.

**2015.** Breakthrough Listen begins, the largest and best-funded SETI programme
so far.

**2018.** Sandberg, Drexler and Ord post "Dissolving the Fermi Paradox".

## Why It Matters

**It is the only argument that turns a non-observation into a claim about our own
future.** If the Great Filter framing is right, then the silence of the sky is
data about how often civilisations like ours survive their own technology. That
makes an astronomical question into a question about existential risk, and it is
why the Fermi problem is taken seriously by people who have no professional
interest in aliens.

**It shaped how the search is done.** Hart's argument is the standing objection to
optimistic Drake numbers, and it is why the field's centre of gravity has shifted
from listening for deliberate radio beacons towards looking for *technosignatures*
— unintended industrial byproducts that would be visible whether or not anyone
wants to be found. Richard Carrigan's search of the IRAS infrared catalogue for
Dyson spheres, and Jason Wright's Glimpsing Heat from Alien Technologies survey,
both exist because of it.

**It made "we haven't found any" a technical quantity.** How much of the search
space has actually been covered, and what a null result licenses you to conclude,
are now questions with published answers rather than rhetorical positions. That
is a real methodological improvement, and it came from taking the paradox
seriously enough to try to dissolve it.

**It is the reason METI is controversial.** Whether humanity should deliberately
broadcast its position is a live policy dispute, and every position in it —
Stephen Hawking's caution, the dark-forest argument, the counter-argument that
our radio leakage has made the question moot — is an argument about which
resolution of the Fermi problem you believe.

## Modern Relevance

**Exoplanets have removed one excuse.** In 1975 Hart had to assume that planets
existed around other stars. They do, in enormous numbers, and rocky planets in
temperate orbits are common. Every year of exoplanet results makes the
astronomical premises stronger and pushes the uncertainty further into biology
and sociology, where it is much harder to reduce.

**Biosignature searches are about to test the first factor.** The James Webb
Space Telescope can attempt atmospheric characterisation of some rocky
exoplanets, and the next generation of extremely large ground-based telescopes
will do better. If oxygen-rich atmospheres turn up on nearby worlds, the
probability of abiogenesis moves from "unknown over two hundred orders of
magnitude" to something measurable — and, on Hanson's reasoning, the news will be
worse for us the better it looks.

**Grabby aliens.** Hanson, Daniel Martin, Calvin McCarter and Jonathan Paulson
published a model in the *Astrophysical Journal* in 2021 that inverts the usual
approach: instead of asking how many civilisations there are, it asks what
distribution of expanding civilisations is consistent with humanity appearing as
early in cosmic history as it has. Their answer is that if loud, expanding
civilisations exist, they must be rare and we are unusually early. It is a good
example of the paradox generating testable structure rather than just a list of
possibilities.

**'Oumuamua and the evidential bar.** The first confirmed interstellar object
passed through the Solar System in 2017 with an unusual shape and a small
non-gravitational acceleration. Avi Loeb argued publicly that artificial origin
should be on the table; most of the field disagreed, favouring exotic but natural
explanations. The episode is instructive independently of who is right: it
demonstrated how quickly the Fermi question turns any anomaly into a contested
one, and how badly the field needs agreed standards of evidence in advance.

**It is a stress test for reasoning under deep uncertainty.** The Fermi problem
is now used as a worked example well outside astronomy — in forecasting, in risk
analysis, in the study of how to handle parameters uncertain across many orders
of magnitude. The 2018 dissolution paper is cited as often for its methodology as
for its conclusion, and that may end up being its more durable contribution.

## Deep Dive

### The Drake equation and what it is for

$$N = R_* \cdot f_p \cdot n_e \cdot f_l \cdot f_i \cdot f_c \cdot L$$

where $R_*$ is the rate of star formation, $f_p$ the fraction with planets, $n_e$
the mean number of habitable planets per such system, $f_l$ the fraction on which
life arises, $f_i$ the fraction of those on which intelligence arises, $f_c$ the
fraction that become detectable, and $L$ the mean duration of detectability.

Drake wrote it as a meeting agenda, not a prediction. Used as a prediction it is
badly behaved, for a reason that has nothing to do with astronomy.

### Why multiplying point estimates fails

Write $X = \prod_{i=1}^{n} x_i$ and take logs:

$$\ln X = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln x_i .$$

If the $\ln x_i$ are independent with means $\mu_i$ and variances $\sigma_i^2$,
then $\ln X$ has mean $\sum \mu_i$ and variance $\sum \sigma_i^2$, and $X$ is
approximately log-normal. For a log-normal,

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \exp\!\left(\textstyle\sum\mu_i + \tfrac12\sum\sigma_i^2\right),
\qquad
\mathrm{median}(X) = \exp\!\left(\textstyle\sum\mu_i\right).$$

The ratio is $\exp\left(\tfrac12\sum\sigma_i^2\right)$, which for factors uncertain
over many orders of magnitude is astronomically large. **The mean and the typical
value are not remotely the same number.**

What you want is not $\mathbb{E}[N]$ but

$$P(N < 1) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{-\sum\mu_i}{\sqrt{\sum\sigma_i^2}}\right),$$

the probability that the Galaxy contains nobody but us. Plugging in point
estimates and multiplying computes something like the first quantity while the
argument requires the second. Sandberg, Drexler and Ord's contribution is to
carry distributions through the calculation from literature-derived priors for
each factor. Because the uncertainty in $f_l$ alone spans on the order of two
hundred orders of magnitude, $\sum\sigma_i^2$ is enormous and $P(N<1)$ comes out
substantial.

This does not prove we are alone. It shows that "we should expect company" was
never a licensed inference from the numbers we actually have.

### Colonisation as a front

Model expansion as a travelling wave. With hop distance $d$, cruise speed $v$ and
settling time $\tau$ per hop, the effective front speed is

$$v_{\text{front}} = \frac{d}{d/v + \tau} = \frac{v}{1 + v\tau/d}.$$

Crossing a galaxy of diameter $D$ takes $T = D/v_{\text{front}}$. For
$D = 10^5$ ly, $d = 10$ ly, $v = 0.01c$ and $\tau = 10^3$ yr:

$$v_{\text{front}} = \frac{0.01c}{1 + (0.01)(1000)/10} = \frac{0.01c}{2} = 0.005c,
\qquad T = 2\times 10^7\ \text{yr}.$$

Twenty million years against a Galactic age of $1.3\times10^{10}$ years, a ratio
of about $1.5\times10^{-3}$. The conclusion is robust to enormous changes in the
inputs: to stretch $T$ beyond the age of the Galaxy you need $v_{\text{front}}$
below about $10^{-5}c$ — three metres per second — sustained by nobody, ever.
This insensitivity is why Hart's argument is hard to defeat by tweaking
parameters, and why the interesting objections attack the premise that anyone
expands at all.

### The Great Filter, formally

Let $p$ be the probability that a given star produces a visibly expanding
civilisation, factored into steps:

$$p = \prod_{i} p_i .$$

Observationally, the Galaxy's $N_* \approx 10^{11}$ stars have produced no
visible expanding civilisation, which bounds

$$p \lesssim \frac{1}{N_*} \sim 10^{-11},$$

with the exact bound depending on how confident you are in Fact A. Some product
of the $p_i$ must therefore be very small: that is the Great Filter.

The inferential structure is the part that matters. Partition the steps into
those already behind us, with product $p_{\text{past}}$, and those ahead, with
product $p_{\text{future}}$. The constraint is on the product, so

$$p_{\text{past}} \cdot p_{\text{future}} \lesssim 10^{-11}.$$

Evidence that raises $p_{\text{past}}$ — finding independent abiogenesis on Mars
or Europa, finding oxygen atmospheres on nearby exoplanets, finding that
eukaryogenesis is easy — must, to preserve the bound, lower $p_{\text{future}}$.
Good news about the past is bad news about the future, and the relationship is
not rhetorical but arithmetical.

The escape from this conclusion is exactly the 2018 argument: if the
observational bound on $p$ is weak, or if our prior on $p_{\text{past}}$ already
sits many orders of magnitude below $10^{-11}$, then no filter needs to be
posited at all, and nothing follows about our future. Which of these is the case
is not known, and this entry does not pretend otherwise.

## Discover

You are about to play a game with a Predictor — a being with an extraordinary
track record. In thousands of previous trials, the Predictor has correctly
forecast each player's choice nearly every time.

On a table in front of you are two boxes.

**Box A** is transparent. You can see it contains £1,000. It will always
contain £1,000.

**Box B** is opaque. It either contains £1,000,000 or nothing at all.

Before you arrived, the Predictor studied your history, personality, and
reasoning patterns. If it predicted you would take *only Box B*, it filled Box
B with £1,000,000. If it predicted you would take *both boxes*, it left Box B
empty.

The prediction has already been made. The boxes have already been filled. The
Predictor has left the room.

You may now take both boxes, or only Box B.

What do you do?

## Understand

The situation seems clear — until you notice that two completely different
arguments reach opposite conclusions, each one apparently sound.

### The case for taking only Box B

The Predictor is nearly always right. Nearly everyone who has taken both boxes
walked away with £1,000. Nearly everyone who took only Box B walked away with
£1,000,000.

If you take both boxes, the Predictor almost certainly predicted that, so Box B
is almost certainly empty. Expected take: roughly £1,000.

If you take only Box B, the Predictor almost certainly predicted that, so Box B
almost certainly contains £1,000,000. Expected take: roughly £1,000,000.

You should take only Box B.

### The case for taking both boxes

The boxes are already filled. Nothing you decide now changes what is in them.
Either Box B contains £1,000,000 or it does not.

**Case 1:** Box B contains £1,000,000. Taking both gets you £1,001,000; taking
only Box B gets you £1,000,000. Taking both is better by £1,000.

**Case 2:** Box B is empty. Taking both gets you £1,000; taking only Box B
gets you nothing. Taking both is better by £1,000.

In either case, taking both boxes gives you £1,000 more. You should take both.

### The conflict

These two arguments use different decision principles, each of which seems
compelling on its own.

**Evidential decision theory (EDT)** says: choose the action that is most
correlated with good outcomes. Given the Predictor's track record, taking one
box is overwhelmingly correlated with a better outcome.

**Causal decision theory (CDT)** says: choose the action that *causes* the
best outcome. Since the boxes are already filled, your choice cannot cause their
contents to change. Whatever is in Box B, you are better off taking both.

The question is not which calculation you should do. Both calculations are
correct given their own premises. The question is which principle should govern
a rational agent's choices.

## Examples

**The smoking lesion.** Suppose a particular genetic lesion causes both a
desire to smoke and a high risk of cancer, but smoking itself does not cause
cancer. Then smoking correlates with cancer, and EDT would tell you not to smoke
— even though your decision to smoke cannot change whether you have the lesion.
CDT says: since the lesion is already there or not, smoking costs you nothing
causally and you may as well enjoy it. Most people find CDT's verdict here
correct.

**The chemo analogy.** Now suppose a doctor finds the lesion and correctly
predicts who will get cancer. Should a patient refuse a treatment they know is
ineffective, just because it correlates with good outcomes in trials where it
worked? No — because in those trials it *caused* the good outcomes. The
difference between correlation and causation is the whole question.

**Why CDT seems to fail here.** The smoking lesion seems to favour CDT; Newcomb
seems to favour EDT. The Predictor is not just correlating with your choice — it
is *responding to* your choice, or to what determines your choice. The
correlation between taking one box and getting £1,000,000 is not a spurious
byproduct of a third cause. It is created by the Predictor's accuracy. A
perfect predictor would make the correlation perfect, and it is hard to feel
comfortable walking away with £1,000 when everyone who reasons like you gets
£1,000,000.

## Explore

**Why philosophers are evenly split.** Surveys of professional philosophers
show a rough three-way division: one-boxing, two-boxing, and undecided. This is
unusual. Most philosophical thought experiments eventually settle, or at least
develop a consensus. Newcomb's Problem, more than fifty years old, has not. The
division tracks a genuine disagreement about what decision theory is *for*.

**The tickle defence of CDT.** A popular response to the smoking lesion says
that a rational agent who is considering smoking must not have the lesion (or
the lesion, if present, cannot be producing the desire to smoke rationally). So
the correlation between the decision and the lesion is broken for a genuine
reasoner. Applied to Newcomb: maybe the Predictor is predicting the *cause* of
your choice (your dispositions), not the choice itself. If so, a truly
reflective two-boxer would be predicted as a two-boxer, and the correlation with
Box B being empty would hold — CDT loses nothing.

**The transparency variant.** Suppose the Predictor tells you what it predicted
before you choose. If it says "I predicted one box," two-boxing is clearly
better now (Box B has the money regardless, and you take both). If it says "I
predicted two boxes," two-boxing is still better (Box B is empty regardless,
take what is there). Yet the Predictor is rarely wrong, so it must have
predicted you would two-box when told your prediction — which means it mostly
predicted two boxes and you mostly get only £1,000 by taking both. Deciding
after learning the prediction differs from deciding in advance.

**Game theory and commitment.** Some argue the real lesson is about
*commitment devices*. An agent who can credibly pre-commit to taking only one
box — perhaps by wiring themselves to do so, perhaps by being constitutionally
incapable of two-boxing — gets £1,000,000. An agent who optimises *after* the
prediction is already locked out. This reframes the problem: maybe rational
pre-commitment is better than causal reasoning applied after the fact, and the
paradox is pointing to a gap in standard decision theory, not a paradox in the
ordinary sense.

## Deep Dive

### Formal setup

Let $p$ be the Predictor's accuracy: the probability that its prediction matches
the player's actual choice. Assume $p$ is close to 1. Let $M$ denote the
million.

Define the outcomes:

|  | Predictor predicted 1 box | Predictor predicted 2 boxes |
|---|---|---|
| Player takes 1 box | $M$ | $0$ |
| Player takes 2 boxes | $M + 1{,}000$ | $1{,}000$ |

The prior probability that the Predictor predicted "1 box" given that you take
1 box is $p$; given that you take 2 boxes it is $1 - p$.

**EDT's expected value calculation:**

$$\mathrm{EU}_{\mathrm{EDT}}(1\text{-box}) = p \cdot M + (1-p)\cdot 0 = pM$$

$$\mathrm{EU}_{\mathrm{EDT}}(2\text{-box}) = (1-p)(M + 1{,}000) + p \cdot 1{,}000$$

For $p = 0.99$ and $M = 1{,}000{,}000$:

$$\mathrm{EU}_{\mathrm{EDT}}(1\text{-box}) \approx 990{,}000$$
$$\mathrm{EU}_{\mathrm{EDT}}(2\text{-box}) \approx 10{,}990$$

EDT recommends one box by a factor of roughly 90.

**CDT's dominance argument:**

For CDT, the contents of the boxes are fixed before the choice. Given any fixed
state of Box B, taking 2 boxes yields £1,000 more. The act of choosing cannot
causally affect what was placed in the box before the choice was made.
Dominance reasoning is valid when actions cannot affect the states being
compared; CDT holds that this is exactly the present case.

### What the disagreement is really about

The two theories disagree about *which conditional* to use. EDT uses the
evidential conditional: what is the expected outcome *given* that I choose this
action? CDT uses the causal conditional: what is the expected outcome *caused
by* this action?

In most decisions these two conditionals give the same answer. Newcomb's Problem
is specifically constructed to pull them apart by introducing a predictor whose
accuracy creates a correlation between choice and outcome that is not mediated by
causation running from choice to outcome.

Causal decision theorists respond that EDT would advise a patient to avoid
effective but unpleasant surgery because surgery correlates with bad health. EDT
theorists respond that Newcomb's case is not symmetric to this: the Predictor is
not a common cause of choice and outcome. It is an *accurate observer of* the
choice's cause, and accurate observation of a cause is not the same thing as a
spurious correlation.

### Functional decision theory

A more recent proposal, functional decision theory (FDT), asks: what is the
expected outcome of my *decision procedure* across all similar situations? FDT
recommends one-boxing, reasoning that one-boxing decision procedures are selected
for £1,000,000 by accurate predictors, so adopting a one-boxing procedure is
causally better in the long run — even though any individual one-boxing choice
does not cause the money to appear.

FDT dissolves the argument for two-boxing by locating the causal power not in
the individual act but in the *type* of decision process. Critics argue this
slides the question, since it is still true that the money is placed before the
decision, and the player cannot retroactively change it.

## History

Robert Nozick introduced the problem to academic philosophy in a 1969 paper.
He attributed it to William Newcomb, a physicist at Lawrence Livermore who had
described it in conversation around 1960. Newcomb's original motivations are not
recorded, and he never published on it.

Nozick's framing was deliberately neutral: he presented both arguments and said
he found it genuinely hard to know which was right. The paper generated
immediate responses from philosophers and decision theorists. Martin Gardner
publicised the problem in his Scientific American column in 1973, bringing it to
a much wider audience and generating reader correspondence that revealed a sharp
split of opinion even among mathematically trained non-philosophers.

The problem became the central test case for the emerging field of causal
decision theory in the 1970s and 1980s. David Lewis, Brian Skyrms, and others
developed CDT partly in response to Newcomb, arguing that EDT's willingness to
recommend choices purely on correlational grounds was a fatal flaw. EDT
proponents, including Frank Jackson and Robert Pargetter, argued back. The
debate has continued without convergence. Recent decades have seen functional
decision theory proposed as a third option.

## Why It Matters

**It forces a precise question about what rationality means.** Before Newcomb,
decision theory treated maximising expected utility as unambiguously correct.
The problem shows that two formulations of "maximise expected utility" — one
evidential, one causal — can diverge, and there is no theory-internal way to
settle which one is right.

**It separates correlation from causation in decision-making.** In medicine,
economics, and public policy, decisions often have to be made in the presence of
correlations that may or may not be causal. The problem makes vivid why the
distinction matters and why it is not always easy to determine which kind of
correlation you face.

**It reveals a structural limit of backwards-looking optimisation.** If the
predictor's accuracy comes from reading your *type* of decision-making rather
than your specific future choice, then optimising act-by-act in isolation misses
the possibility that your decision procedure can be evaluated globally.

## Modern Relevance

**AI alignment.** A sufficiently capable AI system may be modelled as facing
Newcomb-like situations: its decisions may be correlated with the dispositions
its designers instilled in it, and the consequences of those decisions depend on
what other agents expect of it. Understanding which decision theory to implement
in an AI is not a purely academic question.

**Mechanism design.** A principal who can predict agents' strategies can
structure rewards to elicit desired behaviour. The Newcomb structure is an
extreme idealisation of this: what decision procedure should an agent adopt if
it knows that accurate observers will design environments around that procedure?

**Commitment and contracts.** The problem illuminates the value of
pre-commitment — acting as if your decisions were fixed in advance, so that
observers who correctly model you can place the money in the box. Contracts,
constitutions, and credible threats all operate on the same logic.
